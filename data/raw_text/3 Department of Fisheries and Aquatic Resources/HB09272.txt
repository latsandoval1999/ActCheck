                                    EXPLANATORY NOTE

       Article XIII, Section 7 of the 1987 Constitution provides that, “[t]he State shall
protect the rights of subsistence fishermen, especially of local communities, to the
preferential use of local marine and fishing resources, both inland and offshore. It shall
provide support to such fishermen through appropriate technology and research,
adequate financial, production, and marketing assistance, and other services. The
State shall also protect, develop, and conserve such resources.”

       According to the Philippine Fisheries Profile published by the Bureau of
Fisheries and Aquatic Resources (BFAR) in 2022, the country’s total territorial water
area, including the exclusive economic zone, is 2,200,000 sq.km. while the country’s
coastline stretches 36,289 km.1

        As an archipelago composed of 7,641 islands, fishing and fishery-related
livelihoods remain the primary form of livelihood for a total of 2,302,648 registered
Filipino fisherfolk as of 2022.2 However, data from the Philippine Statistics Authority
(PSA) have consistently shown that fisherfolk remain among the poorest sectors in the
country, with a poverty incidence of 30.6% in 20213 and 27.4% in 2023.4

        In 2025, the total volume of fisheries production in 2025 was estimated at 3.96
million metric tons, indicating an annual average decline of 2.5% from the 4.06 million
metric tons output in 2024.5 These decreases in production, as noted in commercial
fisheries, marine municipal fisheries, and aquaculture, are part of an alarming trend of
declining production of the fisheries sector over the years. Furthermore, according to
international conservation group Oceana, Philippine capture fisheries have been
losing an average of 45,472 metric tons of fish each year since 2010, which amounts
to more than 591,000 metric tons of fish that are not wasted nor exported, but just
never allowed to exist in the first place.6 This 591,000 metric tons refers to lost
potential, or rather, fish that would have grown, reproduced, and sustained future
harvests if stocks had been kept within sustainable limits.7

       According to marine biologist Wilfredo Campos, the average annual loss of
45,472 metric tons of fish could have fed around 93,000 Filipino families experiencing
food insecurity. 8

        Unfortunately, in the Philippines, the yearly rate of overfishing, which causes
ecosystems to lose the ability to replenish themselves, compounds the problem, thus
continuously shrinking future catch even if the effort remains the same.9 Among the
identified causes for this is the weak enforcement behind the great ambition of
Republic Act No. 8550, or the Philippine Fisheries Code of 1998.

       Republic Act No. 8550 laid down progressive principles, such as “food security
as a national priority” and “preferential rights for municipal fisherfolk within the 15-
kilometer municipal waters”, while providing conservation tools, such as marine
protected areas and closed seasons.”10 However, at present, several of these
principles, most notably the preferential rights of municipal fisherfolk to the 15-
kilometer municipal waters, have been challenged or unenforced. Weak penalties,
fragmented monitoring, and the persistence of illegal, unreported, and unregulated
(IUU) fishing allowed pressure on fish stocks to intensify, thus leading to sustained
overfishing.11

       In recognition of the need to strengthen the implementation and address the
gaps of Republic Act No. 8550, the amendatory law, Republic Act No. 10654, was
enacted in 2015 to “prevent, deter and eliminate illegal, unreported and unregulated
fishing,” promising “tougher penalties, faster adjudication, modern monitoring and
science-based management meant to reverse decades of overfishing.”12

       However, almost a decade after the enactment of the amendatory law, the data
continue to show lagging implementation and declining fish stocks. Clearly, there is a

need for more decisive action on the part of the government to ensure that the
enforcement of laws and the formulation and implementation of initiatives affecting the
fisheries sector are truly responsive to the urgent needs of the sector, including
livelihood support for fisherfolk and support for the country’s food security.

       As such, this bill seeks to create the Department of Fisheries and Aquatic
Resources to serve as the primary policy and regulatory body on fisheries and aquatic
resources in the country. While the existence and efforts of the Bureau of Fisheries
and Aquatic Resources (BFAR), the agency attached to the Department of Agriculture
(DA) that is currently spearheading initiatives for the fisheries sector, are duly
recognized, the creation of a separate department would provide the much-needed
specialized attention to the needs and concerns of the fisheries and aquatic resources
industry and sector.

        Unlike the current Bureau, the Department proposed in this bill shall have its
own budget and autonomy to formulate and enforce policies and measures on matters
concerning fisheries and aquatic resources. At the same time, this would allow the
Department of Agriculture to focus on the various challenges of land-based farming
that also pose threats to the country’s food security.

        In the 20th Congress, similar bills were filed by Representatives Shirlyn Bañas-
Nograles, Janice Degamo, Lordan Suan, Harold Duterte, Jonathan Keith Flores,
Bienvenido Abante, Tsuyoshi Horibata, Vincenzo Luigi Villafuerte, Miguel Luis
Villafuerte, Terry Ridon, Salvador Pleyto, Ricardo Cruz Jr., Jorge Bocobo, Adiran
Salceda, Jane Castro, Rolando Macasaet, Ferdinand Martin Romualdez, Yedda
Romualdez, Andrew Julian Romualdez, Jude Acidre, Keith Tan, Rufus Rodriguez, and
Maximo Rodriguez, and Senators Francis “Kiko” Pangilinan and Alan Peter Cayetano.
This bill largely adopts Senator Kiko Pangilinan’s Senate Bill No. 221.

       The immediate passage of this bill is earnestly sought.

 4         SEC. 3. The Department of Fisheries and Aquatic Resources. – The
 5   Department of Fisheries and Aquatic Resources, hereinafter referred to as the
 6   “Department,” is hereby created in accordance with the provisions of this Act.
 7
 8          SEC. 4. Mandate. – The Department shall have primary jurisdiction over the
 9   management, conservation, development, protection, utilization, and disposition of all
10   fishery and aquatic resources of the country, including the habitats of fish and all other
11   marine life and other activities which impact these habitats, with the exception of
12   municipal waters, which shall remain under the jurisdiction of local government units
13   (LGUs): Provided, That LGUs shall manage their respective municipal waters in
14   accordance with national fishery policies, laws, rules, and regulations, and in a manner
15   consistent with the policies set forth herein.
16
17         The Department shall have the authority to supervise and regulate the
18   production and capture of fish and fishery products within its jurisdiction, as well as the
19   processing and marketing of all fisheries and aquatic products in the country.
20
21           The Department shall also have the responsibility of rationalizing all ocean
22   sector initiatives that impact fish and marine resources or their habitat, including, but
23   not limited to, exploration and development of marine energy, promotion and
24   management of coastal and marine tourism, designation of shipping lanes, and
25   identification of and management of protected areas and marine industry development
26   areas.
27

35          SEC. 11. Qualifications. – No person shall be appointed Secretary,
