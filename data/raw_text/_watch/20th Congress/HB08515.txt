                                                   EXPLANATORY NOTE

There is an alarming increase in overweight and obesity among Filipino children. In the
Philippines, the prevalence of overweight and obesity among school-age children (5 to 10 years
old) has increased from 5.7% in 2003 to 12.9% in 2023. Among adolescents (10-19 years old), the
prevalence increased from 4.9% in 2003 to 12.5% in 2023. The figures are estimated to increase
by 30% by 2030 if no actions are taken.1 As they grow up, overweight or obese children are
stigmatized and develop noncommunicable diseases such as cancer, cardiovascular disease,
diabetes, and chronic respiratory diseases.2

The 2025 Child Nutrition Report – ‘Feeding Profit: How food environments are failing children’
– highlights how unhealthy food environments drive the global rise in overweight and obesity
among children and adolescents.3 Filipino children live in an increasingly unhealthy food
environment with low availability, accessibility, desirability, and affordability of healthy foods –
and high availability, affordability and promotion or marketing of unhealthy foods.

The State has an obligation to guarantee the proper care and nutrition of children.4 This involves
providing them with access to a wide range of health services including promotive and preventive
care to noncommunicable diseases under Republic Act 11223 or the Universal Health Care Act.
The State also has the duty to protect children from the harmful effects of irresponsible and
inappropriate marketing of unhealthy food and beverages so that they may be able to attain the
highest standard of health and promote a balanced diet for a healthy and vigorous life.56

With the onset of accessibility to social media and other forms of multi-media, Filipino children
are at the mercy of targeted food and beverages marketing that are not necessarily healthful and
contribute to frequent visits to the hospital to seek medical attention for various ailments related
to poor eating habits.7 Admittedly, these marketing strategies prey on children’s immature
cognitive capabilities and defenses leading to higher consumption of calorie-dense and ultra-
processed food and beverages that are rich in salt, sugar, and fat. Such diets are associated with
accelerated increase in rates of childhood obesity. Despite the clear negative impact of deceitful
and exploitative marketing of unhealthy food and beverages to children’s health, there are, yet no
mechanisms to regulate these activities. These insidiously harmful marketing and advertising
tactics that are left unchecked continue to influence children’s eating habits and food
preferences.8

The World Health Organization (WHO) and the United Nations Children’s Fund (UNICEF)9, both
agencies focused on protecting the rights and welfare of children, support the regulation of the
power of and exposure of children to unhealthy food marketing and its persuasive appeal.10

The current policy landscape, however, fails to realize this vision for children and leaves them
vulnerable to exploitation. In 2021, UNICEF assessed that the market for highly processed food
products including soft drinks, sweet snacks, and savory snacks is growing in the Philippines, with
demand driven by creative marketing strategies by the food manufacturing industry.11 These
marketing strategies include the use of misleading nutrition, quality and health claims, product
reformulation to make items appear ‘healthier’, collaborations with movies, and giving away
prizes, among others, to target children. None of these strategies are regulated to address the
harm brought about by aggressive marketing of unhealthy food and beverages to children.

This bill seeks to fill a gap in public health policy: the lack of protection against the targeted
promotion of unhealthy foods and beverages to children. It hampers informed food choices and
fosters an unhealthy food environment. The law aims to clearly define what foods are considered
healthy or unhealthy and to implement measures to shield children from unrestricted promotion
of unhealthy options, involving children more actively in their health decisions.

The undersigned earnestly and urgently seeks the immediate passage of this bill.

16   neglects the performance of a responsibility specifically enjoined by this Act and its implementing
17   rules and regulations, or against any member of the food and beverage industry, including
18   persons working on their behalf, who violates any provision of this Act and its implementing rules
19   and regulations. The court shall exempt such action from the payment of filing fees and shall
20   exempt the plaintiff from the filing of an injunction bond for the issuance of preliminary
21   injunction. The court may award reasonable attorney’s fees, moral damages, and litigation costs
22   as appropriate. The administrative, civil, and criminal actions allowed under this paragraph shall
23   be cumulative and shall not preclude any other person from filing a similar action.

25   SECTION 16. International Cooperation and Provision on Technical and Financial
26   Assistance. The State recognizes that international and regional cooperation is an important
27   component of regulating cross-border marketing of food and beverages that harm children’s
28   health. For this purpose, implementing agencies and its members may seek and receive technical
29   and/or financial assistance from international organizations, except those that belong to or are
30   affiliated with the food and beverage industry as defined herein, for the purpose of implementing

13                                           ARTICLE IV
14                                     PROMOTING HEALTHY DIETS
15
16   SECTION 20. Institutionalization of Programs Promoting Healthy Diets and
17   Lifestyles. (a) National government agencies in the council shall establish and ensure the full
18   implementation of all programs and projects that seek to promote healthy diets, the ultimate aims
19   of which are to address overweight and obesity in children and eradicate malnutrition among
20   them. These programs and projects include, but are not limited to, the following:
21
22      i.      “Green Zone” program. The DepEd, DOH, ECCD, and other national government
23              agencies shall designate the child-centered settings as defined in this Act as “green zones,”
24              or zones where whole and minimally processed food and beverages, as well as clean and
25              safe water, are readily available, accessible, and affordable. To this end, only individuals
26              or entities that sell food and beverages not otherwise prohibited to be marketed to children
27              under this Act shall be included as suppliers or concessionaries in these Green Zones.
28     ii.      Research and development program. The National Task Force shall establish a research
29              and development program to be spearheaded by the NCCT. The NCCT, in coordination
30              with other national government agencies, shall undertake studies, among others, that
31              measure the impact of harmful food marketing to the lives of Filipinos and Filipino
32              children, with the goal of building evidence for policies that continually and gradually
33              reduce exposure to the harmful impacts of food and beverage marketing. The DepEd shall
34              allocate institutional research funding to support this program.
35    iii.      Social and behavioral change (SBC) program. The DOH-HPB shall lead the development
36              and implementation of age and development-specific and culturally-appropriate SBC
37              program to popularize the consumption of healthy diets among Filipinos and increase its
38              demand among them. The DOH-HPB shall ensure the dissemination of its messages
39              across all media forms and shall conduct regular evaluations of the impact of its SBC
40              program.
41    iv.       Instruction on the harmful impact of food marketing as part of the school curriculum.
42              The DepEd shall direct the inclusion of discussions on food marketing to children, as part
43              of the media and information literacy subject in senior high school.
44
45   The DOH and NNC shall also update its health promotion playbooks, job aids, manuals, and
46   health standards to include and recommend the implementation of measures that reduce the
47   exposure of children to the harmful impacts of food marketing and minimize their power.

51                                                 PENALTIES
52
53   SECTION 21. Penalties. The following penalties shall be imposed for each violation of the
54   prohibitions in this Act:
55
56           a. Any entity considered as a large enterprise by the DTI, which violates Section 6, 7, or 8,
57              or fails to comply with the requirements in Section 6(d) of this Act, shall:
58                 i.    Pay a fine of not less than five hundred thousand pesos (PHP 500,000) but not
59                       more than one million pesos (PHP 1,000,000), for the first offense;
60                ii.    Pay a fine of not less than one million pesos (PHP1,000,000) but not more than
61                       five million pesos (PHP 5,000,000), for the second offense;
62               iii.    Suffer the penalty of imprisonment ranging from one (1) year but not more than
63                       ten (10) years, for the third offense.
 1
 2      b. Any entity considered as micro, small, and medium-sized enterprises (MSMEs) by the
 3         DTI, or are otherwise not considered large enterprises by the DTI, which violates Section
 4         6, 7 or 8, or fails to comply with the requirements in Section 6(d) of this Act, shall:
 5           i.    Pay a fine of not less than one thousand pesos (PHP 1,000) but not more than five
 6                 thousand pesos (PHP 5,000) for the first offense;
 7          ii.    Pay a fine of not less than five thousand pesos (PHP 5,000) but not more than ten
 8                 thousand pesos (PHP 10,000) for the second offense;
 9         iii.    Pay a fine of not less than ten thousand pesos (PHP 10,000) but not more than
10                 twenty thousand pesos (PHP 20,000) for the third offense.
11
12      c. Any natural person who violates Section 6, 7, or 8 of this Act shall:
13            i.  Pay a fine of not more than ten thousand pesos (PHP10,000), for the first offense;
14           ii.  Pay a fine of not more than fifty thousand pesos (PHP50,000.00), for the second
15                offense;
16          iii.  Pay a fine of not more than one hundred thousand pesos (PHP100,000) or
17                imprisonment of not more than one (1) year, or both, at the discretion of the court,
18                for the third offense.
19
20      d. In addition to the foregoing fines and penalties, the following corrective actions, if not
21         initiated by the offenders themselves, shall be imposed by the appropriate authorities to
22         the entities that violate or fail to comply with the requisites of this Act:
23            i.   Seizure and condemnation, destruction, and/or appropriate disposition of items
24                 of prohibited marketing;
25           ii.   Issuance of takedown orders against banned advertising, promotions, and
26                 sponsorship content;
27          iii.   Cancellation of applicable licenses, registrations, and permits to operate for
28                 business establishments and operators or drivers of public utility vehicles;
29          iv.    Closure of the food and beverage establishment by LGUs having jurisdiction over
30                 premises where a violation of this Act has been committed.
31
32      e. A violation under Section 13 or 14 of this Act, committed by a government official or
33         employee, shall have the following penalties:
34            i.  Public officials and employees within the jurisdiction of the Civil Service
35                Commission (CSC) shall be subject to administrative disciplinary action pursuant
36                to Rule XIV (Discipline) of the Omnibus Rules Implementing Book V of EO 292,
37                as may be amended, modified, changed, added to, deleted from, or superseded by
38                the enactment of any new law;
39           ii.  Local elective officials shall be subject to administrative disciplinary action by the
40                Secretary of Interior and Local Government, or the Office of the President, as
41                appropriate, and provided by relevant laws, rules and regulations.
42          iii.  Other government officials shall be subject to administrative disciplinary action by
43                the Office of the Ombudsman or the Office of the President, as provided by relevant
44                laws, rules, and regulations.
45
46   SECTION 22. Liability of Juridical and Other Persons. Should a violation of this Act be
47   committed by a juridical person in Section 21 (a) or (b), the Chair of the Board, the President, the
48   General Manager, or the partners and/or the persons directly responsible therefor shall be
49   penalized. If the offender is an alien, they shall be deported after service of sentence and/or
50   payment of fine without further deportation proceedings and shall be permanently barred from
51   re-entering the Philippines. In case a violation is committed by, or in the interest of, a foreign
52   juridical person duly licensed to engage in business in the Philippines, such license to engage in
53   business in the Philippines shall be immediately revoked.

59   SECTION 23. Healthy Children Trust Fund. — A Healthy Children Trust Fund is hereby
60   created. All fines collected under Article V of this Act shall be held in favor of and deposited to

 2   SECTION 24. Compliance Monitoring. Not later than two (2) years after the date of the

12   under this Act, without prejudice to other remedies or penalties and sanctions that are available
13   or may be imposed by law for such delay or non-compliance.

15   SECTION 25. Implementing Rules. The DOH, in consultation with the members of the

17   implementation of this Act within six (6) months from the date of publication of this Act, provided

19   period, the specific provisions of this Act shall immediately be executory.

25   annual General Appropriations Act.

27   SECTION 27. Repealing clause. All laws, decrees, ordinances, administrative orders, rules

29   accordingly.
30
31   SECTION 28. Separability clause. If any part or provision of this Act shall be subsequently
32   declared unconstitutional, invalid or inconsistent with other prevailing laws, the other provisions
33   or parts which are not affect shall remain in full force and effect.
