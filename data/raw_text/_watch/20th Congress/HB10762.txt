                                                       EXPLANATORY NOTE

       A mountain range stretching more for than 540 kilometers across ten provinces1
cannot be effectively managed one permit, one municipality, or one agency at a time.

       The Sierra Madre is the longest mountain range in the Philippines and one of
Luzon’s most important ecological systems. Extending from Cagayan to Quezon, it
contains forests, watersheds, headwaters, river systems, wildlife habitats, ancestral
domains, settlements, agricultural areas, and infrastructure that cut across political and
administrative boundaries.

        Its ecological value is substantial and increasingly under pressure. A satellite-
based study reported that about half of the Philippines’ surviving old-growth forest is
located in the Sierra Madre, while forest gains from 2001 to 2018 were offset by continuing
losses of open and closed forests.2 More recent research in General Nakar, Quezon and
Rodriguez, Rizal likewise documented declining forest cover from 2016 to 2022 and
identified kaingin, infrastructure expansion, timber poaching, small-scale mining,
charcoal production, and natural hazards among the reported pressures on forests.3

       The stakes are especially concrete in Bulacan. The Angat Watershed, situated at
the southern end of the Sierra Madre, supports significant biodiversity and performs
functions vital to water security, agriculture, energy, and downstream communities.
Studies in Norzagaray and Doña Remedios Trinidad have documented considerable
amphibian, reptile, and bird diversity, including Philippine endemic and threatened
species. 4 The Angat water system, meanwhile, supplies more than 90% of Metro Manila’s

        The Sierra Madre is also frequently described as Luzon’s natural defense against
tropical cyclones. Scientific studies found that the mountain range influences wind and
rainfall patterns, but its effects vary depending on location and storm conditions.6 Its
protection need not rest on the claim that it stops every storm. Its forests, watersheds,
biodiversity, slopes, and ecological connectivity already provide compelling reasons for
responsible management.

      The challenge is that these ecological systems are interconnected, while
government authority and management over them is necessarily divided.

       Protected areas have their own statutory management bodies. The Department of
Environment and Natural Resources exercises authority over forests and natural
resources. Local government units perform land-use and development functions.
Indigenous Cultural Communities/Indigenous Peoples possess constitutionally and
statutorily protected rights over ancestral domains and ancestral lands. Other agencies
exercise mandates involving infrastructure, agriculture, energy, water resources, human
settlements, and national development planning.

       These institutions perform necessary functions, but no single one is positioned to
manage the Sierra Madre as an interconnected landscape. A watershed may span several
municipalities. A biodiversity corridor may extend beyond protected-area boundaries.
Forest degradation upstream may affect communities downstream. Several projects that
appear manageable when evaluated individually may, taken together, impose significant
pressure on the same watershed or ecological corridor.

       Environmental impacts do not cease to be cumulative simply because permits are
issued separately.

        This bill addresses that governance gap by establishing the Sierra Madre
Management Area (SMMA) as a common framework for integrated, landscape-level
planning and management. It adopts differentiated management based on ecological
conditions and lawful existing uses. Areas of exceptional ecological, biological,
hydrological, or disaster-risk significance may be delineated as Core Conservation Areas
and accorded heightened protection. Degraded but ecologically important areas may be
prioritized for restoration, while appropriate areas may continue to accommodate
settlements, livelihoods, infrastructure, and responsible economic activity subject to
applicable safeguards.

conditions demand it, restoration where degradation has occurred, and responsible
development where compatible with environmental limits and existing rights. It also
ensures that decisions made within one jurisdiction are assessed in relation to their
broader effects on the Sierra Madre as an interconnected landscape.

       For this purpose, the bill creates the Sierra Madre Conservation and Management
Authority (SMCMA). Its principal role is to provide the whole-of-government planning,
coordination, scientific monitoring, and policy convergence required by a mountain
system that cuts across agencies, regions, provinces, and municipalities.

        At the center of this framework is the Sierra Madre Conservation and Sustainable
Development Plan, integrating ecological zoning, watershed and biodiversity conditions,
land and resource use, climate and disaster risks, major infrastructure, restoration
priorities, cumulative environmental impacts, and measurable performance indicators.

        The measure likewise recognizes that conservation cannot be separated from the
people who have long lived within and depended upon the Sierra Madre. It safeguards
the rights of Indigenous Cultural Communities/Indigenous Peoples, including rights over
ancestral domains, traditional resource use, customary practices, and participation in the
management of ecologically significant areas, while maintaining safeguards against
commercial or destructive exploitation.

       The Sierra Madre contains forests and biodiversity of national significance, while
its watersheds sustain communities and major economic activities. The consequences of
decisions made within it frequently extend beyond the jurisdiction in which those
decisions originate.

      This bill provides a framework that enables the government of seeing the Sierra
Madre as a whole by protecting what must be protected, restoring what has been
degraded, and allowing responsible development where ecological conditions permit.

       In view of the foregoing, the immediate enactment of this bill is earnestly sought.

 1          Section 19. Sierra Madre Plan Consistency Review. – A project located wholly or
 2   partly within the SMMA that is required under applicable law or regulation to undergo
 3   an environmental impact assessment (EIA) shall be subject to a Sierra Madre Plan
 4   Consistency Review by the SMCMA.
 5
 6         The Review shall determine the project’s consistency with the Sierra Madre Plan
 7   and the applicable ecological management zone, taking into account its material effects
 8   on watersheds, biodiversity and ecological connectivity, cumulative environmental
 9   impacts, climate and disaster risks, and conservation and restoration priorities.
10
11          Upon completion of the Review, the SMCMA shall transmit its findings and
12   recommendations to the government agency or local government unit having authority
13   to approve, permit, or implement the project.
14
15          The Sierra Madre Plan Consistency Review shall not constitute a separate permit,
16   clearance, license, certification, or environmental compliance certificate, and shall, to the
17   greatest extent practicable, be integrated into existing environmental, planning,
18   investment, protected area, Indigenous Peoples, or other applicable review and approval
19   processes.
20
21          Where the SMCMA finds that a proposed project is materially inconsistent with
22   the Sierra Madre Plan or the applicable ecological management zone, the SMCMA and
23   the concerned approving or government implementing agency or LGUshall, prior to final
24   action on the project, undertake consultation to determine whether the inconsistency may
25   reasonably be addressed through project modification, alternative siting or design,
26   additional safeguards, or other appropriate measures.
27
28          Where the material inconsistency remains unresolved after such consultation, the
29   competent government agency or LGU may proceed only upon a written determination
30   stating the substantial reasons therefor, including the public interest sought to be served,
31   the reasonable alternatives considered, the reasons such alternatives are not practicable,
32   and the measures to avoid, minimize, restore, or otherwise address adverse impacts. The
33   determination, together with the findings and recommendations of the SMCMA, shall be
34   made publicly accessible, subject to limitations on disclosure provided by law.
35
36          An unresolved material inconsistency involving a major national government
37   project that raises a substantial inter-agency policy conflict may be elevated by the
38   SMCMA or the concerned agency to the Sierra Madre Governing Council for appropriate
39   resolution, without prejudice to the jurisdiction of the agency vested by law with the
40   authority to approve or regulate the project.
41
42          Nothing in this section shall authorize the SMCMA, the Sierra Madre Governing
43   Council, or any approving agency to waive, override, or authorize an exception to a
44   prohibition or requirement expressly imposed under this Act or any other law.

17           Section 21. Penalties. – Any person who knowingly undertakes an activity in
18   violation of Section 13 of this Act without lawful authority shall, upon conviction, be
19   punished by a fine of not less than One million pesos (PhP 1,000,000.00) but not more than
20   Five million pesos (PhP 5,000,000.00), or imprisonment of not less than six (6) years but
21   not more than twelve (12) years, or both, at the discretion of the court.
22
23          Where the same act constitutes an offense under another law imposing an equal or
24   greater penalty, prosecution shall be under such applicable law.
25
26           No ICC/IP shall be held criminally liable under this Act solely by reason of
27   residence or occupation within a Core Conservation Area, or for traditional resource use,
28   subsistence activities, cultural or spiritual practices, or other sustainable customary
29   practices undertaken in the exercise of rights recognized under Republic Act No. 8371 and
30   other applicable laws. In determining whether an activity falls within this protection, due
31   regard shall be given to applicable customary laws and practices, the nature, purpose,
32   scale, and environmental impact of the activity, and whether it is undertaken primarily
33   for subsistence, cultural, spiritual, or community purposes rather than for commercial
34   exploitation.
35
36           The foregoing shall not exempt any person from liability for commercial logging,
37   mining, quarrying, hazardous waste disposal, or other commercial or destructive
38   activities prohibited under this Act or other applicable laws.
39
40          If the offender is a juridical entity, the director, officer, partner, manager, or other
41   responsible person who knowingly authorized, ordered, consented to, or participated in
42   the prohibited act shall be liable.
43
