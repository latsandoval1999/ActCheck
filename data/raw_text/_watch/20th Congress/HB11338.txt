                                  EXPLANATORY NOTE

The Philippines is known for being extremely rich in natural resources and is recognized as one
of only 17 megadiverse countries which together comprise around 70% of the entire world’s
biodiversity (World Economic Forum). Lamentably, it is also “a hotspot of biodiversity loss,
ranking among the top ten countries in the world with the largest number of species threatened
with extinction.” (Interactive Country Fiches) This, combined with the magnitude and severity of
land and marine degradation; air, water and land pollution; and vulnerability to the ravages of
climate change, paints a dismal picture of a severe and compounding environment and natural
resources crisis.

The situation undeniably demands immediate action. The government has responded by, among
others, formulating the first National Adaptation Plan specifically to address the impacts of
climate change; and enacting the Philippine Ecosystem and Natural Capital Accounting System
(PENCAS) Act, “aimed to enhance ecological balance and climate resilience through natural
resource accounting”. Both measures acknowledge the vital role of local government in the
attainment of their objectives. Not only is this self-evident; it is also backed by facts revealed in a
number of studies.

A study on environmental crimes in the Philippines shows that there is a need to “strengthen
localized environmental law enforcement, especially in hotspot areas where illegal activities are
rampant, such as illegal logging, overfishing, mining violations, and improper waste dumping”.
The same study stresses that this entails not only committed funding, but adequate environmental
law officers. (Medrano & Rowela, 2025)

Further underscoring the importance of local environmental law officers is a study stating that
“... the Philippines faces significant biodiversity loss due to economic pressures and illegal
activities like logging and wildlife trade, resulting in over USD1 billion in natural resource loss
annually”. (Caliwan, 2023)

This is a considerable amount, but the costs of an environmental crisis obviously cannot be
measured in terms of money nor in the loss of natural resources alone. The most dire impact is
ultimately felt by people in terms of whether their human rights such as the rights to health,
education, work, and life are protected and fulfilled or violated and abused. Unsurprisingly, those
who work for environmental protection are human rights defenders. Tragically, in the course of
their work in defending the environment, they are constantly at risk of being victims of human
rights violations and abuses.

This is true worldwide and the situation is dire enough that in 2022 a Special Rapporteur on
Environmental Defenders was elected to “take measures to protect any person experiencing or at
imminent threat of penalization, persecution, or harassment for seeking to exercise their rights
under the Aarhus Convention1.” (United Nations Economic Commission for Europe) The most
recent thematic report of the United Nations Working Group on Enforced or Involuntary
Disappearances published in September 2025 is on enforced disappearance in the context of the
defence of land, natural resources and environment. Within this context, the Philippines has
earned the shameful distinction of being the most dangerous country for defenders protecting
land and environmental rights. (Global Witness, 2025)

Having environment and natural resources officers (ENROs) at the provincial, municipal and city
levels is imperative not only for the country’s environment and natural resources but for our
environment defenders as well. Given both the urgency and importance of the functions of
ENROs, and to ensure that they are able to perform their tasks to their fullest capacities without
1
  The Aarhus Convention refers to the UNECE Convention on Access to Information, Public Participation
in Decision-making and Access to Justice in Environmental Matters adopted in 1998 in Aarhus, Denmark.
It is also referred to as the Convention on Environmental Defenders.
the threat of job insecurity on top of having to discharge a wide range of both duties from
policymaking to implementation, it is necessary that their appointment be both mandatory and
permanent.

In view of the foregoing, approval of this measure is earnestly sought.

      AN ACT MANDATING THE PERMANENT APPOINTMENT OF THE
  PROVINCIAL/CITY/MUNICIPAL ENVIRONMENT AND NATURAL RESOURCES
OFFICER (ENRO), PROVIDING ADEQUATE FUND FOR THE SALARY AND OTHER
 EMOLUMENTS OF THE ENRO IN THE LOCAL GOVERNMENT UNIT’S ANNUAL
 APPROPRIATIONS ORDINANCE, AMENDING SECTION 484 (a), ARTICLE XIV OF
REPUBLIC ACT 7160 OTHERWISE KNOWN AS THE LOCAL GOVERNMENT CODE
                              OF 1991


SECTION 1.​ This Act shall be known as the “Mandatory Permanent Appointment of the
Provincial/City/Municipal ENRO Act”.

SEC. 2. Section 484 (a), ARTICLE XIV of Republic Act 7160, otherwise known as the Local
Government Code of 1991 is hereby amended to read as follows:

          (a) No person shall be appointed environment and natural resources officer (ENRO)
              unless he/she is a citizen of the Philippines, PREFERABLY a resident of the local
              government unit concerned, of good moral character, A REPUTABLE
              ENVIRONMENTAL RIGHTS DEFENDER WHO ACTIVELY PROTECTS
              THE HUMAN RIGHT TO A HEALTHY AND SUSTAINABLE
              ENVIRONMENT AND ECOSYSTEM, a holder of a degree preferably in
              environment, forestry, agriculture or any related course from a recognized college
               or university [, and a first grade civil service eligible or its equivalent]. He/She
               must have acquired experience in environmental and natural resources
               management, conservation, and utilization, for at least five (5) years in the case of
               the provincial or city environment and natural resources officer, and three (3)
               years in the case of the Municipal Environment and Natural Resources Officer.

               [The appointment of the environment and natural resources officer is optional for
               provincial, city, and municipal governments.]

               THE PROVINCIAL/CITY/MUNICIPAL ENVIRONMENT AND NATURAL
               RESOURCES OFFICER (ENRO) MUST BE A FIRST GRADE CIVIL
               SERVICE ELIGIBLE OR ITS EQUIVALENT. THE PROVINCIAL AND CITY
               ENRO SHALL HAVE A SALARY GRADE OF 24 AND THE MUNICIPAL
               ENRO SHALL HAVE A SALARY GRADE OF 22. THE APPOINTMENT OF
               THE   ENRO     IS   MANDATORY      AND    PERMANENT     FOR
               PROVINCIAL/CITY/MUNICIPAL GOVERNMENTS.

SEC. 3. The Annual Appropriations Ordinance of the provincial/city/municipal governments
shall provide adequate funds for the salary and other emoluments of the ENRO.

SEC. 4. The provincial/city/municipal governments in coordination with the Department of
Environment and Natural Resources (DENR) shall ensure a human rights-based enabling
environment for the ENROs to fully and effectively discharge their functions toward the prompt
attainment of the goals of their office.

SEC. 5. If any section or provision of this Act is declared unconstitutional or invalid, other
sections or provisions not affected thereby shall remain in full force and effect.

SEC. 6. All laws, decrees, executive orders, rules and regulations and other issuances or parts
thereof inconsistent with the provisions of this Act are hereby repealed, amended or modified
accordingly.

SEC. 7. This Act shall take effect fifteen (15) days after its publication in the Official Gazette or
in two (2) newspapers of general circulation.
