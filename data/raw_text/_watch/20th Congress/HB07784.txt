                               EXPLANATORY NOTE


       Motorcycles are an indispensable part of daily life for millions of Filipinos. For
workers, small entrepreneurs, delivery riders, farmers, and students, motorcycles are
not a luxury but a primary and often essential means of transportation. They provide
affordable mobility, enable access to livelihood, and bridge gaps in areas where public
transport remains limited or unavailable. It is within this social reality that laws
governing motorcycle use must be crafted: firm against crime, yet fair to the law-
abiding majority who depend on motorcycles for work and survival.
       Republic Act No. 11235, or the Motorcycle Crime Prevention Act, was enacted
to address the legitimate concern of crimes committed using motorcycles. While the
objective of enhancing public safety is sound and necessary, experience since its
implementation has shown that certain provisions of the law have imposed undue
burdens on ordinary motorcycle owners and riders who have no criminal intent.
Excessive penalties, impractical compliance requirements, and enforcement actions
disconnected from actual culpability have resulted in hardship, confusion, and a
growing sense of injustice among the riding public.
        This measure seeks to remedy these deficiencies without weakening the
State’s resolve to prevent motorcycle-related crimes. Its guiding principle is simple but
crucial: laws intended to protect public safety must be effective, enforceable, and
proportional. Safety measures lose public legitimacy when they become punitive in a
manner that punishes compliance failures caused by systemic delays, administrative
inefficiencies, or circumstances beyond the control of motorcycle owners.
       The proposed amendments rationalize registration and ownership procedures
by clarifying responsibilities between dealers and buyers, recognizing installment and
conditional sales, and providing clear rules for repossessed motorcycles. These
reforms reflect the reality that many compliance issues arise not from willful disregard

of the law, but from gaps in process and coordination. By streamlining procedures, the
bill promotes compliance rather than penalizing confusion.
        The measure also corrects overly harsh penalties by decriminalizing purely
administrative violations and replacing imprisonment with reasonable fines. This
ensures that criminal sanctions are reserved for acts involving clear intent to deceive
or facilitate crime—such as tampering, falsification, or the deliberate misuse of number
plates. In doing so, the law refocuses enforcement on actual offenders, not ordinary
riders struggling with compliance due to delays in plate issuance or documentation.
         Importantly, the bill rationalizes number plate display requirements to ensure
visibility and identification while removing impractical standards that are difficult to
implement and enforce. It also establishes clear and fair rules for situations involving
lost, stolen, or damaged plates, providing owners with a reasonable timeframe to
report incidents without fear of automatic criminal liability.
       At its core, this measure affirms that crime prevention and social justice are not
mutually exclusive. It advances a balanced approach that protects communities from
crime while safeguarding the rights and livelihoods of millions of law-abiding Filipino
motorcycle owners. A law that is perceived as oppressive or unfair risks eroding public
cooperation, which is essential for effective law enforcement. By aligning penalties
with culpability and recognizing the socioeconomic realities of motorcycle ownership,
the State strengthens its capacity to deter crime.
      In view of the foregoing, the immediate passage of this bill, which passed Third
and Final Reading in the 19th Congress, is earnestly sought.
