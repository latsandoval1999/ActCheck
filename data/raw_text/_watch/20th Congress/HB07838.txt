                                    EXPLANATORY NOTE

        Dubbed as the “biggest corruption scandal in Philippine history”, the 2025 flood
control projects controversy has revealed deep-seated problems in the government
infrastructure program, exposing critical gaps in our public procurement laws and their
implementation.1 Official investigations, as well as credible media reports, have
exposed a grand theft where billions of pesos of flood control funds have been
pocketed by corrupt high-ranking officials, including lawmakers and government
engineers, and their private sector cohorts. They have executed the heist through
multiple ghost projects, substandard construction, and a monopoly of contracts to a
favored group of contractors.2

         Some of the major gaps identified by serious observers of the flood control
projects controversy pertained to several weaknesses in the conduct of procurement
and bidding implementation. There were contractor monopolies, as a staggering P100
billion, or nearly 20 % of the P545 billion flood control budget from 2022 to 2025, was
awarded to only 15 out of over 2,400 accredited contractors. Also, there were “license

budget, and connections to officials with regulatory influence.4

          These gaping holes in the bidding and awarding of infrastructure projects come
into sharper focus when we consider that -- instead of plugging the gaps in our legal
and regulatory frameworks -- the new law governing public procurement, Republic Act
No. 12009, or the “New Government Procurement Act of 2024”, has watered down the
provisions on exacting accountability against dishonest and corrupt public officials and
their private sector accomplices. This recent law completely repeals the penal clauses
of its predecessor, Republic Act No. 9184, or the “Government Procurement Reform
Act”, merging instead in one article the provisions on administrative sanctions and civil
liabilities. This is a step in an epically wrong direction.

        To remedy the situation, and in response to the public clamor to promulgate
stronger legislative measures to curb corruption, especially in the area of government
procurement, this bill seeks to revive the intent and reinstate the major provisions of
Article XXI (Penal Clause) of RA 9184, with certain modifications. In this bill, we seek
to criminalize various acts of machination of government procurement, and further
amend Presidential Decree No. 1606 to empower the Sandiganbayan to try and
decide cases concerning offenses involving public officials with salary of 27 or higher.
Article XX of RA 12009 is sought to be amended to come up with a consolidated new
article that encompasses criminal, civil and administrative liabilities to underscore the
need for comprehensive and integrated accountability mechanisms to combat
corruption in the conduct of bidding and other activities in government procurement.

       By describing and punishing certain reprehensible acts as crimes of
“machinations in government procurement”, and prescribing stiff penalties for their
commission -- including long-term imprisonment and disqualification from holding
public employment or transacting with government -- it is hoped that the proposed law
will have its intended deterrent and retributive effects. Thus, this bill can bolster our
legal arsenal to better confront the lingering problem of pestering corruption and
dishonesty among some of our public officials and employees.

10          Section. 94. Other Acts of Machination in Government Procurement. -- The

5           Section 97. Imposition of Administrative Penalties. - (a) In addition to the
6    provisions of this Article, the head of agency shall impose on bidders or prospective
7    bidders, the administrative penalty of suspension for one (1) year for the first offense,
8    and suspension of two (2) years for the second offense from participating in the public
9    bidding process, for the following violations:
10          1. Submission of eligibility requirements containing false information or falsified
11          documents.
12          2. Submission of bids that contain false information or falsified documents, or
13          the concealment of such information in the Bids in order to influence the
14          outcome of eligibility screening or any other stage of the public bidding.
15          3. Allowing the use of one's name, or using the name of another for purposes
16          of public bidding.
17          4. Withdrawal of a Bid, or refusal to accept an award, or enter into contract with
18          the government without justifiable cause, after he had been adjudged as having
19          submitted the Lowest Calculated Responsive Bid or Highest Rated Responsive
20          Bid.
21          5. Refusal or failure to post the required performance security within the
22          prescribed time.
23          6. Termination of the contract due to the default of the bidder.
24          Refusal to clarify or validate in writing its Bid during post-qualification within a
25          period of seven (7) calendar days from receipt of the request for clarification.
26          Any documented unsolicited attempt by a bidder to unduly influence the
27          outcome of the bidding in his favor.
28          All other acts that tend to defeat the purpose of the Competitive bidding.
29   (b) In addition to the penalty of suspension, the Bid security or the performance
30   security posted by the concerned bidder or prospective bidder shall also be forfeited.
31   (c) The Head of the Procuring Entity may delegate to the BAC the authority to impose
32   the aforementioned administrative penalties.
33            Section 98. Preventive Suspension by Head of Agency. - The head of agency
34   may preventively suspend any member of the TWG or the Secretariat, or the BAC,

38   covered by civil service laws. In all cases, procedural and substantive due process as
39   mandated by the Constitution and civil service laws, rules and regulations shall be
40   strictly observed.
