AN ACT PROHIBITING THE DETENTION OF MOTOR VEHICLE DRIVERS WHO

            ARTICLE 124 OF THE REVISED PENAL CODE

                               EXPLANATORY NOTE

        Article III, Section 14(2) of the Constitution enshrines the presumption of
innocence and the right of the accused to a speedy trial. These rights are
interconnected. A person is presumed innocent until proven guilty beyond reasonable
doubt, and the burden rests squarely upon the prosecution to establish culpability.
Until then, the State must tread carefully. Arbitrary detention is not a minor procedural
error or inconvenience which can be ignored; it is a violation of a constitutional right
and a damage to reputation, livelihood, and even peace of mind.

       In practice, individuals involved in traffic incidents are often subjected to
automatic detention and inquest proceedings, even in situations where available
evidence strongly indicates the absence of negligence. This bill seeks to address that
harsh reality.

       The proposed Defensive Driving Act of 2026 amends Article 124 of the Revised
Penal Code on Arbitrary Detention. It recognizes that in this modern age, traffic
incidents are no longer solely reconstructed through testimonial accounts. Today,
technology offers objective, contemporaneous documentation of events. Dashcams,
CCTV footage, and videos captured by bystanders have already become
commonplace. These tools can establish whether a driver was complying with traffic
laws before and during an incident. They can show speed, traffic signals, and the
sudden, unforeseeable acts of third parties.The bill therefore provides that a person

driving a motor vehicle who is involved in a traffic incident may not be detained by the
arresting officer if he or she can provide proof of defensive driving, such as dashcam
video, CCTV footage, or other credible visual evidence demonstrating compliance
with traffic rules prior to and during the incident. This bill does not offer an exemption
from investigation, nor an immunity from prosecution. It only operationalizes
Constitutional safeguards by providing against premature and unnecessary detention.

        As a case in point, in an incident on February 12, 2026, at the LRT-1 Fernando
Poe Jr. station, a driver was detained for inquest proceedings after hitting an individual
who reportedly jumped or fell onto the northbound lane of EDSA.1 Available dashcam
footage circulating online showed that the said driver was not speeding, was not
recklessly swerving, and had no reasonable opportunity to avoid the collision. Despite
this, he was subjected to custodial procedures, and only after significant public
attention and clamor on social media was he eventually released. Justice, however,
should not depend on virality. For every incident that draws public sympathy, there
are many others that do not. There are many drivers—ordinary Filipinos—who spend
days under detention, separated from their families and burdened with the stigma of
arrest, even when available evidence shows the absence of negligence.

        This measure affirms that the presumption of innocence must be given full
effect from the outset of the criminal process. Arrest or detention should not be
automatic responses to every traffic incident involving injury or death. When credible
and readily available evidence shows that a driver exercised due care and complied
with traffic laws, the government must respond with proper restraint.

         At the same time, the bill carefully balances individual rights with public safety
and accountability. It grants arresting officers the authority to acquire and process the
personal information of the driver involved in the incident, or impound the vehicle, if
necessary. It also categorically provides that a driver shall not be exonerated from any
liability under the law based solely on the evidence presented at the scene. Moreover,
this measure does not extinguish criminal, civil, or administrative liability. Injured
parties and even the heirs of victims retain full access to legal remedies, and our courts
of justice remain the ultimate arbiters of guilt or innocence.

detention facilities and jails are notoriously congested.2 Pre-trial detainees comprise

Be it enacted by the Senate and House of Representatives of the Philippines in
Congress assembled:

SECTION 1. Short Title. – This Act shall be known as the “Defensive Driving Act of
2026”.

SECTION 2. Definition of Terms. - For purposes of this Act, the following terms shall
be defined as follows:

   (a) Defensive Driving refers to a proactive approach to road safety and involves
       anticipating and responding to potential hazards, keeping an appropriate
       distance from other vehicles, and staying alert for possible risk on the road.

   (b) Dashboard Camera (DashCam) refers to a camera that is mounted in or around
       the dashboard of a motor vehicle. It faithfully records all it sees as the vehicle
       moves about its daily business.

SECTION 3. Article 124 of Act No. 385, otherwise known as the Revised Penal Code,
as amended, is hereby amended to read as follows:


                                           4
Article 124. Arbitrary detention. - Any public officer or employee who, without
legal grounds, detains a person, shall suffer;
    1. The penalty of arresto mayor in its maximum period to prision
        correccional in its minimum period, if the detention has not exceeded
        three days;
    2. The penalty of prision correccional in its medium and maximum periods,
        if the detention has continued more than three but not more than fifteen
        days;
    3. The penalty of prision mayor, if the detention has continued for more
        than fifteen days but not more than six months; and
    4. That of reclusion temporal, if the detention shall have exceeded six
        months. The commission of a crime, or violent insanity or any other
        ailment requiring the compulsory confinement of the patient in a hospital,
        shall be considered legal grounds for the detention of any person.

PROVIDED, A PERSON DRIVING A MOTOR VEHICLE WHO IS INVOLVED
IN A TRAFFIC INCIDENT, MAY NOT BE DETAINED BY THE ARRESTING
OFFICER IF HE OR SHE CAN PROVIDE PROOF OF HIS OR HER
DEFENSIVE DRIVING SUCH AS, BUT NOT LIMITED TO:

   A. A DASHBOARD CAMERA (DASHCAM) VIDEO TAKEN BY THE
      DRIVER OF THE MOTOR VEHICLE SHOWING THAT HE OR SHE
      WAS NOT VIOLATING ANY TRAFFIC LAWS BEFORE AND DURING
      THE INCIDENT;
   B. A CLOSED-CIRCUIT TELEVISION (CCTV) FOOTAGE SHOWING
      THAT THE DRIVER OF THE MOTOR VEHICLE WAS NOT
      VIOLATING ANY TRAFFIC RULES BEFORE AND DURING THE
      INCIDENT; OR
   C. ANY OTHER VIDEO FOOTAGE FROM ANY WITNESS IN THE
      VICINITY OF THE INCIDENT SHOWING THAT THE DRIVER OF THE
      VEHICLE WAS NOT VIOLATING ANY TRAFFIC RULES BEFORE
      AND DURING THE INCIDENT.

PROVIDED, FURTHER, THAT THE ARRESTING OFFICER SHALL HAVE
THE AUTHORITY TO ACQUIRE AND PROCESS THE PERSONAL
INFORMATION OF THE DRIVER OF THE VEHICLE INVOLVED IN THE
INCIDENT. THE ARRESTING OFFICER SHALL ALSO HAVE THE
AUTHORITY TO IMPOUND THE VEHICLE INVOLVED IN THE INCIDENT, IN
ACCORDANCE TO PERTINENT LAWS AND RULES AND REGULATIONS.

PROVIDED, FINALLY, THE DRIVER OF THE VEHICLE INVOLVED IN THE
TRAFFIC INCIDENT SHALL NOT BE EXONERATED FROM ANY LIABILITY

SECTION 4. Separability Clause. – If any provision of this Act is held invalid or
unconstitutional, the other provisions not so declared shall remain in force and effect.

SECTION 5. Repealing Clause. – All laws, decrees, orders, and rules and regulations
contrary to or inconsistent with the provisions of this Act are hereby repealed or
amended accordingly.

SECTION 6. Effectivity. – Notwithstanding the non-issuance of the implementing
rules and regulations, this Act shall take effect fifteen (15) days after its publication in
the Official Gazette or in a newspaper of general circulation.
