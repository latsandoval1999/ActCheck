                          EXPLANATORY NOTE

Republic Act No. 6981, otherwise known as the Witness Protection, Security
and Benefit Act, was enacted on April 24, 1991 to ensure that individuals who
possess material knowledge of crimes are afforded protection, security, and
support in exchange for their testimony. It is designed to encourage individuals
with knowledge of criminal activities to come forward and testify. To achieve
this, the Department of Justice (DOJ) is responsible for developing and
implementing the Witness Protection, Security and Benefit Program.

Three decades on, the law has fallen behind the evolving nature of crime and
the heightened risks faced by those who dare to testify. The structural and
operational gaps have undermined the State’s power to secure convictions in
serious cases. Without immediate reform, these gaps will continue to weaken
prosecutions, discourage cooperation, and entrench impunity in our justice
system.
First, the current law does not provide a mechanism for the perpetuation and
preservation of the testimony of witnesses who are elderly, seriously ill, under
threat, or otherwise unable to testify during trial. In high-risk cases, justice
can be derailed by delay. When a witness dies, becomes incapacitated, or is
silenced before trial, critical evidence is permanently lost. Without a clear
statutory authority for timely perpetuation of testimony, prosecutions of grave
crimes may collapse—not for lack of truth, but for lack of preserved evidence.

Second, Section 3 (d) excludes members of the Philippine National Police, the
Armed Forces of the Philippines, and other law enforcement agencies from
joining the program.1 This blanket exclusion weakens the fight against
corruption and syndicated crimes, particularly those involving state actors.
Honest officers who are willing to testify against wrongdoing within their own
ranks are left vulnerable and unprotected. This exclusion not only discourages
whistleblowing but also undermines the constitutional guarantee of equal
protection by denying protection solely on the basis of institutional affiliation.2

Finally, and equally urgent, the provision on financial assistance under Section
8 (b) of the law is inadequate. The current text merely provides that the DOJ
shall, “whenever practicable,” assist the witness in obtaining a means of
livelihood and that financial assistance shall be granted “in such amount and
for such duration as the Department shall determine.” This language provides
no minimum guarantee, no clear standard, and no mechanism for periodic
adjustment. In practice, this may result in inconsistent support, prolonged
uncertainty, and financial insecurity for witnesses and their families.

Witnesses who are relocated often lose employment, housing, and community
support. Without sufficient and predictable financial assistance, they may face
economic hardship that discourages cooperation or exposes them to renewed
risk. Protection must be meaningful and sustainable; otherwise, the very
objectives of the Program are undermined.

Hence, this bill which seeks to: (1) expressly authorize the perpetuation of
testimony of witnesses admitted into the Program by allowing the filing of
petitions before the Regional Trial Court; (2) extend the coverage of the

1 Rep. Act No. 6981, Sec. 3(d).
2 Section 1, Article III, 1987 Constitution.
Witness Protection, Security and Benefit Program to allow the participation of
law enforcement personnel who may serve as material witnesses against their
own agencies; (3) comprehensively establish and strengthen the rights and
privileges of witnesses before, during, and after the giving of testimony; (4)
grant witnesses the right to receive livelihood and skills training, including
appropriate technical and vocational training from the Technical Education
and Skills Development Authority (TESDA); (5) provide a guaranteed
minimum monthly allocation of One Hundred Thousand Pesos (P100,000) as
financial assistance with a provision for periodic review and adjustment
indexed to inflation; and (6) strengthen safeguards against intimidation and
retaliation by increasing the penalty for harassment of witnesses to a fine of
not more than Two Hundred Thousand Pesos (P200,000.00), without prejudice
to imprisonment and perpetual disqualification from public office in the case
of public officers.

These reforms are not merely procedural improvements; they are necessary
safeguards to ensure that justice is neither delayed nor denied. By
institutionalizing testimony preservation, expanding eligibility, and securing
reliable funding, this measure fortifies the Witness Protection, Security and
Benefit Program against present realities and emerging threats.
