                                                       EXPLANATORY NOTE

      In a country blessed with fertile soil, vast coastlines, and industrious citizens,
hunger should be an aberration, but for too many Filipino families, the uncertainty of the
next meal is routine.

       Involuntary hunger weakens more than the body. It weakens the nation. A
malnourished child struggles to learn. An underfed worker cannot perform at full
capacity. A community deprived of stable access to food becomes vulnerable to
exploitation, instability, and chronic poverty. No serious discourse on development can
ignore the foundational truth that food security underpins health, education,
productivity, and democratic participation.

        The right to adequate food is not a charitable aspiration. It is a recognized human
right inherent in every person. It has been authoritatively defined as the right “to have
regular, permanent and unrestricted access, either directly or by means of financial
purchases, to quantitatively and qualitatively adequate and sufficient food…which
ensures a physical and mental, individual and collective fulfilling and dignified life free
of fear.”1 This articulation reminds us that the right to adequate food is not satisfied by
subsistence alone. It demands stability, quality, accessibility, and dignity.

        Despite this recognition, our domestic legal framework remains fragmented.
Programs addressing agriculture, nutrition, social protection, disaster response, and
market regulation operate across different agencies without a unified statutory anchor.
While constitutional principles on social justice and human dignity provide moral
direction, they lack a comprehensive legislative structure that translates those ideals into
enforceable standards and coordinated policy.

       This bill builds on the previous measures filed since the Sixteenth Congress that
sought to establish a framework for the right to adequate food. It refines their core
principles, incorporates lessons from past deliberations, and strengthens provisions on

1   Jean Ziegler, Report by the Special Rapporteur on the Right to Food, E/CN.4/2001/53 (July 2, 2001)
institutional coordination, monitoring, and enforceability. It establishes a clear, rights-
based framework defining availability, accessibility, adequacy, sustainability, and safety
as core elements of the right to adequate food. It delineates the State’s obligations to
respect, protect, and fulfill this right.

       Crucially, it affirms that recognition without remedy is insufficient. As observed
in legal scholarship, “a human right, to be complete and fully realized, must have the
capacity to be claimed by right-holders and the capacity to hold duty-bearers
accountable.”2 The absence of remedies for hunger is itself a violation. This measure seeks
to transform the right to adequate food from a rhetorical commitment into a legally
enforceable guarantee, so that freedom and food, justice and jobs, liberty and prosperity
may truly go together.3

       A government that protects liberty must also secure the conditions that make
liberty meaningful. Freedom is fragile when the stomach is empty. Opportunity is
theoretical when nourishment is uncertain. Governance that overlooks hunger cannot
claim to have fulfilled its most basic duty.

      This measure gives operational substance to the moral conviction that no Filipino
should suffer hunger in a land capable of feeding its people.

            For these reasons, the passage of this measure is earnestly sought.

 5         Section 3. Definition of Terms. – As used in this Act:
 6
 7         (a)   Dietary needs refer to a mix of nutrients for physical and mental growth,
 8               development and maintenance, and physical activity, that follow human
 9               physiological needs at all stages throughout the life cycle, and according to
10               gender and occupation;
11
12         (b)   Food refers to solid, liquid and semi-liquid nourishment, as well as drinking
13               water, and when taken into the body serves to nourish, build and repair
14               tissues, supply energy, or regulate body processes;
15
16         (c)   Food blockade refers to an act of cutting off food supplies from a particular area
17               by force, either in part or totally;
18
19         (d)   Food emergency refers to a situation in which access to food is endangered, as
20               may be caused by natural events like drought, floods, storms, earthquakes,
21               or crop failures resulting from pests or diseases; or by human agency such as
22               internal or international armed conflict; or infectious disease outbreaks or
23               pandemics, such as Severe Acute Respiratory Syndrome (SARS) and the
24               COVID-19;
25
26         (e)   Hunger refers to a condition in which people do not get enough food to eat to
27               provide the necessary nutrients for fully productive, active and healthy living
28               due to the unavailability and inaccessibility of food, which may be: (1) acute,
29               such as during a major disaster when food supply channels are cut, (2)
30               chronic, when people are regularly not getting enough food to conduct an
31               active life for a long time, or (3) a condition of starvation, which is not having
32               enough food of any sort to eat, or undernourishment, which is having enough
33               food to eat, but of inadequate quality.
34
35         (f)   Right to adequate food refers to the right to have regular, permanent, and
36               unrestricted access, either directly or by means of financial purchases, to
37               quantitatively and qualitatively adequate, sufficient, and safe food,
38               corresponding to the cultural traditions of the people to which a consumer
39               belongs, and which ensure a person’s physical and mental well-being, as well
40               as an individual and collective, fulfilling and dignified life, free of fear;
41
42         (g)   Vulnerable groups refer to those who are particularly disadvantaged. These
43               include indigenous peoples; ethnic, linguistic, or religious minorities; persons
44               with disabilities; persons living with Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV)

 6                                      CHAPTER II
 7                             THE RIGHT TO ADEQUATE FOOD
 8
 9            Section 4. Normative Content of the Right to Adequate Food. – Every person has
10   the right to have regular, permanent and unrestricted access, either directly or by means
11   of financial purchases, to quantitatively and qualitatively adequate, sufficient and safe
12   food, corresponding to the cultural traditions of the people to which a consumer belongs,
13   and which ensure physical and mental, individual and collective, fulfilling and dignified
14   life, free of fear.
15
16           The right to adequate food is realized when every man, woman and child, alone
17   or in community with others, have physical and economic access at all times to adequate
18   food, or means for its procurement. It shall encompass availability, accessibility,
19   adequacy, safety, and sustainability. It shall not be interpreted in a narrow or restrictive
20   sense that equates it solely with caloric sufficiency but shall include nutritional quality,
21   cultural appropriateness, and stability of access.
22
23           The right to adequate food shall be realized progressively. However, the core
24   obligation is to take the necessary action to mitigate and alleviate hunger, even in times
25   of natural or other disasters. The right to be free from hunger ensures a minimum daily
26   nutritional intake and the bare survival of a person. The right to adequate food goes
27   beyond freedom from hunger.
28
29          Section 5. Food Adequacy Standard. – Central to the realization of the right to
30   adequate food is the standard of adequacy in terms of quality, quantity, cultural
31   acceptability, sustainability of food availability, and access.
32
33          The standard of adequacy includes a number of factors which are considered in
34   determining whether particular foods or diet that are accessible can be considered the
35   most appropriate under particular circumstances. Sustainability is intrinsically linked to
36   the concept of adequate food or food security, implying that food must be accessible to
37   both present and future generations.
38
39          Section 6. Availability Standard. – The concept of availability refers to the
40   possibilities either for feeding oneself directly from productive land, or other natural
41   resources, or for well-functioning distribution, processing and market systems that can
42   move food from the site of production to where it is needed in accordance with demand.
43
44          Section 7. Accessibility Standard. – The concept of food accessibility includes both



                                                           RIGHT TO ADEQUATE FOOD FRAMEWORK ACT | 3
 1   economic and physical.
 2
 3          Economic accessibility means personal or household financial costs associated
 4   with the acquisition of food for an adequate diet should be at a level such that the
 5   attainment and satisfaction of other basic needs are not threatened, or compromised.
 6   Economic accessibility applies to any acquisition pattern or entitlement through which
 7   people procure their food, and is a measure of the extent to which it is satisfactory for the
 8   enjoyment of the right to adequate food.
 9
10           Physical accessibility means that adequate food must be accessible to everyone,
11   including physically vulnerable individuals, such as infants and young children, senior
12   citizens, those belonging to the informal sector, persons with disabilities, persons with
13   chronic or life-threatening illness, and persons with mental health conditions. Victims of
14   natural disasters, people living in disaster-prone areas, and other specially disadvantaged
15   groups, and many indigenous peoples’ groups whose access to their ancestral lands is
16   threatened, need special attention and priority consideration with respect to accessibility
17   of food.
18
19         Socially vulnerable groups such as landless persons and other particularly
20   impoverished segments of the population may need attention through special programs.
21
22          Section 8. Cultural or Consumer Acceptability. – In providing food, there is a need
23   to take into account, as far as possible, perceived non-nutrient-based values attached to
24   food and food consumption and the consumers’ concerns on the nature of accessible food
25   supplies.
26
27           Food must be free from adverse substances to ensure food safety. Measures must
28   be taken to maintain, adapt or strengthen dietary diversity and appropriate consumption
29   and feeding patterns, including optimal breast-feeding, to ensure that changes in
30   availability and access to food supply do not negatively affect dietary composition and
31   intake. Care must also be taken to identify and avoid or destroy naturally occurring toxins.
32
33          Freedom from adverse substances implies that the requirements for food safety
34   and a range of protective measures are implemented to prevent contamination of
35   foodstuff through adulteration, bad environmental hygiene, or inappropriate handling at
36   different stages throughout the food chain.
37
38         Section 9. Conditions for the Exercise of the Right to Adequate Food. – Every
39   person has the right to live in conditions that shall enable them to:
40
41          (a) Acquire food directly from productive land or other natural resources, or rely
42              on well-functioning food distribution, processing and market systems, or both;
43
44          (b) Afford sufficient quantity and quality of food and to satisfy other basic needs;



                                                            RIGHT TO ADEQUATE FOOD FRAMEWORK ACT | 4
 1
 2          (c) Be protected from the risk of losing access to food, as a consequence of sudden
 3              shocks, like an economic or climatic crisis, or one that is brought about by a
 4              national health emergency, or one that caused the internal displacement of
 5              people, or cyclical events, such as seasonal food insecurity;
 6
 7          (d) Have the opportunity of good food utilization through access to adequate diet,
 8              clean water, sanitation and health care and to reach a state of nutritional well-
 9              being, where all physiological needs are met; and,
10
11          (e) Access food or diet that is the most appropriate under given circumstances, in
12              terms of their nutritional value and cultural acceptability.
13
14         Every infant, girl and boy has a right to adequate food and to optimal health,
15   development and nutrition adequate for their age, growth and development.
16
17         Every woman has a right to adequate food and adequate nutrition during
18   pregnancy and lactation.
19
20          No limitation on the right to adequate food may be allowed, unless it is provided
21   by law, is necessary for the purpose of a compelling public interest, and is compatible
22   with the nature of the right to adequate food.
23
24          Section 10. Freedom from Hunger. – Every person has a right to be free from
25   hunger. Every person suffering from hunger or undernourishment, or at risk of suffering
26   from hunger or undernourishment, is entitled to a minimum amount of food according
27   to one’s age, sex, health status, and occupation, as provided for in this Act.
28
29          Section 11. Non-discrimination. – Any distinction, exclusion, or restriction made
30   on the basis of race, color, sex, age, language, religion, political or other opinion, national
31   or social origin, property, birth or other status, which has the effect or purpose of
32   impairing or limiting the capacity of an individual to exercise the right to adequate food,
33   is unlawful and shall be sanctioned in accordance with law.
34
35         All forms of discrimination against women with regard to the guaranteed right to
36   adequate food, including less favorable treatment of women for reasons of pregnancy and
37   maternity, shall be eliminated and prevented. The equality of opportunities between men
38   and women shall be promoted.
39
40          The prohibition of discrimination shall not preclude government from adopting
41   measures designed to remedy past effects of discrimination against particular individuals
42   or groups and to promote substantive equality in the enjoyment of the right to adequate
43   food.

 6          (f) Empowerment: People have the power, capacity, capability and access means to
 7              improve their own lives, including the power to seek from the State remedies

21              for anyone whose rights are violated, as well as the guarantee of due process
22              in all legal proceedings.

24                                       CHAPTER III
25                                    STATE OBLIGATIONS
26
27          Section 13. State Obligations. –The State, as the primary duty-bearer, has the duty
28   to respect, protect and fulfill the right to adequate food, and maintain food sovereignty. It
29   has the core obligation to take the necessary action to mitigate and alleviate hunger, even
30   in times of natural or other disasters. The right to be free from hunger ensures a minimum
31   daily nutritional intake and the bare survival of a person.
32
33          (a) Respect. It is the duty of the State not to interfere with or impair the enjoyment
34              of the right to adequate food. No public authority may deprive any person of
35              food or means for its procurement, apply laws and regulations, or pursue a
36              policy or practice, in a way that could result in preventing the enjoyment of or
37              infringing the human right to adequate food, or repeal formally or suspend
38              legislation necessary for the continued enjoyment of the right to adequate food.
39
40          (b) Protect. It is the duty of the State to provide guarantees against threats and risks
41              stemming from private actors or societal forces that are controllable by State
42              action. It is bound to take preventive measures necessary to protect persons
43              whose capacities to access sufficient and adequate food or means for its
44              procurement are endangered by the acts of others. It also must review the



                                                             RIGHT TO ADEQUATE FOOD FRAMEWORK ACT | 7
 1              relevant administrative and legislative framework ensuring that activities
 2              within their competence undertaken by private actors do not infringe on the
 3              right to adequate food of others.
 4
 5          (c) Fulfill. It is the duty of the State to facilitate the enjoyment of the right to
 6              adequate food by adopting or pursuing appropriate policies and measures that
 7              promote the human right to adequate food and to create and maintain
 8              conditions under which every person can freely and regularly enjoy the right
 9              to adequate food. It is equally the duty of the State to provide for right to
10              adequate food, by adopting and putting in place measures to provide food, or
11              means for its procurement, to persons who cannot take care of their own needs
12              due to reasons beyond their control, in particular for children whose parents
13              die, disappear or otherwise no longer take care of them.
14
15          Section 14. Progressive Realization and National Targets. – The State shall pursue
16   the progressive elimination of the incidence of hunger and malnutrition. Within ten (10)
17   years from the effectivity of this Act, the State shall endeavor to achieve zero hunger,
18   subject to periodic review and adjustment based on verified national data, fiscal capacity,
19   and extraordinary circumstances.
20
21          In the implementation of this Act, priority shall be given to areas with high
22   prevalence of chronic malnutrition.
23
24         In measuring the incidence of hunger, interim benchmarks shall be established in
25   the Implementing Rules and Regulations (IRR), based on harmonized baseline data from
26   the Philippine Statistics Authority (PSA), the National Nutrition Survey, and other
27   recognized indicators.

38   progressive realization of the right to adequate food through a coherent, integrated, and

40   relating to food security, nutrition, and sustainable food systems, consistent with this Act
41   and other applicable laws.

10          Section 20. Whole-of-Government Approach for Eradicating Hunger. – To pursue

15   portfolio boundaries to achieve an integrated government response to pursue the

13          Section 22. Standards on the Minimum Amount of Food. – Within one (1) year
14   from the effectivity of this Act, the CRAF, in consultation with the Department of Social
15   Welfare and Development (DSWD), NNC, DOH, and concerned LGUs, shall issue
16   guidelines on the minimum amount of food for persons who are suffering from hunger
17   or undernourishment, or are at risk from suffering from hunger or undernourishment,
18   but who cannot take care of their own needs due to reasons beyond their control,
19   including, but not limited, to children whose parents die or disappear, or otherwise no
20   longer take care of them, senior citizens, and persons with disabilities. The guidelines shall
21   include:
22
23          (a) The exact quantity of calories, proteins and micronutrients, to which the
24              minimum amount of food will correspond, according to the age, sex, health
25              status and occupation of the person;
26          (b) A simple and accessible application or certification procedure for the minimum
27              amount of food entitlement with transparent, fair and non-discriminatory
28              eligibility, or certification criteria; and
29          (c) A relief mechanism to ensure that such individuals are provided with their
30              minimum food requirement.
31
32           Specific support measures shall be designed and adopted to prevent or
33   compensate for disadvantages that identified vulnerable persons or groups suffer from,
34   with regard to the enjoyment of their right to food. The CRAF shall review proposed
35   support measures and, where necessary, shall give further guidance, so as to ensure that
36   all groups are covered appropriately.
37
38          There shall likewise be established fair, independent, and accessible recourse
39   procedures to the Commission for complaints and appropriate remedies in case of a
40   determined violation of the right to adequate food. There shall also be an appropriate
41   monitoring and evaluation mechanism in which the DSWD shall report to the CRAF on a
42   regular basis.

28                    ACCOUNTABILITY, REMEDIES, AND PENALTIES

30          Section 28. Remedies and Cause of Action. – Any person whose right under this
31   Act is violated or threatened with violation may file the appropriate civil, criminal, or
32   administrative action before the proper court or competent body. In cases involving the
33   non-performance of a clear and specific legal duty, an aggrieved party may seek
34   mandamus under Section 32 of this Act.
35
36         The remedies provided herein are without prejudice to other remedies available
37   under existing laws.
38
39          Section 29. Prohibited Acts; Criminal Liability. – It shall be unlawful, without
40   lawful justification, for any person, whether public or private, to commit any of the
41   following acts, when such act results in, materially contributes to, or creates a clear and
42   imminent risk of the starvation, severe hunger, or unlawful denial of access to food of any
43   person or group:
44



                                                            RIGHT TO ADEQUATE FOOD FRAMEWORK ACT | 15
 1      (a) Food Blockade or Obstruction of Aid – Willfully and unlawfully cutting off, restricting,
 2          or blocking the movement, delivery, or distribution of food or water to a
 3          community or area, including deliberate obstruction of duly authorized
 4          humanitarian or government food relief operations;
 5
 6      (b) Diversion, Misuse, or Theft of Food Assistance – Knowingly diverting, converting,
 7          misappropriating, stealing, selling, trading, or otherwise misusing food, funds, or
 8          goods intended for food assistance, feeding programs, or emergency response,
 9          including through corruption, black marketeering, or similar schemes;
10
11      (c) Discriminatory Denial of Access – Willfully denying, restricting, or conditioning
12          access to any government food, nutrition, feeding, or relief program on the basis
13          of a prohibited ground under this Act, or imposing eligibility criteria that are
14          manifestly arbitrary, non-transparent, or discriminatory;
15
16      (d) Willful or Grossly Negligent Non-Implementation of Mandated Duties – For a public
17          officer or employee, willfully or through gross neglect of duty, failing or refusing
18          to perform a specific duty expressly required under this Act, its IRR, or a lawful
19          order issued pursuant thereto, after due notice and within a reasonable period,
20          when such failure results in or materially contributes to hunger, starvation, or
21          denial of access to food;
22
23      (e) Hoarding, Profiteering, or Manipulation During Emergencies – During a declared food
24          emergency, calamity, armed conflict, or comparable crisis, knowingly hoarding,
25          stockpiling, price manipulating, or profiteering in essential food items or drinking
26          water in a manner that causes or aggravates scarcity or renders access to food
27          materially unaffordable, without prejudice to enforcement under existing price
28          and consumer protection laws;
29
30      (f) Distribution of Unsafe or Unfit Food in Public Programs – Knowingly, or through gross
31          negligence, distributing expired, contaminated, adulterated, or otherwise unsafe
32          food or drinking water in public feeding programs or emergency relief operations,
33          where such distribution causes or creates a clear and imminent risk of serious
34          harm;
35
36      (g) Contamination or Destruction of Food and Water Sources – Willfully contaminating,
37          poisoning, destroying, or rendering unusable food stocks, agricultural or fisheries
38          production, or potable water sources, including through illegal dumping,
39          unlawful discharge, or other acts that foreseeably impair access to safe food or
40          water.

15          Section 30. Civil and Administrative Liabilities. –
16
17          (a)   Civil Liability – Any person who willfully or through gross negligence
18                commits any act or omission in violation of this Act, and thereby causes
19                injury, damage, or unlawful deprivation of the right to adequate food, shall
20                be liable for actual, moral, and, where appropriate, exemplary damages, in
21                accordance with the Civil Code and other applicable laws.
22
23                Civil liability under this Act shall arise only upon proof of:
24                (1) the existence of a legal duty under this Act or its IRR;
25                (2) a breach of such duty through willful act or gross negligence; and
26                (3) a causal connection between the breach and the injury suffered.
27
28                Nothing in this Section shall be construed to create strict liability in the
29                absence of fault.
30
31          (b)   Administrative Liability of Public Officers – Any public officer or employee who,
32                in the performance of official duties, willfully, through gross neglect of duty,
33                or in bad faith, obstructs, defeats, violates, or unduly delays the
34                implementation of a specific obligation expressly required under this Act or
35                its IRR shall be subject to appropriate administrative sanctions under existing
36                civil service laws, without prejudice to criminal or civil liability as may be
37                warranted.
38
39          Section 31. Liability of Juridical Persons. – If the offender is a corporation,
40   partnership, association, or other juridical entity, the officers who authorized, directed, or
41   knowingly tolerated the prohibited act shall be held personally liable, without prejudice
42   to penalties that may be imposed upon the juridical entity under applicable laws.
43
44          Section 32. Ministerial Duties; Mandamus. – Where this Act or its IRR impose a



                                                           RIGHT TO ADEQUATE FOOD FRAMEWORK ACT | 17
 1   clear and specific legal duty upon a public authority, and such authority unlawfully
 2   neglects or refuses to perform the same after due demand, an aggrieved party may file an
 3   action for mandamus to compel performance or other appropriate relief in accordance
 4   with the Rules of Court.

 7   mandated by law, leaving no room for the exercise of discretion as to the performance of
