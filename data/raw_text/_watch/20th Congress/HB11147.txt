                                         EXPLANATORY NOTE

        The Constitution recognizes education as a fundamental instrument of national development
and directs the State to establish and maintain an educational system that is responsive to the needs
of the people and society. In an increasingly technology-driven and rapidly changing economy, this
responsibility extends beyond expanding access to education. It also requires ensuring that the
knowledge, skills, and competencies acquired by Filipinos remain relevant to the changing demands
of work and enable them to obtain productive, decent, and sustainable livelihoods. The Philippines
has made significant gains in educational access and attainment. Nevertheless, persistent
unemployment, underemployment, skills shortages, and education-to-employment mismatches
continue to challenge the country's human capital development. These conditions suggest that the
issue is not simply whether Filipinos have access to education, but whether education and training
are sufficiently connected to labor-market opportunities and the evolving requirements of the
economy.

        Data from the Philippine Statistics Authority (PSA) underscore the continuing challenge. In
January 2026, the unemployment rate stood at 5.8 percent, equivalent to approximately 2.96 million
unemployed Filipinos. In May 2026, approximately 2.50 million Filipinos remained unemployed,
while the underemployment rate stood at 12.2 percent. These figures demonstrate that the challenge
extends beyond creating jobs; it also involves improving the quality of employment and ensuring
that workers are able to find jobs that appropriately utilize their skills and provide opportunities for
advancement.1. 2

        The concern is particularly significant as the Philippine economy undergoes technological
and structural transformation. The World Bank's Philippines Country Growth and Jobs Report 2025
identified skills scarcity as one of the constraints affecting technology adoption and productivity
among Philippine firms. It emphasized the need to address skills mismatches and strengthen human
capital to support the creation of better and more productive jobs.3 Earlier World Bank research
likewise found that Philippine employers experienced difficulty finding workers with the technical
and socioemotional competencies required in the workplace, including communication,
interpersonal skills, work ethic, adaptability, and other capabilities necessary for effective

         The education-employment mismatch has also been recognized by Philippine stakeholders.
The Philippine Business for Education has consistently called attention to the gap between the
competencies developed through education and the requirements of employers and has supported
initiatives involving stronger industry participation and skills-based approaches to workforce
development. The Philippine Institute for Development Studies has likewise identified skills
mismatch as an issue affecting Philippine economic growth and the efficient utilization of human
capital.5

         The challenge, therefore, is not necessarily the absence of programs or institutions. Rather,
it is the need for stronger coherence among the country's education, technical-vocational training,
labor-market information, and employment systems. A stronger education-to-employment
framework can help ensure that labor-market information is meaningfully considered in curriculum
development, program review, quality assurance, skills training, career guidance, and workforce
planning. Reliable information concerning occupational demand, skills shortages, vacancies, wages,
emerging occupations, and regional labor-market conditions can enable educational and training
institutions to make better-informed decisions while allowing government to identify areas requiring
intervention. However, should not reduce education to a short-term response to employer vacancies.
Educational institutions serve broader purposes, including intellectual development, professional
formation, research, citizenship, creativity, and lifelong learning. Accordingly, labor-market
information should be treated as an important source of evidence for continuous improvement, not
as the sole or mechanical determinant of academic decisions. Academic freedom, institutional
autonomy, diverse educational missions, and learner choice must remain protected.

        The transition from education to employment likewise requires greater attention. Possession
of a qualification does not automatically guarantee successful entry into the workforce. Students and
graduates may encounter barriers involving insufficient workplace experience, limited access to
career information and professional networks, geographic constraints, or differences between
classroom instruction and actual workplace requirements. Strengthening work-integrated learning,
apprenticeships, internships, enterprise-based training, career guidance, job placement, and
recognition of prior learning can help address these barriers.

        The proposed Comprehensive Education-to-Employment Alignment Act seeks to respond to
these challenges by strengthening coherence among the existing education, training, and labor-
market systems. It seeks to encourage closer coordination among the Commission on Higher
Education (CHED), TESDA, and the Department of Labor and Employment (DOLE), while
respecting their respective statutory mandates and preserving the autonomy and academic freedom
of educational institutions. The measure likewise recognizes that education-to-employment
challenges differ across regions, sectors, and learner populations. Labor-market conditions and skills
requirements vary between regions and industries; consequently, national policy should provide a
coherent framework while allowing appropriate regional and sectoral responsiveness. Particular
attention should also be given to first-time jobseekers, displaced workers, disadvantaged learners,
and workers whose occupations are being transformed by technological or economic change.

       Ultimately, the objective of the proposed measure is not merely to increase the number of
graduates who obtain their first job. It is to strengthen an education and workforce-development
system capable of producing relevant competencies, facilitating successful transitions into work,

 1   SECTION 1. Short Title. - This Act shall be known as the "Comprehensive Education-to-
 2   Employment Alignment Act."
 3   SEC. 2. Declaration of Policy. - It is the policy of the State to advance education and workforce
 4   development as shared national responsibilities and centrals pillars of national development, social
 5   mobility, and human dignity. Higher education, technical-vocational education and training, and
 6   workforce development shall function as an integrated and mutually reinforcing system that enables
 7   every learner to attain productive, decent, and sustainable employment, meaningful livelihood, and
 8   lifelong economic participation.

 9   The State shall ensure the coherent alignment of quality assurance, curriculum, program delivery,
10   and credential recognition across the full span of education, training, and employment. Such
11   alignment shall be pursued within the respective statutory mandates of the Commission on Higher
12   Education (CHED), the Technical Education and Skills Development Authority (TESDA), and the
13   Department of Labor and Employment (DOLE), guided by labor market realities, national
14   development priorities, and technological transformation, while respecting academic freedom,
15   institutional autonomy, and the diversity of educational missions.

16   SEC. 3. Education-to-Employment Framework. – An Education-to-Employment Framework,
17   hereinafter referred to as the Framework, is hereby established as the national policy architecture
18   for ensuring that education and training operate in a coherent, integrated, and outcomes-oriented
19   manner from initial learning entry through workforce participation and lifelong skills upgrading.

20   The Framework shall apply to all public and private higher education institutions, technical-
21   vocational institutions, training providers, and all post-secondary learning programs, whether
22   degree-granting or non-degree-granting, including alternative learning pathways and industry-
23   recognized training arrangements authorized by law.

24   The Framework shall ensure coherence and alignment among the following:

25      (a) Learning Standards and Competency Frameworks – These refer to the definitions and
26          determination of the knowledge, skills, values, and competencies required for effective
27          participation in the labor market and for lifelong learning, and which shall be periodically
28          reviewed to reflect technological change and national development priorities;
29      (b) Curriculum Design and Delivery – These refer to an assessment of labor market needs
30          which shall be outcomes-based, competency-aligned, and responsive to labor market needs,
31          and which may incorporate work-integrated learning, apprenticeships, internships,
32          simulation, and blended modalities, consistent with minimum academic standards and
33          applicable professional requirements;
34      (c) Quality Assurance and Accreditation – This refers to the overall system of quality
35          assurance to ensure meeting professional standards and the corresponding system of
36          accreditation which shall assess not only academic rigor and institutional capacity but also
37          workforce relevance, program effectiveness, and graduate outcomes, supporting continuous
38          improvement rather than mere regulatory compliance; and
39      (d) Employment and Lifelong Learning Outcomes – These refer to the corresponding
40          learning outcomes and rubrics of performance, including employability, job quality, skills
41          adaptability, career mobility, and access to upskilling and reskilling, which shall be
42          recognized as essential indicators of program effectiveness and societal value.

43   Provided that the Framework shall be guided by labor market intelligence, industry engagement,
44   and evidence-based policy, and shall take into account regional and sectoral conditions, priority
45   industries, and inclusive development goals.

46   Sec. 4. Institutional Responsibilities. – CHED, TESDA, and DOLE shall jointly implement this
47   Act, each within its respective statutory mandate. Their specific responsibilities are as follows:

48      (a) CHED shall exercise primary jurisdiction over higher education policy, academic program
49          standards, outcomes-based education, and higher education quality assurance, including
50          program approval, monitoring, evaluation, and accreditation reform;

51      (b) TESDA shall exercise primary jurisdiction over technical-vocational education and training,
52          including competency-based standards, training regulations, assessment and certification
53          systems, quality assurance of non-degree and skills-oriented programs, and refinement of
54          program registration and external accreditation mechanisms consistent with national
55          qualifications frameworks; and

56      (c) DOLE shall serve as the principal source and coordinating agency for labor market
57          intelligence, including employment statistics, occupational demand and supply data, skills
58          shortage analyses, vacancy trends, wage information, and projections of emerging and
59          declining occupations. DOLE shall provide such data to CHED and TESDA in a timely,
60          systematic, and usable manner, and shall support school-to-work transition through its
61          employment facilitation and labor market services.

62   Provided, that nothing in this section shall impair institutional autonomy, academic freedom, or the
63   diversity of educational missions, nor authorize undue interference in academic decision-making.

64   SEC. 5. Inter-Agency Coordination. – CHED, TESDA, and DOLE shall establish and maintain a
65   structured inter-agency coordination mechanism to ensure policy coherence, joint planning, and
66   synchronized implementation across education, training, and employment systems. The mechanism
67   shall facilitate regular consultations, information-sharing, harmonized issuance of policies, and
68   development of interoperable data systems, without duplicating the functions or regulatory
69   processes of any agency.

70   Within ninety (90) days from the effectivity of this Act, CHED, TESDA, and DOLE shall execute
71   a joint memorandum of agreement operationalizing this mechanism, specifying meeting schedules,
72   data-sharing protocols, and coordination procedures. The mechanism shall operate on a co-equal
73   basis, with decisions made by consensus and within each agency's statutory mandate.

74   SEC. 6. Quality Assurance and Accreditation Reform. – CHED and TESDA shall, within their
75   respective jurisdictions, recalibrate and modernize quality assurance and accreditation systems to
76   establish a direct and enduring link between education quality, workforce outcomes, and societal
77   relevance. Quality assurance and accreditation shall function not merely as regulatory instruments
78   but as strategic mechanisms for continuous improvement, workforce alignment, and public
79   accountability.

80   Accreditation standards and processes shall explicitly incorporate the following:

81      (a) Substantive weight given to employability, workforce readiness, skills adaptability, and
82          career progression, in addition to academic and institutional inputs;
83      (b) Ladderized pathways across technical-vocational education and training and higher
84          education, including recognition of prior learning to encourage learner progression and
85          upskilling, consistent with Republic Acts No. 10647 and No. 12124;
 86      (c) Recognition of programs with strong and sustained industry engagement, work-integrated
 87          learning, enterprise partnerships, or professional alignment;
 88      (d) Developmental feedback and outcomes assessment rather than prescriptive or purely
 89          compliance-based requirements, to support innovation and continuous improvement; and
 90      (e) Streamlining of accreditation and program registration processes to reduce unnecessary
 91          regulatory burden and eliminate duplicative documentation.

 92   Provided, that any reform to accreditation systems shall be consistent with the respective statutory
 93   mandates of CHED and TESDA and shall not undermine the independence and credibility of
 94   recognized accrediting agencies.

 95   SEC. 7. Expanded Quality Assurance Criteria. – CHED and TESDA shall expand their quality
 96   assurance criteria to systematically incorporate the following workforce relevance indicators
 97   alongside established academic and institutional standards:

 98      (a) Graduate employment outcomes, including employment rates, underemployment rates, and
 99          job-matching quality, assessed using labor market information, tracer studies, and
100          employment data provided or validated by DOLE;
101      (b) Curriculum alignment with industry-validated competencies, national qualifications
102          frameworks, and applicable professional or licensure requirements, including the regularity
103          of curriculum updates;
104      (c) Work-integrated learning, including the extent, quality, duration, and workforce relevance
105          of apprenticeships, internships, and other forms of experiential learning appropriate to the
106          program and sector;
107      (d) Employer and industry participation in curriculum development, program design, and
108          review, including employer feedback on graduate readiness; and

109      (e) Regional and sectoral responsiveness, including alignment with national and local
110          development plans, priority industries, and inclusive growth objectives.

111   Provided, that workforce relevance indicators shall be contextualized according to institutional
112   typology, program nature, and learner profile, and shall not be applied uniformly or mechanically.
113   Provided further, that these indicators shall support continuous improvement, and shall not result in
114   the withdrawal of program recognition or accreditation without due process and reasonable
115   transition mechanisms.

116   SEC. 8. Differentiated Quality Assurance Pathways. – CHED and TESDA shall establish
117   differentiated quality assurance and accreditation pathways for emerging, priority, and rapidly
118   evolving sectors, including digital technologies, health and care services, advanced manufacturing,
119   agriculture and agri-technology, green and climate-resilient industries, and creative and knowledge-
120   based sectors. Such pathways may include accelerated or adaptive accreditation cycles, modular or
121   stackable accreditation approaches, and provisional or pilot accreditation mechanisms to allow
122   innovation while ensuring minimum quality thresholds.

123   Provided, that in sectors where accrediting bodies or formal program standards are nascent, CHED
124   and TESDA shall provide technical and institutional support, including grants where appropriate, to
125   facilitate their establishment. Provided further, that differentiated pathways shall be implemented in
126   a manner that ensures quality, protects learners, and maintains public trust.

127   SEC. 9. Mandatory Graduate Tracer Studies and Employer Feedback. –

128      (a) Mandatory Conduct. All covered higher education institutions and technical-vocational
129          institutions and training providers shall conduct regular and systematic graduate tracer
130          studies and structured employer feedback surveys as integral components of their quality
131          assurance and continuous improvement processes.
132      (b) Components and Composition. Graduate tracer studies shall generate reliable and
133          actionable data on the employment status, job relevance, skills utilization, job quality, and
134          career progression of graduates within a defined period after program completion, subject to
135          applicable data privacy laws. Employer feedback mechanisms shall assess graduate
136          preparedness, workplace adaptability, technical and transversal competencies, and capacity
137          for continuous learning, as observed in actual employment settings

138      (c) Mandatory Submission of Report. Submission of Report Findings shall be formally
139          submitted to CHED or TESDA, as applicable, and shall be meaningfully integrated into
140          curriculum review, program redesign, and faculty development. Aggregated data shall be
141          utilized by CHED and TESDA to inform quality assurance standards, accreditation
142          processes, and national policy. Provided, that tracer study and employer feedback
143          requirements shall be calibrated according to institutional typology, program nature, and
144          scale of operations, and shall not impose disproportionate administrative burden, particularly
145          on small or developing institutions.

146   SEC. 10. Curriculum-to-Occupation Mapping. – CHED and TESDA shall require all covered
147   institutions to undertake and maintain curriculum-to-occupation mapping, which refers to the
148   documented correspondence between program learning outcomes and recognized occupations, job
149   roles, or competency standards relevant to the labor market, as a mandatory component of program
150   development, approval, review, and quality assurance processes. Such mapping shall demonstrate
151   clear and evidence-based alignment between what programs teach and what employers and the labor
152   market require.

153   Programs that are unable to demonstrate reasonable occupational alignment, as determined in
154   accordance with evidence-based guidelines issued by CHED or TESDA, shall be subject to review,
155   redesign, consolidation, or phase-out, undertaken in a developmental manner with the primary
156   objective of improving relevance and outcomes rather than imposing punitive measures.

157   Provided, that any decision to redesign, consolidate, or phase out a program shall be implemented
158   with due regard for learner protection, including reasonable transition arrangements, teach-out plans,
159   and credit transfer or bridging options.

160   Sec. 11. Continuous Program Review. – CHED and TESDA shall require all covered academic and
161   training programs to undergo systematic and outcomes-oriented program review at least every three
162   (3) years to ensure continued relevance, quality, and alignment with labor market needs and national
163   development priorities.

164   At a minimum, each review shall assess the following:

165      (a) Labor market demand, informed by intelligence provided or validated by DOLE, including
166          sectoral, regional, and occupational trends;
167      (b) Currency of curricula with respect to technological change, evolving occupational standards,
168          and emerging competencies;
169      (c) Quality and sufficiency of work-integrated learning components, including apprenticeships,
170          internships, and enterprise-based training; and
171      (d) Graduate outcomes and employer feedback, including findings from tracer studies and
172          employer surveys.

173   Programs in rapidly evolving sectors, including digital technologies, health and care services,
174   advanced manufacturing, and green industries, shall be subject to shorter and more adaptive review
175   cycles, as may be determined by CHED or TESDA. Provided, that program review shall be
176   implemented with due regard for academic freedom, institutional autonomy, and learner protection.

177   Sec. 12. Rapid Curriculum Adjustment for High-Mismatch Sectors. – CHED and TESDA shall
178   establish accelerated and adaptive mechanisms for curriculum adjustment education-to-employment
179   mismatches, skills shortages, or rapid occupational change. Such mechanisms may include
180   expedited program approvals, provisional program offerings, modularized or stackable course
181   structures, and flexible modes of delivery, consistent with quality assurance standards and learner
182   protection sectors exhibiting persistent or emerging requirements.

183   Such mechanisms shall be informed by labor market intelligence, skills gap analyses, employer
184   Education-to-Employment Gap Assessment under Section 14 of this Act, and shall prioritize sectors
185   critical to national and regional development. Provided, that rapid curriculum adjustment
186   mechanisms shall not compromise academic standards, quality assurance requirements, or learner
187   protection, and shall be subject to minimum standards, monitoring arrangements, and periodic
188   review.
189   Sec. 13. Management of Low-Outcome Programs. – CHED and TESDA shall systematically
190   identify and monitor academic and training programs that exhibit consistently poor employment
191   outcomes over a reasonable review period as determined by CHED or TESDA, persistent graduate
192   underemployment, or significant job mismatch, hereinafter referred to as low-outcome programs.
193   Such identification shall be based on evidence from tracer studies, labor market data provided or
194   validated by DOLE, employer feedback, and quality assurance indicators.

195   Upon identification of a low-outcome program, CHED or TESDA, as applicable, may, in accordance
196   with guidelines duly issued for this purpose, require any of the following corrective measures:

197      (a) Program redesign or curriculum enhancement to improve alignment with labor market needs
198          and graduate outcomes;
199      (b) Adjustment or reduction of student intake pending demonstrated improvement in outcomes;
200      (c) Consolidation with related or complementary programs;
201      (d) Transformation into alternative learning pathways or skills programs; or

202      (e) Gradual phase-out of the program, subject to the learner protection measures prescribed
203          below.

204   Any corrective action shall be undertaken in a developmental and evidence-based manner.
205   Institutions shall be afforded due process, reasonable timelines for compliance, and access to
206   technical assistance where appropriate. Provided, that in all cases, affected learners shall be
207   protected through approved teach-out arrangements, credit transfer or bridging options, and
208   articulation to related programs, to ensure continuity of studies without undue disadvantage.

209   Sec. 14. National Education-to-Employment Gap Assessment. – CHED, TESDA, and DOLE shall
210   jointly conduct a National Education-to-Employment Gap Assessment at least once every three (3)
211   years to identify and analyze mismatches between educational and training offerings and actual labor
212   market demand. The Assessment shall serve as a core evidence-based planning tool for the agencies
213   in carrying out their respective mandates under this Act.

214   The Assessment shall include:

215      (a) Analysis of skills shortages and surpluses by sector, occupation, and region, including
216          current and projected workforce demand;
217      (b) Identification of low-outcome programs producing               graduates    with   persistent
218          underemployment, unemployment, or job mismatch;
219      (c) Analysis of occupations with high vacancy rates despite available graduate supply, including
220          barriers such as credential misalignment or geographic mismatch; and

221      (d) Identification of emerging occupations insufficiently addressed by existing curricula or
222          training regulations, particularly in priority and fast-changing sectors.

223   The results of the Assessment shall be formally documented and publicly disseminated, and shall
224   directly inform:

225      (a) Curriculum review and enhancement under Section 11;
226      (b) Program rationalization and phase-out decisions under Sections 10 and 13;
227      (c) Rapid curriculum adjustment under Section 12;

228      (d) Funding and scholarship prioritization by CHED, TESDA, and other relevant agencies; and
229      (e) Targeted workforce development interventions by DOLE.

232   applied with due regard for institutional autonomy, academic freedom, and regional and sectoral

278   SEC. 18. Labor Market Intelligence and Data Integration. – DOLE shall serve as the primary

281   information, and projections of emerging and declining occupations. DOLE shall generate, validate,
282   and regularly update such data using reliable methodologies and employer inputs, consistent with

285   CHED and TESDA shall systematically utilize labor market intelligence from DOLE to inform

289   databases, dashboards, and standardized indicators, to enable timely and accurate exchange of
290   information across education, training, and employment systems.
291   Labor market intelligence shall be disaggregated, where feasible, by sector, occupation, region,
292   gender, and age group to support targeted, inclusive, and regionally responsive policy interventions.
293   Provided, that labor market intelligence shall serve as a strategic planning and improvement tool
294   and not as the sole or mechanical basis for academic decisions, and shall be interpreted with due
295   regard for institutional mission, learner pathways, and long-term human development goals.

296   SEC. 19. Accountability for Education-to-Employment Outcomes. – CHED and TESDA shall
297   integrate education-to-employment outcome indicators into institutional and program-level quality
298   assurance systems as an essential component of continuous improvement and evidence-based
299   policy. Such indicators may include graduate employment and underemployment rates, job
300   relevance and skills utilization, career progression, employer feedback, and alignment with labor
301   market demand, selected and applied with methodological rigor and contextual sensitivity.

302   These indicators shall be used primarily for developmental purposes, including identification of
303   strengths and gaps, curriculum enhancement, program redesign, and capacity-building
304   interventions. They shall not be applied in a mechanistic or punitive manner, nor used as the sole
305   basis for sanctions, funding withdrawal, or adverse regulatory action.

306   SEC. 20. Annual Education-to-Employment Alignment Report. – CHED, TESDA, and DOLE
307   shall jointly prepare and submit to the Senate and the House of Representatives an Annual

309   instrument under this Act.

310   The Report shall contain: (a) a summary of identified education-to-employment gaps, priority skills
311   mismatches, and regional or sectoral disparities; (b) the policies, programs, and corrective actions
312   undertaken in response; (c) trends in employment and workforce outcomes based on available data;
313   (d) the implementation status of this Act; and (e) recommended legislative, regulatory, or budgetary
314   measures to address emerging challenges and support priority sectors and regions.

315   SEC. 21. Capacity Building and Institutional Support. – CHED and TESDA, in coordination with
316   DOLE, shall provide and institutionalize capacity-building programs and technical assistance to
317   support the effective implementation of workforce-responsive quality assurance systems and
318   curriculum reform under this Act. Such support shall include training and professional development
319   for faculty, trainers, administrators, and quality assurance personnel; technical guidance on
320   curriculum redesign and outcomes-based education; assistance in the use of labor market
321   intelligence; and support for the development of industry partnerships and work-integrated learning
322   arrangements. Subject to existing laws and available appropriations, incentives may be provided to
323   encourage innovation and sustained improvement in workforce alignment.

324   SEC. 22. Funding. – The funds necessary for the implementation of this Act shall be included in

327   SEC. 23. Implementing Rules and Regulations. – CHED, TESDA, and DOLE shall jointly
328   promulgate the Implementing Rules and Regulations of this Act within one hundred eighty (180)
329   days from its effectivity. In promulgating the Implementing Rules and Regulations, the agencies
330   shall conduct public consultations with higher education institutions, technical-vocational
331   institutions, employers, labor organizations, and learner groups.

332   SEC. 24. Separability Clause. - If any part or provision of this Act which shall be held to be invalid
333   or unconstitutional, other parts or provisions hereof which are not affected thereby shall continue to
334   be in force and effect.
335   SEC. 24. Repealing Clause. – All other laws, decrees, ordinances, administrative order, rules and
336   regulations, or any part thereof, which are inconsistent with this Act are hereby repealed or amended
337   accordingly.
338   SEC. 25. Construction. - In the interpretation of this law, the best interest of the child shall prevail.
339   SEC. 26. Effectivity. - This Act shall take effect fifteen (15) days after its publication in the Official
340   Gazette and at least two (2) newspapers of national circulation.
