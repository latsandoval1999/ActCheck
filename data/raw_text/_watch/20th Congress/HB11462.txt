                                        EXPLANATORY NOTE

       The Constitution recognizes that every Filipino has the right to health and to a
balanced and healthful ecology. Specifically Section 15, Article II directs the State to
protect and promote the right to health, while Section 16 mandates the State to protect
and advance the right of the people to a balanced and healthful ecology.

       This constitutional responsibility takes on particular importance as the Philippines
becomes increasingly dependent on electronic devices and digital technologies. The
growth of electronics, however, also means a growing stream of discarded computers,
mobile phones, appliances, batteries, spare parts and other electronic products. In 2022
alone, the Philippines generated approximately 537,000 tonnes of e-waste, equivalent to
4.7 kilograms per capita.1 But the problem appears more urgent when viewed from a
global perspective. Reports from the Global E-waste Monitor 2024 show that a record 62
million tonnes of e-waste were generated globally in 2022, representing an 82 percent
increase from our generation in 2010.2 Without intervention, the volume is projected to
reach 82 million tonnes by 2030. The scale is difficult to ignore: the 62 million tonnes
generated in 2022 would fill an estimated 1.55 million 40-ton trucks, which are enough to
form a bumper-to-bumper line around the Earth’s equator.3

       Despite these, the Philippines has yet to enact a comprehensive law governing e-
waste. Apart from environmental consequences, this gap also presents a pressing public
health concern. Electronic products may contain lead, mercury, and other hazardous
substances. When discarded, dismantled, or burned without appropriate safeguards,

respiratory effects, adverse birth outcomes, and effects on the immune system.4 Children,

preventive public health measure and an investment in the health of the next generation.

      Existing environmental laws provide important foundations, but their scopes still
leave a gap: Republic Act No. 9003 or the Ecological Solid Waste Management Act of
2000 primarily governs municipal solid waste, Republic Act No. 6969 or the Toxic
Substances and Hazardous and Nuclear Wastes Control Act of 1990 addresses toxic
substances and hazardous wastes largely in industrial and chemical contexts, while
Republic Act No. 11898 or the Extended Producer Responsibility Act of 2022 established
extended producer responsibility for plastic packaging but excludes electronics.

       The proposed National Electronic Waste Management Act fills that gap by
providing rules specifically responsive to the distinct environmental and health risks and
recovery potential of e-waste. Specifically, it creates a national framework for the proper
segregation, collection, transport, storage, reuse, recycling, and disposal of e-waste;
prohibits its mixed disposal with ordinary municipal waste; requires consumers and
establishments to surrender e-waste through authorized collection and treatment
channels; and assigns defined responsibilities to the DENR, DTI, LGUs, DOLE, TESDA,
and other relevant agencies.

       The measure also recognizes that the government cannot address the e-waste
problem alone. It institutionalizes Extended Producer Responsibility (EPR) by requiring
manufacturers, brand owners, and importers to establish or participate in systems for the
recovery, recycling, or safe disposal of the electronic products they introduce into the
Philippine market. Progressive recovery targets and Producer Responsibility
Organizations provide a mechanism for producers to assume responsibility beyond the
point of sale and encourage better product design, reuse, material recovery, and
circularity.

       At the same time, the bill does not overlook the workers already engaged in
informal recycling. It provides for their training, provision of appropriate safety equipment,
accreditation, and integration into formal collection and recycling supply chains.
Incentives for qualified enterprises and accredited facilities further support the
development of a domestic e-waste recycling and resource-recovery industry.

        The proposed measure is, therefore, a whole-of-nation approach to a problem that
will only grow with continued technological advancement. The national government will
establish the standards and infrastructure; producers will share responsibility for the
products they place in the market; LGUs and communities will participate in proper
collection and segregation; and workers in the informal sector will be given a pathway
toward safer and more formalized livelihoods. In doing so, the bill seeks to turn e-waste

Be it enacted by the Senate and the House of Representatives of the Philippines in
Congress assembled:

SECTION 1. Short Title. – This Act shall be known as the "National Electronic Waste
Management Act."

SEC. 2. Declaration of Policy - Consistent with Section 16, Article II of the 1987
Philippine Constitution to protect and advance the right of the people to a balanced and
healthful ecology, it is the policy of the State to:
   a) institutionalize a national framework for the segregation, collection, transport,
       storage, reuse, recycling, and environmentally sound disposal of Electronic Waste
       (E-waste);
   b) Adopt the principle of Extended Producer Responsibility (EPR) by holding
       manufacturers, importers, and distributors accountable for the lifecycle of their
       electronic products;
   c) Protect informal waste handlers and surrounding communities from hazardous
       chemical        exposures                     associated with unsafe       e-
       waste dismantling; and
   d) Promote resource recovery, urban mining, and the transition toward a circular
       economy.

SEC. 3. Definition of Terms. – As used in this Act:
   a) Electrical and Electronic Equipment (EEE) – Refers to equipment dependent on
      electric currents or electromagnetic fields to work properly, including household
      appliances, IT and telecommunications equipment, consumer electronics, and
      medical devices.




                                           4
   b) Electronic Waste or E-Waste – Refers to discarded, obsolete, broken, or end-of-
      life EEE, including all components, subassemblies, and consumables that are part
      of the product at the time of discarding.
   c) Extended Producer Responsibility (EPR) – Refers to an environmental policy
      approach in which a producer's responsibility for a product is extended to the post-
      consumer stage of its lifecycle.
   d) Obliged Enterprises – Refers to manufacturers, brand owners, or importers who
      introduce EEE into the domestic market.
   e) Treatment, Storage, and Disposal (TSD) Facility – Refers to a facility accredited
      by the Department of Environment and Natural Resources (DENR) equipped to
      safely handle, process, recycle, or dispose of hazardous wastes, including e-
      waste.


SEC. 4. Institutional Framework. – The Department of Environment and Natural
Resources (DENR) shall be the primary regulatory and enforcement agency. Through the
Environmental Management Bureau (EMB), the DENR shall establish standards, accredit
TSD facilities, monitor EPR compliance, and maintain a National E-Waste Registry.
The Department of Trade and Industry (DTI) shall enforce mandatory product labeling,
component standards for reusability, and monitor compliance among EEE retailers and
distributors.
Local Government Units (LGUs) shall integrate e-waste collection into their local solid
waste management plans, establish designated E-Waste Materials Recovery Facilities
(E-MRFs) or collection points, and conduct public information campaigns.


SEC. 5. Prohibition on Mixed Disposal and Landfilling. –
   a) The disposal of e-waste alongside general municipal solid waste is strictly
      prohibited.
   b) No operator of a sanitary landfill, open dump, or waste-to-energy facility shall
      accept unsegregated e-waste.
   c) Consumers and commercial establishments shall surrender e-waste exclusively to
      accredited LGU collection points, retailer buy-back stations, or licensed TSD
      facilities.

SEC. 6. Extended Producer Responsibility (EPR) Framework for Electronics.
  a) Mandatory Take-Back Programs: Within twelve (12) months from the effectivity of
      this Act, all Obliged Enterprises shall establish or participate in an EPR program
      for the recovery, recycling, or safe disposal of their products.
  b) Minimum Recovery Targets: Obliged Enterprises shall meet progressive annual
      recovery targets set by the DENR, starting at a minimum of twenty percent (20%)
      of their average annual sales volume within three (3) years of enactment, scaling
      up to sixty percent (60%) within eight (8) years.
  c) Producer Responsibility Organizations (PROs): Enterprises may form or join
      registered PROs to collectively fulfill their compliance obligations under this Act.


SEC. 7. Formalization of the Informal Recycling Sector. — The Department of Labor
and Employment (DOLE), in coordination with the DENR, Technical Education and Skills

Development Authority (TESDA), and LGUs, shall formulate a National Transition Plan
to:

   a) Provide technical training, safety equipment, and accreditation to informal waste
      collectors and junkshop operators;
   b) Integrate informal workers into formal collection, aggregation, and sorting supply
      chains connected to certified TSD facilities.

SEC. 8. Incentives. –
  a) Fiscal Incentives: Registered enterprises and accredited TSD facilities engaging
      in specialized e-waste processing and high-grade material extraction shall be
      eligible for fiscal incentives under the Corporate Recovery and Tax Incentives for
      Enterprises (CREATE) Act or applicable investment priority plans.
  b) Non-Fiscal Incentives: Simplified permitting and prioritization in government green
      procurement programs.


SEC. 9. Prohibited Acts and Penalties.

   a) Indiscriminate Disposal or Open Burning of E-Waste:

             First Offense: Fine of Php 10,000 to Php 50,000.

             Subsequent Offenses: Fine of Php 50,000 to Php 200,000, or community

             service, or both.

   b) Non-Compliance with EPR Obligations by Obliged Enterprises: Fine ranging from
      Php 200,000 to Php 5,000,000 depending on the scale of enterprise, or suspension
      of business permit until compliance.

   c) Operating an Unaccredited E-Waste TSD Facility or Illegal Dismantling:

             Fine of Php 500,000 to Php 2,000,000 and imprisonment of two (2) to six
             (6) years.


   SEC. 10. E-Waste Management and Innovation Fund. – A special account under
   the DENR is hereby created, to be funded through administrative fines, fees, and
   voluntary contributions. The fund shall be dedicated strictly to research and
   development on sustainable electronics, LGU infrastructure subsidies, and capacity-
   building programs for informal sector transition.


   SEC. 11. Implementing Rules and Regulations (IRR) - Within six (6) months from
   the approval of this Act, the DENR, in consultation with DTI, DILG, DOLE, DOST, and
   relevant industry stakeholders, shall promulgate the rules and regulations necessary
   for its implementation.
