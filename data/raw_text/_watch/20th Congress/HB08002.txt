                                                    EXPLANATORY NOTE

        In June 2021, the Philippines was placed on the Financial Action Task Force
(FATF)1 Grey List2. This is a sober reminder that in the global financial system, reputation
is currency. The designation carried consequences beyond technical compliance reports,
such as increased transaction scrutiny, higher costs of doing business, diminished investor
confidence, and reputational strain on legitimate Filipino enterprise.

       Through coordinated executive action and legislative reform, the Philippines
completed all of the 18 FATF Action Plan items and was formally removed from the Grey
List in February 2025. That milestone deserves recognition. But recognition is not
immunity.

        The FATF has commenced its Fourth Round of Mutual Evaluations3, which will
assess not only whether our laws are compliant on paper, but whether our institutions are
effective in practice. The Philippines is scheduled for evaluation in March 2027. The
question before us now is no longer whether we can enact reforms, but it is whether we
can sustain them with speed, competence, and institutional independence.

      These proposed amendments to the Anti-Money Laundering Act (AMLA) seek to
address the aforesaid technical compliance requirements arising from the updated FATF
Standards.

       This bill aims to strengthen the Anti-Money Laundering Council (AMLC) as an
operationally independent financial intelligence unit in three critical dimensions:
authority, capacity, and integrity.


1 The Financial Action Task Force (FATF) is an Inter-governmental organization that sets the international standards on anti-money laundering and

countering the financing of terrorism, known as the FATF 40 Recommendations. All countries are assessed as to the technical compliance of their
laws and regulations with the FATF Recommendations.
2 List of Jurisdictions Under Increased Monitoring

3 Mutual Evaluation is a peer assessment of the technical compliance of our laws and regulations with the FATF Recommendations, and the effective

implementation of these laws and regulations. The ME looks at the totality of the AML/CFT system including supervision, beneficial ownership,
criminal justice system (intelligence, investigation, prosecution, confiscation), non-profit organizations, targeted financial sanctions and
proliferation financing of weapons of mass destruction.
       First, it aligns the AMLA with updated international standards. The bill expressly
covers Virtual Asset Service Providers (VASPs), trust and company service providers,
online gambling operators, and other high-risk sectors. It clarifies the obligations of
professionals engaged in defined financial and business activities, while respecting
constitutional and professional safeguards. It modernizes the catalogue of predicate
offenses to reflect contemporary threats, including cybercrime, environmental offenses,
agricultural economic sabotage, financial fraud, and emerging digital schemes.

       Second, it equips the AMLC with calibrated and time-bound administrative tools
to prevent the dissipation of illicit assets. The authority to issue transaction suspension
orders and administrative freeze orders, subject to prompt judicial review, ensures that
the law can act before proceeds vanish across borders or through digital channels. It
authorizes the freezing and forfeiture of assets of equivalent value when the original
proceeds have been concealed, commingled, or transferred abroad.

       Third, it strengthens the country’s anti-corruption architecture. Corruption is not
merely a breach of ethics, but it is also an economic crime with national security
implications. Public funds diverted through fraud, kickbacks, ghost projects, procurement
manipulation, and illicit enrichment are laundered through financial systems to conceal
their origin. Without a strong financial intelligence and asset-tracing framework,
corruption prosecutions become hollow victories. They become mere convictions without
recovery.

        This proposed measure reinforces the capacity of the State to trace, freeze, and
forfeit the proceeds of corruption, whether held directly, layered through corporate
vehicles, concealed in real estate, or converted into digital assets. By expanding unlawful
activity coverage, strengthening subpoena authority, enhancing administrative sanctions,
and allowing limited retention of forfeited assets to augment operational capability, the
measure ensures that anti-corruption enforcement is not merely symbolic but systemic.

       If reputation is currency in global finance, integrity is capital. This measure ensures
that the country does not merely exit monitoring regimes, but graduates from it with
strengthened institutions against corruption, financial crime, and the erosion of public
trust.

     The immediate passage of this bill will give the Philippines sufficient time to
demonstrate effective implementation before the conduct of the Mutual Evaluation.
