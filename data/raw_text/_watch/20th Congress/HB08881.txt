                                    EXPLANATORY NOTE

       Article II, Section 13 of the 1987 Constitution provides that, “[t]he State
recognizes the vital role of the youth in nation-building and shall promote and protect
their physical, moral, spiritual, intellectual, and social well-being.”

       At present, the country is grappling with multiple crises that threaten the
development of our youth, and by extension, that of the country’s future. According to
a recent study and data from the Second Congressional Commission on Education
(EDCOM 2), Filipino student proficiency is in critical decline.1 By the end of school year
2024-2025, only 30.52% of Grade 3 learners were considered proficient in key
subjects, dropping to a staggering 0.40% by Grade 12, whereby mastery diminishes
as students advance.2 In the same year, 48.76% of learners were not reading at grade
level by Grade 3, with the deficit compounding over time.3

       This alarming finding is reflected even in the Programme for International
Student Assessment (PISA) 2022 results, which showed the Philippines ranking 6th to
the last in reading and mathematics and 3rd to the last in science among 81 countries.4
Further emphasizing the direness of the situation, in 2025, even the United Nations

Children’s Fund (UNICEF) warned of a “real and widespread learning crisis” in the
Philippines, citing data from a 2019 study that shows “90% of Filipino children in Grade
5 cannot read at their expected level, while 83% continue to struggle with basic
mathematics.”5 According to UNICEF Education Chief Akihiro Fushimi, the study’s
findings actually predate the COVID-19 pandemic, which has even worsened the
country’s education problem due to prolonged school closures and limited access to
alternative learning, particularly among underprivileged and isolated communities.6 If
left unaddressed, this education crisis may lead to the diminishing quality of a new
generation of Filipino learners and workers, possibly posing problems to the country’s
human resource assets and economic future.

       This systemic failure of the country’s education sector is attributed to a wide
array of factors. Among the primary factors identified, however, is stunting in the early
years, which causes irreversible neurological damage even before they enter school.
In 2023, 23.6% of Filipino children under five were stunted – a rate that remains higher
than in many countries at a similar stage of development.7 According to the World
Food Program (WFP), at least 1.7 million public school children in the Philippines
remain malnourished, which contributes to poor academic performance nationwide.8
While existing school feeding programs of the government seek to address
undernutrition, they still fall short in tackling broader nutrition issues like micronutrient
deficiencies, with Filipino school children suffering from iron, vitamin, and zinc
deficiencies that impact both the children’s learning and long-term health.9

        As such, there is a need for a more comprehensive school feeding program to
sustainably address these issues affecting Filipino school children’s health, nutrition,
and education. This bill seeks to establish a comprehensive free breakfast program
for all public school children from daycare to Grade 12. The program shall provide
public school children with nutritious and fortified breakfast meals that adhere to
national dietary and nutritional guidelines set by the Department of Health (DOH) and
the National Nutrition Council (NNC). A nutritionist shall also be an integral part of the
program, tasked with educating students, parents, and communities on healthy eating
habits, proper nutrition, the benefits of fortified meals, and sustainable food
consumption.

       Additionally, the bill mandates direct procurement from local farmers and
fisherfolk of not less than fifty percent (50%) of the produce required for the school
feeding program. By doing this, the bill aims to ensure fresh, quality products for the
public school children, sustainably support local economies, and implement the spirit
of the Sagip Saka Act.

 1         SECTION. 1. Short Title. – This Act shall be known as the “Free Breakfast
 2   Program and Sustainable Agriculture Act.”
 3
 4         SEC. 2. Declaration of Policy. – It is hereby declared the policy of the State to:
 5           (a) Prioritize the health and nutritional well-being of all public school
 6               children, recognizing that proper nutrition is crucial for their cognitive
 7               development, academic performance, and overall growth;
 8           (b) Address the pervasive issue of malnutrition, stunting, and learning
 9               poverty among Filipino children;
10           (c) Strengthen the agricultural and fisheries sectors by providing stable and
11               direct market access for local farmers and fisherfolk, thereby boosting
12               their incomes, improving their quality of life, and contributing to national
13               food security;
14           (d) Promote a holistic and integrated approach to food systems, linking local
15               production directly to consumption within communities.
16

18           (a) Accredited Farmers' and Fisherfolk's Cooperatives and Associations

36          SEC. 4. Establishment of the Free Breakfast Program. – There is hereby
37   established a Free Breakfast Program for all Public School Children from Daycare to
38   Grade 12 in all public schools nationwide, hereinafter referred to as the School
39   Feeding Program. The Department of Education (DepEd), in coordination with the
40   Department of Social Welfare and Development (DSWD) for daycare centers, shall be
41   the primary implementing agency of this Program.
42
43         SEC. 5. Program Components. – The School Feeding Program shall include,
44   but shall not be limited to the following components:

                                                2
 1             (a) Provision of Nutritious and Fortified Breakfasts. – Daily nutritious and
 2                 fortified breakfast meals shall be provided to all Public School Children,
 3                 adhering to national dietary and nutritional guidelines set by the
 4                 Department of Health (DOH) and the National Nutrition Council (NNC).
 5                 The meals shall be culturally appropriate and responsive to local food
 6                 preferences;
 7
 8             (b) Direct Procurement from Local Farmers and Fisherfolk. – To ensure
 9                 fresh, quality products, support local economies, and implement the
10                 spirit of the Sagip Saka Act, a substantial portion, not less than fifty
11                 percent (50%) of the produce required for the School Feeding Program
12                 shall be directly procured from accredited Local Farmers and Fisherfolk,
13                 or their cooperatives and associations. The procurement process shall
14                 be streamlined, transparent, and shall prioritize their fair farmgate and
15                 fisherfolk prices, consistent with the provisions and intent of Republic Act
16                 No. 11321, otherwise known as the "Sagip Saka Act;"
17
18             (c) Integrated Nutrition Education, Behavioral Transformation, and Social
19                 Mobilization. – The Program shall be complemented by a public health,
20                 nutrition, and values campaign. A nutritionist shall be an integral part of
21                 this complement, tasked with educating students, parents, and
22                 communities on healthy eating habits, proper nutrition, the benefits of
23                 fortified meals, and sustainable food consumption. Orientation-training
24                 on the objectives and mechanics of the Program shall be conducted
25                 among personnel of concerned National Government Agencies (NGAs)
26                 and Local Government Units (LGUs), parent volunteers, and parent-
27                 teacher associations (PTAs) to generate broad support. Concerned
28                 NGAs shall also endeavor to mobilize community organizations to
29                 continue nutrition education outside of the school;
30
31             (d) Monitoring and Evaluation. – A robust monitoring and evaluation system
32                 shall be established to assess the Program's impact on children's
33                 nutritional status, academic performance, school attendance, and the
34                 economic benefits of farmers and fisherfolk; and
35
36             (e) Capacity Building. – Capacity building programs shall be provided to
37                 accredited farmers' and fisherfolk's cooperatives and associations to
38                 ensure consistent supply of quality produce, adherence to food safety
39                 standards, and improved enterprise management.
