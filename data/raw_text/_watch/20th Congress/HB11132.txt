                                   EXPLANATORY NOTE

        The strength of our democratic institutions rests upon the bedrock of a judiciary that is
not only competent but, more importantly, insulated from the chilling effects of external
pressures, physical threats, and other occupational hazards. Under Section 1, Article VIII of
the 1987 Constitution, judicial power is vested in one Supreme Court and in such lower courts
as may be established by law. To ensure the effective exercise of this power, the 1987
Constitution provides several safeguards to maintain the independence of the members of the
Bench. Section 3, Article VIII also guarantees the fiscal autonomy of the Judiciary, ensuring
that appropriations may not be reduced by the legislature below the amount appropriated for
the previous year, and shall be automatically and regularly released. Furthermore, Section 10,
Article VIII protects the stability of the Bench by mandating that the salary of the Chief Justice
and the Associate Justices of the Supreme Court, and of judges of lower courts, shall be fixed
by law and shall not be decreased during their incumbency in office.

       In light of these constitutional protections, the 2025 Code of Judicial Conduct and
Accountability (CJCA) adopted by the Supreme Court of the Philippines was implemented to
ensure that judicial independence is upheld as a precondition to the rule of law. The CJCA
demands that judges stay resolute and fearless of threats, as Canon I of the CJCA requires that
a judge must pursue their actions independently, resisting and rejecting any form of undue
influence, intimidation, or pressure from whatever source.

        Despite the Herculean efforts of the members of the Bench, the grim reality on the
ground presents a bleak outlook for achieving these ideals. In recent decades, the Philippine
legal community, and the judges in particular, have faced an unprecedented surge in violence,
with at least 61 lawyers, judges, and prosecutors having been killed since 2016. From 2004 to
2021, public reports indicate that only seven (7) cases have resulted in charges filed in court.
As of 2022, documented attacks on legal professionals reached 271. These targeted assaults
have continued into 2025 and 2026. Such violence constitutes a direct assault on the judiciary
and threatens to diminish the public’s trust in the administration of justice.

        Tragically, the physical and psychological threats are not confined to the courtroom.
Judges frequently risk their lives in transit to remote court stations, navigating land, air, and
sea to serve as Acting Presiding Judges in areas lacking permanent appointees. For these
magistrates, the journey itself is as hazardous as the high-stakes cases they adjudicate. In fact,
there were noted incidents of death by accidents while judges were in transit, an ominous fact
staring the members of the Bench in the face.

         Moreover, it is worth emphasizing that the adoption of Republic Act No. 11576, or the
Act expanding the jurisdiction of first-level courts, has significantly increased the workload of
first-level court judges, who are now tasked with adjudicating the heavier, more complex civil
cases once reserved for second-level courts. The shift in the jurisdictional threshold, however,
has not created a distinction in safety. Current threat assessments reveal that judges in both first
and second-level courts face the same level of professional risks and physical threats.
Accordingly, while their legal duties are differentiated, the danger to their personal security
remains a shared and equally urgent reality across both tiers.

         Complementing the preventive approach of Republic Act No. 11691, or the “Judiciary
Marshals Act,” a monumental step toward securing the physical safety of the Bench, with a
measure that aims both to recognize the devotion to duty of magistrates and to augment their
financial resources for security, is vital to maintaining the judiciary as a public institution and
a workplace. The persistent threat of retaliation, the necessity of living under constant
surveillance, and the hazards inherent in the judicial robe remain occupational risks that even
the Judiciary Marshals Act itself recognizes by granting hazard compensation to the Marshals
themselves. If such compensation is mandated for those sworn to protect, it is only fair and just
that it be equally mandated for the judges who are the primary targets of those very threats.
This would effectively place judges on a similar footing with public health workers,1 public
social workers,2 and scientists (and science and technology personnel),3 among others, whose
indispensable presence on the ground define the fulfilment of the service-oriented purposes of
their organizations.

        Finally, this bill provides a long-overdue legislative response to the Supreme Court’s
call for protection. By instituting tax-exempt hazard compensation, the State recognizes that
while the judge is responsible for upholding ethical and legal standards, the State is accountable
for safeguarding the judge’s physical and economic security.

Be it enacted by the Senate and the House of Representatives of the Philippines in Congress
assembled:

     SECTION 1. Short Title. – This Act shall be known as the “Judicial Hazard
Compensation Act.”

        SECTION 2. Declaration of Policy. – In accordance with the State’s duty to protect
those who stand as the last bulwark of democracy, it is hereby declared the policy of the State
to promote the welfare of the judges by recognizing the life-threatening risks inherent in
judicial service. The State shall ensure that judicial independence is not compromised by
physical threats, the economic burden of personal security, and the occupational hazards
encountered while in transit to and from their places of work.

   SECTION 3. Judicial Hazard Compensation. – All duly appointed members of the
Judiciary in the first and second-level courts shall be entitled to a monthly Judicial Hazard
Compensation, which shall be a percentage of their basic monthly salary based on the following
schedule:
               a.       All duly appointed judges in Regional Trial Courts
                        (RTC) and Regional Trial Judges-at-large shall be
                        granted a hazard compensation during their incumbency
                        in the amount equivalent to thirty percent (30%) of their
                        basic monthly salary.

               b.     All duly appointed judges in the Metropolitan Trial
                      Courts (MeTC), Municipal Trial Courts in Cities
                      (MTCC), Municipal Trial Courts (MTC), Municipal
                       Circuit Trial Courts (MCTC), Shari’a Courts, and
                       Municipal Trial Judges-at-large shall be granted a hazard
                       compensation during their incumbency in the amount
                       equivalent to thirty percent (30%) of their basic monthly
                       salary.

        SECTION 4. Tax Exemption and Non-Diminution of Benefits. – The Judicial Hazard
Compensation mandated under this Act is a recognition of the occupational risks, including but
not limited to, the threat of retaliation and the perils of transit to remote stations across land,
air, and sea. As a consequence, the Judicial Hazard Compensation granted under this Act shall
not be subject to tax. Nothing in this Act shall be construed to diminish any benefit granted by
existing laws, rules, or local ordinances favorable to judges.

       SECTION 5. Appropriations. – The initial funding required to implement this Act
shall be sourced from the current appropriations of the Judiciary under the General
Appropriations Act (GAA). For subsequent years, the necessary budget shall be specifically
earmarked and included in the annual GAA of the year following its enactment.

       SECTION 6. Implementing Rules and Regulations (IRR). – Within sixty (60) days
from the effectivity of this Act, the Department of Budget and Management, in close
coordination and consultation with the Supreme Court of the Philippines, shall promulgate the
implementing rules and regulations (IRR) to ensure the seamless release of these funds.

       SECTION 7. Separability Clause. – If, for any reason, any provision of this Act is
declared unconstitutional or invalid, parts of provisions of this Act which are not affected shall
continue to be in full force and effect.

       SECTION 8. Repealing Clause. – All laws, decrees, executive orders, presidential
decrees, Implementing Rules and Regulations inconsistent with this Act are hereby repealed or
modified accordingly.

       SECTION 9. Effectivity. – This Act shall take effect fifteen (15) days after its
publication in the Official Gazette or in a newspaper of general circulation.
