                             AN ACT
PROHIBITING THE IMPOSITION AND RECOVERY OF NON-TECHNICAL SYSTEM
LOSS CHARGES FROM ELECTRICITY CONSUMERS, AMENDING FOR THE
PURPOSE SECTION 43(F) OF REPUBLIC ACT NO. 9136, OTHERWISE KNOWN
AS THE “ELECTRIC POWER INDUSTRY REFORM ACT OF 2001,” AND FOR
OTHER PURPOSES

                                  EXPLANATORY NOTE

       Electricity is an important commodity. It is a basic necessity that sustains
households, hospitals, schools, and industries alike. Precisely because of its
indispensable character, Republic Act No. 11659 classifies the distribution of electricity
as a public utility business affected with public interest1, subject to the State’s power
to regulate rates and charges to protect the consuming public from abuse.

        This protective mandate becomes even more cogent amid the reality that, as
of June 2026, the Philippines recorded the highest average electricity rate in Southeast
Asia at ₱12.43 per kilowatt-hour, driven by rising fuel costs and persistent supply
constraints.2 Worse, there is a perennial issue that an average Filipino consumer’s
electricity bill does not reflect a just and reasonable pricing, but that it instead includes
charges for certain losses (including pilferages) in the distribution or delivery of
electricity which are caused by the fault, negligence, or criminal acts of others.

      It was against this backdrop that President Ferdinand R. Marcos, Jr., in his
2026 State of the Nation Address, called on Congress to act:

     This bill answers that call, but does so with the precision that sound policy
demands.

       The system loss charge, which makes up roughly five percent (5%) of a
consumer’s electricity bill,3 is composed of two distinct components under the 2017
Edition of the Philippine Distribution Code: Technical Loss, which is the loss inherent
and unavoidable in the physical transmission and distribution of electricity through
wires, transformers, and other equipment; and Non-Technical Loss, which consists of
energy lost due to pilferage, meter tampering, erroneous meter reading, and other
causes attributable to human action, whether intentional or negligent. 4

       In other words, Technical Loss is a scientific inevitability; no amount of vigilance
by the consumer or the distribution utility (DU) can reduce it to zero5. Non-Technical
Loss, by contrast, is preventable. It is the direct consequence of pilferage committed
by third parties, defective or tampered metering, and administrative or billing error,
none of which the paying consumer has any control over, and none of which the
consumer should be made to shoulder.

       Yet under the present framework, Section 43(f) of Republic Act No. 9136, or the
“Electric Power Industry Reform Act of 2001” (EPIRA), empowers the Energy
Regulatory Commission (ERC) only to set caps on the recoverable rate of system
losses caps that, under ERC Resolution No. 10, Series of 2018, are computed by
combining Technical Loss and Non-Technical Loss, whichever is lower than the
prescribed cap, in addition to actual sub-transmission and substation losses.6 Thus,
for as long as a DU’s combined losses remain within the ERC-approved cap, it may
continue to pass on to consumers the cost of energy stolen by pilferers, lost to
tampered meters, or misrecorded through billing error. This setup, however
administratively convenient, offends basic notions of fairness and cost-causation.

        This bill therefore seeks to amend Section 43(f) of the EPIRA to expressly
confine the ERC’s rate-setting and cap-setting authority to Technical Loss alone, and
to categorically prohibit distribution utilities from imposing, charging, or recovering any
cost attributable to Non-Technical Loss from their consumers, whether through the
System Loss Rate or any other charge, fee, or cross-subsidy mechanism. The cost of
Non-Technical Loss shall instead be borne by the DU as an ordinary cost of doing
business. Consequently, this scheme can create a direct financial incentive for
distribution utilities to invest in anti-pilferage measures, meter security, and collection
efficiency, without prejudice to their right to pursue civil and criminal remedies against
persons liable for pilferage or tampering under Republic Act No. 7832, or the “Anti-
Electricity and Electric Transmission Lines/Materials Pilferage Act of 1994.”

      To ensure faithful implementation, the bill requires distribution utilities to
segregate and report their Technical and Non-Technical Losses to the ERC with
corresponding administrative penalties imposed on distribution utilities and
responsible officials who fail to comply.

        Ultimately, this bill gives concrete legislative form to the President’s directive:
that no Filipino consumer should be made to pay for electricity that was stolen,
mismeasured, or lost to another’s fault. When the very lifeline that powers every
Filipino home is priced fairly, only then can the promise of affordable and reliable
electricity be truly fulfilled.

       The immediate passage of this bill is earnestly sought.
