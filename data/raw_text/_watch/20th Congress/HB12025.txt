EXPLANATORY NOTE

Sa isang hospital counter, ang usapan ay maaaring tumagal lamang ng ilang minuto:
may pasyenteng kailangang bantayan nang wala pang isang araw, may papel na kailangang
iproseso, at may pamilya na naghihintay kung saan magmumula ang susunod na lunas. Ngunit
kapag ang baha ay lumubog sa kalsada, ang bagyo ay pumigil sa biyahe, o ang matinding init
ay nagpalala ng karamdaman, ang mas mabigat na tanong ay hindi lamang kung gaano katagal
mananatili ang pasyente sa pasilidad. Ang tanong ay kung makararating ba ang pasilidad, ang
gamot, ang health worker, at ang tamang referral sa pamilya bago maging sakuna ang isang

gamutang dapat sana ay maaga at malapit.

The central challenge is therefore not the absence of concern, skill, or existing
emergency institutions. It is the interval between disruption and restored primary care: the
hours or days in which a fixed health center is inaccessible, a transport route is impaired,
records cannot be retrieved, medicines are not where patients need them, and a local health
system must decide how to continue care while its ordinary facilities are under strain. Disaster
response is often understood as rescue, evacuation, and hospital referral. Yet public health
emergencies also unfold in the quieter continuity needs of pregnant persons, infants, older
persons, persons with disabilities, and patients whose treatment, maintenance medicines,
wound care, or clinical monitoring cannot simply be postponed until roads reopen. A clinic
that can be deployed with a prepared team, protected supplies, referral links, and usable
information systems is not merely a temporary structure. It is the local health system

remaining present when its usual doorway is closed.

May mga hakbang na dapat kilalanin at palakasin. Noong Agosto 4, 2020, nagpalabas
ang Department of Health ng Administrative Order No. 2020-0036, ang mga alituntunin sa
pag-institutionalize ng disaster risk reduction and management in health (DRRM-H) sa
province-wide at city-wide health systems.’ Alinsunod naman sa Republic Act No. 11223 0
Universal Health Care Act, may mga patakaran ang Department of Health para sa integration
ng local health systems sa province- at city-wide health systems, sa pagtatatag ng health care
provider networks, at sa pagmonitor ng pag-usad ng mga local government unit sa integration
ng health services.? These foundations show that the country has moved beyond treating
health services as isolated facilities. The remaining task is to ensure that this integrated system
has an operational, locally planned, and rapidly deployable primary-care capability when
fixed sites and ordinary mobility fail.

The Government has also begun equipping health facilities for climate-related risks.
According to Americares, the Philippines Department of Health and Americares launched the
Climate Resilience Toolkit for Health Centers in the Philippines on July 22, 2025; it is
available in English, Tagalog, Ilocano and Cebuano and provides information intended to
reduce health impacts from extreme heat, floods and typhoons.’ That achievement deserves
recognition because preparedness begins with practical guidance that health workers and
communities can use. But a toolkit, essential as it is, cannot by itself unlock supplies,
assemble a surge team, establish a service point in a cut-off community, or preserve the
continuity of a patient's care when connectivity is unavailable. This measure supplies that
operational bridge through locally integrated readiness plans for deployable community
health clinics, clear activation protocols, pre-positioned medical logistics, and rapid-response

financing aligned with Department of Health standards.

Hindi rin nagsisimula sa wala ang panukalang ito. Ayon sa Philippine Red Cross, ang
Philippine Red Cross Health in Disaster and Emergency program ay sumusuporta sa
pamahalaan sa pamamagitan ng community-based surveillance at epidemic control sa tulong
ng Red Cross 143 Community Health Volunteers, at kabilang sa emergency response nito ang
deployment ng emergency medical teams na may kakayahang mag-deploy ng field hospitals
at basic health care units, katuwang ang mga ospital at professional medical organizations.‘
May mga nakabinbing panukala rin para sa isang Department of Health emergency auxiliary
reinforcement team (kabilang ang House Bill Nos. 1547, 1926, 3225, 5155 at 11142) at para
sa mobile clinics para sa geographically isolated and disadvantaged areas. Ang Lapit-Lunas
ay hindi kapalit ng mga gawaing ito at hindi pag-aangkin sa tungkulin ng mga emergency
medical team, ng Philippine Red Cross, o ng local health authorities. Ang puwang na
pinupunan nito ay ang malinaw na local readiness system: sino ang mag-aactivate, anong
clinic package ang handa, saan nakaimbak ang supplies, paano ikokonekta ang pasyente sa
referral network, at paano mapopondohan ang unang mga araw ng tugon nang hindi

naghihintay sa post-disaster liquidity.

The strongest objection to a deployable-clinic framework is a serious one: another
program may create duplicative equipment, fragmented reporting, insecure patient data, or
clinics that cannot operate once internet access or initial funding is interrupted. This bill
answers that objection by making interoperability and sustainability part of readiness, rather
than treating them as afterthoughts. Deployable clinics must be planned within local
integrated health systems, linked to established referral pathways, and designed for offline-
capable recording and later secure synchronization under applicable patient-data protections.
Digital tools must assist, not displace, health workers and clinical judgment. The antagonist
is not the nurse trying to document care on a crowded day, the rural health worker carrying
multiple responsibilities, or the local official working with limited means. The antagonist is
the missing system that asks each of them to improvise the same solutions after every

disruption.

The architecture of this measure rests on one principle: when disaster cuts off the

supplied, staffed, protected, and funded. Accordingly, the proposed framework directs the
establishment of Department of Health-aligned local readiness standards for deployable
community health clinics; the pre-positioning and accountable management of medicines,
maternal and child health supplies, and equipment for continuity of chronic care; surge-
workforce arrangements and training; interoperable referral and reporting systems that can
function offline; patient-data safeguards; and financing mechanisms that can be accessed
promptly upon activation. It asks for disciplined coordination rather than parallel command,

AN ACT ESTABLISHING A NATIONAL FRAMEWORK FOR DISASTER-READY
DEPLOYABLE COMMUNITY HEALTH CLINICS, PROVIDING FOR LOCAL
HEALTH SYSTEM READINESS STANDARDS, PREPOSITIONED MEDICAL

LOGISTICS, SURGE HEALTH WORKFORCE, INTEROPERABLE REFERRAL
AND OFFLINE-CAPABLE HEALTH INFORMATION SYSTEMS, RAPID
RESPONSE FINANCING, AND APPROPRIATING FUNDS THEREFOR

Be it enacted by the Senate and House of Representatives of the Philippines in Congress

assembled:

SECTION 1. Short Title. — This Act shall be known as the "Lapit-Lunas Act".

SECTION 2. Declaration of Policy. — Pursuant to Article II, Section 15 and Article
XII, Section 11 of the Constitution and Republic Act No. 10121, otherwise known as the
“Philippine Disaster Risk Reduction and Management Act of 2010,” it is the policy of the
State to protect the health, dignity, continuity of care, and access to essential health services
of every person during disasters, public health emergencies, and climate-related disruptions.
The State shall enable communities whose fixed health facilities, transport routes,
communications, or ordinary supply chains are disrupted to receive timely primary care,
essential medicines, maternal and child health services, chronic-care support, disease

prevention services, and referral assistance.

The State shall establish a coordinated, locally integrated, and Department of Health-
aligned framework for deployable community health clinics that complements existing health
emergency response systems and humanitarian medical services. The framework shall
prioritize geographically isolated and disadvantaged areas, communities with disrupted
access to care, and local health systems with limited facility, transport, communications, or

health-workforce capacity. Such priority shall not be used to deny essential care to any person.

The State shall support preparedness before an emergency, rapid and accountable
mobilization during an emergency, and sustained continuity of care after an emergency. No
local government unit shall be required to establish, operate, procure for, or maintain a
deployable community health clinic without funds, supplies, personnel support, or other

assistance made available under this Act or under another lawful funding source.

SECTION 3. Definition of Terms. — As used in this Act:

(a) "Deployable Community Health Clinic" or "Clinic" refers to a temporary, mobile,
modular, transportable, or rapidly reconfigurable health-service capability that is
activated to provide primary care and related services when ordinary access to a fixed

health facility is disrupted or insufficient;

(b) "Disaster-Ready" refers to the condition in which a Clinic has an approved
readiness plan; identified personnel; minimum supplies and equipment; referral and
reporting arrangements; a means of communication; and procedures for safe

deployment, demobilization, and continuity of care;

(c) "Geographically Isolated and Disadvantaged Area” or "GIDA" refers to an area
identified by the Department of Health or a competent government authority as having
substantial barriers to access to health services due to geographic, transport, security,

communications, economic, or similar objectively verifiable conditions;

(d) "Health Emergency" refers to a disaster, public health event, climate-related event,
or other occurrence that disrupts access to health services or creates an actual or

imminent surge in health needs;

(a) Communities and facilities that may experience disrupted access to care, with first
planning and support priority for GIDAs and other areas meeting the objective access-

barrier criteria under Section 2 of this Act;

(b) Appropriate deployment sites, transport routes, alternate routes, safe water,

sanitation, communications, power, and security arrangements;

(c) The essential services that a Clinic may provide, including triage, primary care,

maternal and child health services, continuity of treatment for persons with chronic

continuity of medicines and care for persons receiving treatment, including pregnant persons,
children, older persons, persons with disabilities, and persons with chronic health needs,

SECTION 6. Surge Health Workforce. —- The Department of Health shall develop
standards for a Surge Health Workforce, including competency assessment, emergency
orientation, deployment protocols, supervision, occupational safety, and referral of personnel

needing support.

Participation in a Surge Health Workforce shall be voluntary, except as otherwise
provided by applicable law. No health worker shall be assigned beyond the worker's lawful
qualifications, scope of practice, demonstrated competency, or safe working conditions.
Personnel shall not be held administratively or professionally accountable for a failure caused
by the unavailability of medicines, equipment, transport, communications, referral capacity,
or other resource outside their reasonable control, provided that they promptly document the

condition and take reasonable measures within their authority to protect patients.

The Department of Health shall prioritize training and roster development for
personnel serving GIDAs and local health systems with limited health-workforce capacity.
Allowances, insurance, transportation, accommodation, hazard-related benefits, and other
financial support for Surge Health Workforce deployment shall be subject to the availability
of appropriations and applicable law, including Republic Act No. 7305, otherwise known as
the “Magna Carta of Public Health Workers,” and Republic Act No. 11712, otherwise known
as the “Public Health Emergency Benefits and Allowances for Health Care Workers Act.”
No local government unit shall be required to provide such benefits without an identified

lawful funding source.

transparency, auditability, and safeguards against diversion.

SECTION 9. Accountability, Monitoring, and Continuous Improvement. — The
Department of Health shall establish a monitoring framework for this Act. The framework
shall assess readiness, timeliness of deployment, continuity of essential services, supply
availability, referral completion, data protection, patient safety, and restoration of regular

local health services.

Performance assessment shall compare similarly situated local health systems and
shall recognize both achievement and measurable improvement from a documented baseline.
An adverse finding shall identify whether the cause is attributable to the concerned
implementing entity, a shortage or failure in national supply, delayed fund release, transport
or communications disruption, an unavailable referral facility, or another cause outside the
entity's reasonable control. Corrective action shall be proportionate to the cause and shall

prioritize technical assistance, system improvement, and restoration of service.

The Department of Health shall publish an annual implementation report containing
aggregated information, lessons learned, assistance provided, and recommendations for

improvement. The report shall not disclose personal health information.
