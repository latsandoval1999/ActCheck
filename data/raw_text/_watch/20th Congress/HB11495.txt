                                   EXPLANATORY NOTE

         The Animal Welfare Act of 1998 (Republic Act No. 8485), as amended in 2013,
was a landmark in Philippine law — the first to impose criminal liability for acts of
cruelty against animals. However, more than a decade of enforcement has proven
that the law is no longer equal to the scale of the problem. Institutional authority
over animal welfare remains fragmented, penalties are weak and undifferentiated,
population management tools are absent, and accountability for government-run
facilities is virtually nonexistent.1

       Science has also advanced. International standards now recognize animals as
sentient beings capable of suffering, fear, and positive emotional states. Animal
welfare policy is now based on the Five Domains framework: nutrition, physical
environment, health, behavioral expression, and mental state.2 Philippine law has not
kept pace.

       The gap between law and reality is stark. According to a report issued by Mars

government programs. Worse, in 2020, the Care and Responsibility for Animals
(CARA) Welfare Philippines, Inc. reported 3,000 cases of animal abuse, including
beating, kicking, and mutilating.5

       Hence, this Bill, which is an overhaul of the Animal Welfare Act of 1998. It
anchors Philippine policy on the Five Domains of Animal Welfare and the Philippines’
international commitments, including the Convention on Biological Diversity and the
Convention on International Trade in Endangered Species.6 It replaces a weak and
scattered enforcement architecture with a single empowered agency, and it closes
the legal loopholes that have allowed cruelty to go unpunished.

       Specifically, this Bill seeks to:
       (1) Replace the Committee on Animal Welfare with a National Commission on
           Animal Welfare, an independent and autonomous quasi-judicial agency
           with full fiscal autonomy, rule-making authority, licensing and enforcement
           powers, and the mandate to facilitate all efforts in prosecuting animal
           cruelty cases;7

       (2) Mandate local government units to establish dedicated personnel with their
           local government veterinarians, deploy fully capacitated and trained
           personnel, and implement Trap-Neuter-Vaccinate-Return programs as the
           primary, humane method of controlling stray animal populations;8

       (3) Impose strict public accountability on pounds, including mandatory spay-
           neuter prior to release, publicly accessible impoundment records, and
           minimum operating hours open to the public;

       (4) Prohibit animal experimentation in elementary schools and require all
           secondary and tertiary institutions to adhere to global standards in the
           preservation and care of animals, such as the internationally recognized
           3Rs Principle (Replacement, Reduction, Refinement);9
