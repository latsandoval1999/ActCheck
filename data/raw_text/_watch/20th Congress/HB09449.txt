                                                       EXPLANATORY NOTE

         The rapid advancement of artificial intelligence has enabled the creation of highly
realistic synthetic audio, image, and video content, commonly referred to as “deepfakes.” While
these technologies have legitimate artistic, educational, and commercial uses, they have also
been weaponized to destroy reputations, deceive voters, exploit women, defraud the public, and
erode trust in institutions.

        The danger is no longer speculative. The World Economic Forum has identified
AI-generated misinformation and disinformation as among the most severe short-term global
risks to democratic processes and public trust1. In the Philippine context, where digital media
consumption is among the highest in the world, the societal impact of malicious synthetic media
is particularly acute. Non-consensual explicit deepfakes, in particular, have inflicted grave
emotional and psychological harm, disproportionately targeting women and private individuals
who lack the means to defend themselves2.

         Empirical research demonstrates that manipulated media significantly undermines public
confidence in institutions and can distort political discourse3. A single fabricated video can
influence elections, incite public unrest, or permanently stain a person’s name. These harms are
not theoretical, they are documented, measurable, and escalating. Yet despite these risks,
legislative responses must be carefully calibrated to ensure that the cure does not become more
dangerous than the disease.

        Article III, Section 4 of the 1987 Constitution provides that “no law shall be passed
abridging the freedom of speech, of expression, or of the press.” The Supreme Court has
consistently held that prior restraint, or any government action that suppresses speech before it
is published, bears a heavy presumption against constitutional validity. In Chavez v. Gonzales

        This bill does not regulate opinion, criticism, satire, or dissent. It regulates harmful
conduct. It punishes only the malicious use of synthetic media - that is, when a person
intentionally uses fabricated digital content to deceive, defraud, defame, harass, threaten, or
sexually exploit another. By focusing on demonstrable harm and culpable intent, the measure
avoids unconstitutional overbreadth and vagueness. The Supreme Court has long required
precision in speech regulation to prevent chilling effects, and this bill adheres to that standard by
narrowly defining prohibited acts.

       For public officials and public figures, the bill adopts the constitutional standard of “actual
malice,” as recognized in New York Times v. Sullivan5. This means that no public official may
recover unless it is shown that the malicious deepfake was created with knowledge that it was
false or with reckless disregard for the truth. This safeguard ensures that political debate
remains robust, that satire remains protected, and that citizens remain free to criticize those in
power without fear.

        Rather than impose government censorship before speech occurs, the bill adopts a
measured and constitutional approach. It requires social media platforms to act upon verified
reports of malicious deepfakes after publication, through a clear notice-and-takedown directed
at platforms. By imposing post-publication remedial obligations rather than pre-publication
censorship, the measure respects constitutional boundaries while providing victims with
meaningful recourse. The State’s interest here is substantial: protecting citizens from fraud,
exploitation, and identity abuse.

       The bill also prioritizes civil remedies over broad criminal sanctions. Legal scholarship
and comparative legislation indicate that civil liability frameworks are less intrusive to speech
while still deterring harmful conduct.6 Victims are given the right to seek damages in court.
Criminal sanctions are reserved only for clearly unprotected acts such as fraud, identity theft,
and sexual exploitation. This balanced framework recognizes that while speech is protected,
deception and abuse are not.

        Ultimately, this measure recognizes that freedom of expression is not absolute. Our
freedom does not include the right to weaponize falsehood to destroy another person’s dignity
or to undermine public institutions. The Constitution protects speech, but not fraud, identity theft,

constitutional principles. This bill therefore seeks to protect individuals, preserve democratic

fundamental freedoms that define our constitutional order. This measure seeks to defend both
the Filipino people and the freedoms guaranteed to them.

                               AN ACT
  REGULATING THE MALICIOUS USE OF SYNTHETIC MEDIA AND DEEPFAKES,
     PROVIDING CIVIL REMEDIES AND CRIMINAL PENALTIES THEREFOR,
       ESTABLISHING CONTENT-NEUTRAL PLATFORM OBLIGATIONS,
                      AND FOR OTHER PURPOSES


Be it enacted by the Senate and the House of Representatives of the Philippines in
Congress assembled:

                                   CHAPTER I
                               GENERAL PROVISIONS

      Section 1. Short Title. - This Act shall be known as the "Anti-Deepfake and
Synthetic Media Regulation Act”.

       Section 2. Declaration of Policy. It is hereby declared the policy of the State to
protect the dignity, security, privacy, and democratic rights of the people from malicious
digital impersonation, fraud, and exploitation committed through synthetic media
technologies.

        The State likewise recognizes that freedom of speech, expression, and of the
press are fundamental rights guaranteed under Article III, Section 4 of the 1987
Constitution. Any regulation of synthetic media shall therefore be narrowly tailored to
address demonstrable harm, avoid prior restraint, and preserve robust political debate,
satire, artistic expression, academic inquiry, and lawful dissent.
       This Act regulates harmful conduct involving malicious synthetic media and shall
not be interpreted to impose blanket bans, speech licensing, mandatory user
registration, or viewpoint-based restrictions.

       Section 3. Definition of terms. For the purposes of this Act:
   a.​ “Actual Malice” – For public officials and public figures, liability shall attach only
       upon proof that the creator acted with knowledge of falsity or reckless disregard
       for truth
   b.​ “Election Speech” - refers to any expression, communication, satire, parody,
       commentary, advocacy, criticism, or opinion relating to:
           i.​ A candidate for public office;
          ii.​ A political party or political organization;
         iii.​ A public policy issue submitted to voters; or
         iv.​ The conduct of public officials or electoral processes.
   c.​ “Malicious Deepfake” – refers to any synthetic media created, published, or
       distributed with specific intent to:
           i.​ Deceive or defraud;
          ii.​ Defame a person through materially false representations;
         iii.​ Harass, intimidate, or threaten;
         iv.​ Facilitate identity theft;
          v.​ Sexually exploit a person without consent; or
         vi.​ Cause demonstrable and material harm to an identifiable individual.
       The term shall not include clearly identifiable parody, satire, commentary, artistic
       expression, or fictionalized entertainment.
   d.​ “Material Harm” – refers to any direct, demonstrable, and substantial injury that
       is not speculative, hypothetical, or trivial, including:
           i.​ Financial loss or property damage;
          ii.​ Loss of employment or professional opportunity directly attributable to the
               malicious synthetic media;
         iii.​ Reputational harm meeting the elements of actionable defamation;
         iv.​ Severe emotional distress arising from non-consensual sexually explicit
               synthetic media;
          v.​ Identity theft or unlawful impersonation; or
         vi.​ Material interference with a lawful election process through deception of
               verifiable factual information.
   e.​ “Notice-and-Takedown” – refers to a post-publication remedial process whereby
       a platform, upon receipt of a verified complaint meeting statutory requirements,
       acts within the prescribed period under this act to remove, restrict, or label
       malicious synthetic media.
   f.​ “Online Platform” – refers to a digital service that enables users to create,
       upload, share, or view user-generated content.
   g.​ “Synthetic Media” – refers to digitally altered or artificially generated audio,
       image, or video content that realistically depicts a person engaging in speech or
       conduct that did not occur in real life.

       Section 4. Prohibited Acts. It shall be unlawful for any person to knowingly
create, publish, distribute, or cause the distribution of malicious synthetic media with
specific intent to:
   a.​ Defraud any person or the public;
   b.​ Commit identity theft or unlawful impersonation;
   c.​ Defame another through materially false factual representation;
   d.​ Harass, threaten, or incite violence against a person;
   e.​ Produce or distribute non-consensual sexually explicit synthetic media; or
   f.​ Interfere with the integrity of elections through materially deceptive synthetic
       media designed to mislead voters regarding factual matters.


       Section 5. Exemptions. This Act shall not apply to:
   a.​ Content created for satire, parody, or artistic expression;
   b.​ Deepfakes created for educational or research purposes;
   c.​ Content where the individual depicted has given explicit, verifiable, and informed
       consent
   d.​ Political speech, unless it falls within the unprotected categories defined in
       Section 4.

      The foregoing shall not apply to similar types of content where a reasonable
person would not interpret the same as asserting actual facts, nor to content
accompanied by clear and conspicuous disclosure that it is synthetic.

       Section 6. Content-Neutral Platform Obligations. Online platforms shall
establish accessible mechanisms for users to report malicious synthetic media. Upon
receipt of a verified complaint demonstrating prima facie violation of Section 4,
supported by sufficient documentation, platforms shall act within forty-eight (48) hours
to:
          a.​ Remove or disable access to the content;
          b.​ Apply a clear and conspicuous label indicating the content is disputed
              synthetic media; or
          c.​ Temporarily restrict distribution pending review.
      Platforms shall notify the uploader and provide an opportunity to contest the
complaint. Nothing in this Act shall require platforms to monitor content proactively or to
implement pre-publication review systems.

       Section 7. Penalties. Any person who violates this act shall suffer imprisonment
of six (6) to twelve (12) years and shall pay a fine of not less than Five hundred
thousand pesos (Php 500,000.00), or both, at the discretion of the court.

        Any person aggrieved by malicious synthetic media may file a civil action for
damages, including actual, moral, and exemplary damages, and may seek injunctive
relief after publication. Courts may order the removal of malicious synthetic media upon
a finding of probable violation of this Act.

       The remedies under this Act shall be without prejudice to other remedies
available under the Civil Code, the Revised Penal Code, the Cybercrime Prevention
Act, the Safe Spaces Act, and other applicable laws.

      Section 8. Implementing Rules and Regulations. Within ninety (90) days from
the effectivity of this Act, the Department of Information and Communications
Technology (DICT), in consultation with the Department of Justice (DOJ), Department of
Science and Technology (DOST), and Department of Trade and Industry (DTI) shall
promulgate the necessary implementing rules and regulations to ensure the effective
enforcement of this Act.

       Section 9. Appropriations. The amount necessary to carry out the provisions of
this Act shall be included in the annual General Appropriations Act.

        Section 10. Separability Clause. If any provision of this Act is declared
unconstitutional or invalid, the remaining provisions shall continue to be in full force and
effect.

       Section 11. Repealing Clause. All laws, decrees, executive orders, rules and
regulations, or parts thereof inconsistent with the provisions of this Act are hereby
repealed or modified accordingly.

       Section 12. Effectivity. This Act shall take effect fifteen (15) days after its
publication in the Official Gazette or in a newspaper of general circulation.
