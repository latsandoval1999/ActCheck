                                                   EXPLANATORY NOTE

      Article II, Section 16 of the 1987 Constitution mandates the State to protect and
advance the right of the people to a balanced and healthful ecology, while Article XVI,
Section 9 directs the State to protect consumers from trade malpractices and hazardous
or substandard products. These constitutional principles recognize that environmental
stewardship and consumer protection are essential to sustainable national
development.

       In recent years, however, a growing practice known as greenwashing has
emerged as a serious threat to both objectives. Greenwashing refers to the use of false,
exaggerated, or unsubstantiated environmental claims in promoting products, services,
or business operations. Labels such as "eco-friendly," "green," "carbon neutral," "net
zero," or similar representations are often used without credible scientific basis or
independent verification, misleading consumers and creating an unfair advantage over
businesses that genuinely invest in sustainable practices.

       The international community has recognized greenwashing as a significant
barrier to effective climate action and fair competition. In 2022, the United Nations
High-Level Expert Group on the Net-Zero Emissions Commitments of Non-State
Entities1 warned that misleading environmental claims erode public trust, misdirect
investments, and delay meaningful climate action. Likewise, the Organisation for
Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD)2 has identified greenwashing as a
market failure that weakens environmental governance and distorts competition. The
European Commission likewise found that more than half of the environmental claims it

       The Philippines is not immune to these risks. As more Filipino consumers choose
products and services based on environmental considerations, businesses have
increasingly incorporated sustainability claims into advertising, packaging, and
corporate communications. While many enterprises have made genuine commitments
to responsible environmental practices, others exploit the absence of clear legal
standards by making environmental representations that cannot be independently
substantiated.

       Although Republic Act No. 7394, or the Consumer Act of the Philippines,
prohibits deceptive sales practices, it does not specifically regulate environmental
marketing claims or establish standards for substantiating such representations.
Existing environmental laws likewise focus primarily on pollution control and natural
resource management rather than the truthfulness of sustainability claims made in the
marketplace. Consequently, consumers remain vulnerable to deceptive environmental
marketing, while responsible businesses face unfair competition from those that merely
project the appearance of sustainability.

       The consequences extend beyond consumer deception. Greenwashing weakens
market incentives for innovation, discourages legitimate investments in cleaner
technologies, and undermines the country's transition toward a low-carbon,
climate-resilient economy. More importantly, it affects Filipino families and future
generations. The Philippines remains among the countries most vulnerable to the
impacts of climate change, while children and economically vulnerable communities
continue to bear its greatest consequences. Every misleading environmental claim
delays meaningful action toward sustainability and diverts consumer support away from
businesses that genuinely reduce environmental harm.

       This proposed Anti-Greenwashing and Consumer Truth Act seeks to establish
the country's first comprehensive legal framework against deceptive environmental
marketing. The measure defines prohibited greenwashing practices; requires that
environmental claims be supported by competent, credible, and verifiable scientific
evidence; prohibits deceptive environmental labels and self-created certifications;
regulates the use of claims such as "carbon neutral," "net zero," and similar
representations unless independently substantiated; and strengthens enforcement
under the Consumer Act of the Philippines.
        The bill likewise promotes transparency and accountability by requiring
businesses making environmental claims to maintain adequate supporting
documentation and authorizing the appropriate regulatory agencies to investigate,
verify, and penalize deceptive environmental marketing practices. In doing so, the
measure protects consumers, encourages responsible corporate conduct, rewards
businesses that undertake genuine sustainability initiatives, and promotes a fair and
competitive marketplace where honesty earns public trust.

        By institutionalizing standards for truthful environmental marketing and
strengthening consumer protection, this measure advances the constitutional duty of the
State to safeguard both the people's right to a balanced and healthful ecology and their
right to fair and honest commercial practices.

      In view of the foregoing, the immediate passage of this bill is earnestly sought.

                                        AN ACT
                     PROHIBITING AND PENALIZING GREENWASHING
                        AND DECEPTIVE SUSTAINABILITY CLAIMS,
        INTEGRATING CONSUMER PROTECTIONS INTO THE CONSUMER ACT OF THE
          PHILIPPINES, MANDATING TRANSPARENT DISCLOSURES IN CORPORATE
                  OPERATIONS, AND APPROPRIATING FUNDS THEREFOR

Be it enacted by the Senate and the House of Representatives of the Philippines in
Congress assembled:

                                   CHAPTER I
                               GENERAL PROVISIONS

         Section 1. Short Title. - This Act shall be known as the "Anti-Greenwashing
Act.”

       Section 2. Declaration of Policy. It is the policy of the State to protect and
advance the right of the people to a balanced and healthful ecology under Article II,
Section 16 of the Constitution, and to protect consumers against trade malpractices
under Article XVI, Section 9 of the Constitution. Towards this end, the State shall ensure
that all environmental and sustainability claims made by business enterprises in the
course of trade or commerce are accurate, substantiated, and transparent, thereby
protecting consumers, promoting fair competition, and fostering a genuinely sustainable
economy.

       Section 3. Definition of Terms. For the purposes of this Act:
   (a) Greenwashing - refers to the practice of providing false, vague, deceptive, or
       misleading information about the environmental soundness, sustainability, or
        climate impact of products, services, corporate operations, or business
        relationships.
   (b)​Carbon-Intensive Entity - refers to any business enterprise operating in
        high-emitting sectors whose operations contribute substantially to anthropogenic
        GHG emissions.
   (c)​Carbon Neutral or Net-Zero Claim - refers to any marketing or corporate
        assertion that the net GHG emissions associated with a product, service, or
        business entity are zero, or have been fully offset.
   (d)​Competent and Reliable Scientific Evidence - refers to tests, analyses,
        research, or studies that have been conducted and evaluated in an objective
        manner by persons qualified to do so, using procedures generally accepted by
        the relevant scientific discipline to yield accurate and reliable results.
   (e)​Covered Business Enterprise - refers to any corporation, partnership, sole
        proprietorship, or juridical entity engaged in commerce, industry, or trade within
        the Philippines, including foreign-owned entities operating domestically.
   (f)​ Deceptive Sustainability Claim - refers to any unsubstantiated assertion,
        symbol, or certification implying that a product, service, or business has neutral,
        positive, or reduced greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions, or is otherwise
        environmentally benign, without competent and reliable scientific evidence, as
        defined in this Section.
   (g)​Material or Materially Misleading - refers to a representation, omission, or
        practice that is likely to affect a reasonable consumer's decision to purchase,
        use, invest in, or otherwise transact with respect to a product, service, or
        business enterprise.
   (h)​Micro, Small, and Medium Enterprise (MSME) - refers to any business
        enterprise classified as a micro, small, or medium enterprise under Republic Act
        No. 9501, or its successor law, and the implementing rules of the Department of
        Trade and Industry.
   (i)​ Precautionary Principle - refers to the principle that the absence of full scientific
        certainty shall not, by itself, justify withholding disclosure of a material
        environmental or public health risk that is supported by a preponderance of the
        then-available competent and reliable scientific evidence.
   (j)​ Value Chain - refers to the full range of a covered business enterprise's direct
        and indirect greenhouse gas emissions, consistent with the Scope 1, Scope 2,
        and Scope 3 categories defined under the Greenhouse Gas Protocol Corporate
        Accounting and Reporting Standard, or its successor framework.

        Section 4. Lead Implementing Agency. — The Department of Trade and
Industry (DTI) shall be the principal implementing agency of this Act, with primary
jurisdiction over consumer-facing greenwashing claims, advertising enforcement, and
coordination with the Consumer Act of the Philippines. The Securities and Exchange
Commission (SEC) shall have primary jurisdiction over corporate disclosure,
sustainability reporting, and transition-plan compliance. The Climate Change
Commission (CCC) shall serve as the lead technical and scientific authority responsible
for verifying GHG-emissions data, decarbonization plans, and the climate integrity of all
sustainability claims.

                                    CHAPTER II
                                  PROHIBITED ACTS

       Section 5. Prohibited Acts. It shall be unlawful for any business entity operating
within the Philippines to knowingly or through gross negligence engage in the following
deceptive practices:
   (a)​Making false, vague, or misleading claims regarding the environmental
       soundness or sustainability of its products, services, or operations;
   (b)​Making unsubstantiated "carbon neutral", "net-zero", or "climate friendly" claims
       without public submission of a scientifically verified Decarbonisation Plan and an
       Annual Compliance Report mapping absolute emission cuts across its entire
       value chain;
   (c)​Displaying unsubstantiated third-party certifications, ecological labels, or
       self-created "green" stamps that do not comply with accredited national or
       international standards;
   (d)​Failing to disclose a material environmental or public health risk arising from a
       business activity, where such risk is supported by a preponderance of competent
       and reliable scientific evidence, notwithstanding the absence of full scientific
       consensus, consistent with the Precautionary Principle;
   (e)​Retaliating, directly or indirectly, against any consumer, advocate, whistleblower,
       employee, or civil-society organisation that reports suspected greenwashing or
       cooperates with any investigation under this Act.

                              CHAPTER III
           INSTITUTIONAL RESPONSIBILITIES AND THE CLEAR FUND

       Section 6. Enforcement by the Fair Trade Enforcement Bureau. — The Fair
Trade Enforcement Bureau (FTEB) of the Department of Trade and Industry shall serve
as the primary enforcement arm of this Act. In addition to its existing mandate under
Republic Act No. 7394 and Executive Order No. 913, the FTEB shall:
   (a) Receive, mediate, and adjudicate citizen, consumer, and civil-society complaints
       of greenwashing and deceptive sustainability claims under this Act, applying its
       existing Notice of Violation, Formal Charge, and administrative adjudication
       procedures;
   (b) Issue cease-and-desist orders, show-cause directives, and subpoenas in aid of
       investigations and adjudications under this Act;
   (c) Maintain a public Greenwashing Registry of enterprises found administratively or
       judicially liable under this Act, for a period of not less than five (5) years per
       listing;
   (d) Certify to the Government Procurement Policy Board (GPPB), for purposes of
       Section 10 of this Act, any covered business enterprise found liable for repeated
       violations of this Act; and
   (e) Jointly with the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) and the Climate
       Change Commission (CCC), submit an annual public report to Congress on
       compliance levels among covered business enterprises and on the enforcement
       performance of this Act, including recommended legislative or budgetary
       adjustments.

        Section 7. Interagency Coordination. — The DTI, through the FTEB, the SEC,
and the CCC shall jointly issue coordination guidelines to prevent duplicative or
conflicting proceedings arising from the same act or transaction under this Act. Where a
covered business enterprise is subject to concurrent proceedings before more than one
of the three agencies, the agency with primary jurisdiction over the specific claim under
Section 4 shall proceed, and the others shall hold their proceedings in abeyance
pending its resolution.

      Section 8. Accreditation of Third-Party Verifiers. — The Bureau of Philippine
Standards (BPS) of the DTI shall accredit third-party verifiers and certification bodies for
purposes of this Act, applying its existing accreditation standards under the Philippine
National Standards and International Organization for Standardization frameworks. The
FTEB shall rely on BPS-accredited verifiers in evaluating the sufficiency of
environmental claims and certifications submitted in its proceedings.

       Section 9. Debarment from Public Procurement. — Upon certification by the
FTEB under Section 6(d) that a covered business enterprise has been found liable for
repeated violations of this Act, the matter shall be referred to the GPPB's Blacklisting
Committee for appropriate action under Republic Act No. 12009 and its implementing
rules and regulations.

     Section 10. Establishment of the Consumer Legal Enforcement,
Accountability, and Redress (CLEAR) Fund. — The Consumer Legal Enforcement,
Accountability, and Redress (CLEAR) Fund is hereby established as provided under
Section 9 of this Act, and administered by the Bureau of the Treasury upon
recommendation of the Fund Management Committee. It shall serve as the primary
redress mechanism of the State for consumers harmed by greenwashing and deceptive
sustainability claims.

        The CLEAR Fund shall be used to provide redress and compensation to qualified
claimants, which may take the following forms:
        (b) Restitution and refunds to consumers who purchased a product or service in
reliance on a Deceptive Sustainability Claim;
        (c) Funding of independent testing, verification, and investigation of
environmental claims in aid of enforcement under this Act;
        (d) Public information and consumer-education campaigns on sustainability
claims and greenwashing indicators;
        (e) Grants to BPS-accredited third-party verifiers and to consumer-advocacy or
civil-society organisations engaged in monitoring compliance with this Act;
        (f) Rewards to whistleblowers whose reports lead to a finding of liability under
this Act; and
        (g) Capacity-building and technical support for the FTEB, SEC, CCC, and BPS in
implementing this Act.

       Section 11. Custody and Administration of the CLEAR Fund. — There is
hereby created a special trust account in the Bureau of the Treasury (BTr) to be known
as the Consumer Legal Enforcement, Accountability, and Redress (CLEAR) Fund. The
BTr shall hold and invest the CLEAR Fund, subject to disbursement only upon the
recommendation of the Fund Management Committee created.
   (a) Fund Management Committee. — There is hereby created a Fund Management
       Committee composed of a representative each from the DTI, SEC, CCC, and
       BTr, which shall determine claimant eligibility and recommend disbursements
       consistent with Section 11 of this Act. The Committee shall submit disbursement
       recommendations to the BTr and shall report annually to Congress on Fund
       utilization.
   (b) Sources of the Fund. — The CLEAR Fund shall be sourced from: (i) an initial
       appropriation of Twenty Million Pesos (Php 20,000,000.00) under the General
       Appropriations Act following the effectivity of this Act; (ii) all fines and penalties
       collected under Section 16; and (iii) donations, grants, and endowments from
       domestic and international sources, subject to existing auditing and
       accountability laws.
                                   CHAPTER IV
                           DISCLOSURE AND ENFORCEMENT

       Section 12. Mandatory Corporate Disclosure and Precautionary Reporting.
To maintain absolute transparency and protect consumer truth:
   (a)​Covered business enterprises, other than micro and small enterprises as
       classified under Section 3, shall observe a due diligence standard of care. They
       are required to measure, analyse, and publicly report their direct and indirect
       GHG emissions across their value chain (Scope 1, 2, and 3 emissions) to the
       Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) and the Climate Change
       Commission (CCC): Provided, That medium enterprises shall only be required to
       report Scope 1 and Scope 2 emissions, unless they make an express
       carbon-neutral or net-zero claim under Section 3, in which case full value-chain
       reporting shall apply.
   (b)​Under the Precautionary Principle, if scientific certainty linking a business activity
       to a plausible environmental or public health threat is not yet fully established, the
       business and the SEC, assisted by the CCC, are legally obligated to disclose to
       the public that there may be potential risks arising from the business activity.
   (c)​All high-emitting and carbon-intensive entities must submit detailed transition
       plans assessing the long-term sustainability of their operations and their direct
       impacts on human rights and climate systems.
   (d)​Failure to file, late filing, or submission of falsified or materially incomplete reports
       shall constitute a prohibited act under Section 5.

      Section 13. Proportionality for Micro, Small, and Medium Enterprises. —
Micro and small enterprises shall be exempt from the reporting requirements under
Section 12(a), without prejudice to their liability for prohibited acts under Section 5. The
SEC, in coordination with the DTI, shall provide a phase-in period of not less than two
(2) years from the effectivity of the implementing rules and regulations for medium
enterprises to comply with Section 12(a), and may issue simplified reporting templates
proportionate to enterprise size.

      Section 14. Integration with the Consumer Act of the Philippines. —
Deceptive and misleading sustainability claims and greenwashing practices prohibited
under this Act shall be legally classified as Unfair and Deceptive Sales Acts or Practices
and prosecuted under the Consumer Act of the Philippines, in addition to applicable
environmental statutes.

      Section 15. Protection of Public Participation (Anti-SLAPP). Any consumer,
environmental advocate, child-led group, or civil society organisation that exposes
greenwashing, asserts environmental rights, or files complaints under this Act shall be
protected from Strategic Lawsuits Against Public Participation (SLAPP).
   (a) Motion to Dismiss or Suspend. — A respondent in any suit who believes the
       action was filed to harass, vex, or stifle the exercise of rights protected under this
       Act may file a verified motion, within the reglementary period to file a responsive
       pleading, alleging that the suit is a SLAPP. The motion shall be resolved by the
       court or the FTEB, as the case may be, within thirty (30) days through a
       summary hearing.
   (b) Effect of Determination. — If the suit is determined to be a SLAPP, the action
       shall be dismissed, and the entity or person at whose instance the suit was filed
       shall be liable for damages equivalent to fifteen percent (15%) of its average
       annual gross income for the three (3) years preceding the filing of the suit, in
       addition to attorney's fees and costs.

       Section 16. Penalties. — Any business enterprise found, after due notice and
hearing, to have knowingly or through gross negligence violated the provisions of this
Act shall face administrative and criminal penalties, which shall accrue to the CLEAR
Fund:
   (a)​First Offense: A fine of not less than Five Hundred Thousand Pesos (Php
       500,000.00) nor more than Two Million Pesos (Php 2,000,000.00), and a
       mandate to immediately retract all deceptive advertisements and greenwashing
       materials;
   (b)​Second Offense: A fine of not less than Two Million Pesos (Php 2,000,000.00)
       nor more than Ten Million Pesos (Php 10,000,000.00), suspension of operations
       for a period of not less than sixty (60) days, and a mandate to implement
       approved environmental-compliance mechanisms;
   (c)​Third Offense: Permanent and immediate closure of the business establishment.
       If the offender is a juridical person, the president, manager, directors, or officers
       directly related to the operations shall suffer the penalties provided under this Act
       and the Consumer Act of the Philippines, including imprisonment of not less than
       six (6) years nor more than twelve (12) years.

        Criminal liability under paragraph (c) of this Section shall attach only upon proof
of willful intent or gross negligence on the part of the responsible officer. Good-faith
reliance on a report, certification, or opinion issued by a BPS-accredited verifier shall be
a defense to criminal liability, without prejudice to the administrative liability of the
business enterprise.

        The fines prescribed under this Section shall be reviewed and adjusted for
inflation by the DTI, in coordination with the Philippine Statistics Authority, once every
three (3) years without need of further legislation, but not to exceed a cumulative
adjustment of one hundred percent (100%) of the amounts herein prescribed.

       Any liable entity shall be publicly listed in the Greenwashing Registry maintained
by the FTEB for a period of not less than five (5) years.

       Section 17. Implementing Rules and Regulations. Within ninety (90) days
from the effectivity of this Act, the Department of Trade and Industry (DTI), the
Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC), and the Climate Change Commission
(CCC), in consultation with the Fund Management Committee created under Section
9(a), consumer-advocacy groups, and climate scientists, shall jointly promulgate the
necessary IRR, which shall provide specific, rigorous guidelines on greenwashing
indicators, verification protocols, accreditation of third-party certifiers, and
claims-processing procedures for the CLEAR Fund.

        Section 18. Separability Clause. If any provision of this Act is declared
unconstitutional or invalid, the remaining provisions shall continue to be in full force and
effect.

       Section 19. Repealing Clause. All laws, decrees, executive orders, rules and
regulations, or parts thereof inconsistent with the provisions of this Act are hereby
repealed or modified accordingly.

       Section 20. Effectivity. This Act shall take effect fifteen (15) days after its
publication in the Official Gazette or in a newspaper of general circulation.
