                                   EXPLANATORY NOTE

        The Constitution imposes upon the State the duty to protect and advance the right
of the people to a balanced and healthful ecology in accord with the rhythm and harmony
of nature.1 This constitutional command is not abstract. In Oposa v. Factoran, the Supreme
Court recognized that the protection of the country’s forests is inseparable from the
principle of intergenerational responsibility, and that every generation bears a duty to
preserve the environment for those yet to come.2

       In the Philippines, deforestation remains a serious environmental, climate, and
governance concern. The Climate Change Commission has reported that from 2001 to
2022, the country lost approximately 1.42 million hectares of tree cover, while the
country’s total forest cover as of 2022 was estimated at 7.22 million hectares, or only
24.07% of the country’s land mass. 3 This is particularly alarming for a country recognized
as one of the world’s megadiverse countries, with exceptional levels of endemism and
biodiversity that depend heavily on healthy forest ecosystems. 4 Globally, deforestation is
also closely linked to commodity supply chains, with the Food and Agriculture

       The Philippines already has laws protecting forests, protected areas, wildlife
habitats, ancestral domains, and climate resilience. However, while these laws regulate
forest protection and environmental management, they do not yet establish a specific,
uniform, and enforceable rule that would prevent the government itself from purchasing
goods whose supply chains are linked to deforestation, illegal logging, unlawful forest
conversion, or degradation of protected and ecologically sensitive areas. This gap is
especially important because the Government is a major consumer of paper, furniture,
timber, wood-based construction materials, fixtures, and other products that may carry
forest-risk inputs.

       Existing policy already recognizes the role of public procurement in
environmental protection. Executive Order No. 301, series of 2004, directed government
agencies to establish Green Procurement Programs and include environmental criteria in
public tenders whenever practicable.6 More recently, Republic Act No. 12009, or the New
Government Procurement Act, institutionalized Sustainable Public Procurement and
Green Public Procurement, requiring procuring entities to integrate environmentally
relevant criteria into procurement design and specifications, and directing the
Government Procurement Policy Board (“GPPB”) to implement green procurement in
phases.7 This bill builds on that framework. It does not create a parallel procurement
regime. Rather, it supplies a clear deforestation-free standard for covered products within
the existing procurement system.

      This proposed measure institutionalizes a Deforestation-Free Government
Procurement Policy. It requires public agencies to procure covered forest-risk products
only when these are certified or verified as deforestation-free. It directs the Department
of Environment and Natural Resources to prescribe the environmental standard; the
GPPB and the Department of Budget and Management to integrate the requirement into
procurement rules, bidding documents, contract conditions, and PhilGEPS systems; and

      The bill is designed to be practical and implementable. It adopts a phased
approach, beginning with products where government demand and verification systems
are most feasible, such as paper, pulp, timber, plywood, lumber, furniture, fixtures, and
wood-based construction materials. It allows risk-based verification, recognizes
equivalent certifications and government documents, provides exceptions for
emergencies and temporary market unavailability, and requires simplified compliance
pathways for small producers, cooperatives, community-based forest management
organizations, MSMEs, and Indigenous Peoples’ enterprises.

       The measure also aligns the Philippines with emerging global best practices. The
European Union, for example, has adopted a deforestation-free products regulation
requiring covered commodities and products to be deforestation-free and produced in
accordance with the laws of the country of production before being placed on or exported
from the EU market.8 The Philippines does not need to copy foreign models wholesale.
Instead, this bill adapts the core principle to Philippine realities: fragmented land records,
smallholder production, island-based supply chains, community-based forest
management, public procurement capacity gaps, and the need to protect both the
environment and local livelihoods.

       By using government purchasing power, this measure creates market incentives
for responsible production and discourages supply chains linked to forest destruction. It
strengthens climate action, biodiversity protection, transparency in public spending, and
sustainable local enterprise. Public funds should not finance products that contribute to
the destruction of the very forests the State is constitutionally bound to protect.

       In view of the foregoing, the immediate passage of this bill is earnestly sought.

AN ACT INSTITUTIONALIZING A DEFORESTATION-FREE GOVERNMENT
    PROCUREMENT    POLICY,  ESTABLISHING   STANDARDS    AND
    VERIFICATION MECHANISMS THEREFOR, AND FOR OTHER PURPOSES

       Be it enacted by the Senate and House of Representatives of the Philippines in Congress
assembled:

     SECTION 1. Short Title. – This Act shall be known as the “Deforestation-Free
Government Procurement Act.”

       SEC. 2. Declaration of Policy. – It is the policy of the State to protect and advance
the right of the people to a balanced and healthful ecology, promote sustainable public
procurement, conserve forests and biodiversity, strengthen climate action, and ensure
that public funds are spent in a manner consistent with environmental protection,
responsible production, and value for money.

        Towards this end, the State shall use government procurement as a market-based
instrument to discourage supply chains linked to deforestation, forest degradation,
illegal logging, and unlawful land conversion, while supporting sustainable producers,
community-based forest management organizations, indigenous cultural communities,
small farmers, cooperatives, and micro, small, and medium enterprises that comply with
verifiable environmental standards.

      This Act shall be implemented in a manner consistent with Republic Act No.
12009, otherwise known as the “New Government Procurement Act,” existing
environmental and forestry laws, the rights of indigenous cultural communities and
indigenous peoples, and the country’s international obligations.

       SEC. 3. Scope and Application. – This Act shall apply to the procurement of
covered products by all branches, departments, bureaus, offices, agencies,
instrumentalities, government-owned or -controlled corporations, government financial
institutions, state universities and colleges, and local government units, regardless of
source of funds.

       For purposes of this Act, “covered products” shall refer to goods, materials,
supplies, and inputs identified under the implementing rules as having material risk of
contributing to deforestation, forest degradation, illegal logging, or unlawful forest
conversion. The initial coverage shall include timber, lumber, plywood, veneer, paper,
pulp, furniture, fixtures, wood-based construction materials, and such other forest-risk
commodities and derivative products as may be determined through the phased
implementation plan under this Act.

       No procuring entity shall procure a covered product unless the same is certified
or verified as deforestation-free in accordance with this Act and its implementing rules.

      SEC. 4. Definition of Terms. – For purposes of this Act:

          (a) “Deforestation-free product” refers to a covered product, including its
              material inputs, that is verified as not having been produced, harvested,
              processed, or sourced from illegal logging, unlawful forest conversion, or
              conversion of natural forests, mangrove forests, protected areas, critical
              habitats, ancestral domains without required consent, or other ecologically
              sensitive areas identified by law or by the Department of Environment and
              Natural Resources (“DENR”).

          (b) “Deforestation-free certification or verification” refers to the validation,
              certification, recognition, or clearance issued or accepted under this Act
              showing that a covered product complies with the applicable deforestation-
              free standard, traceability requirements, legality requirements, and risk
              controls.

          (c) “Forest-risk commodity” refers to a product, raw material, or derivative that
              has material supply-chain risk of being associated with deforestation, forest
              degradation, illegal logging, or unlawful land conversion, based on criteria
              issued under this Act.

          (d) “Traceability” refers to the ability to identify and verify the origin,
              movement, processing, and transfer of a covered product or its material

              inputs from source to point of procurement, using documents, permits,
              geospatial information, chain-of-custody records, or other verifiable means
              appropriate to the product and supplier.

       SEC. 5. Deforestation-Free Procurement Requirement. – All procuring entities
shall incorporate deforestation-free requirements in the Annual Procurement Plan,
technical specifications, terms of reference, bidding documents, contract conditions,
inspection and acceptance procedures, and post-qualification requirements for covered
products.

       A bidder, supplier, contractor, or service provider offering a covered product shall
submit proof of deforestation-free certification or verification, together with such
supporting documents as may be required under the implementing rules. Such
documents may include source declarations, chain-of-custody records, transport permits,
tree plantation or harvesting permits, import documents, geotagged source information,
third-party certification, supplier due diligence reports, or other equivalent proof.

       The requirement under this section shall apply equally to locally produced and
imported covered products and shall be based on the product’s source, legality,
traceability, and environmental risk, and not on the nationality or place of business of the
supplier.

      SEC. 6. Deforestation-Free Standard. – The DENR, in consultation with the
Government Procurement Policy Board (“GPPB”), Department of Budget and
Management (“DBM”), Department of Trade and Industry (“DTI”), Department of
Agriculture (“DA”), Department of Interior and Local Government (“DILG”),
Commission on Audit (“COA”), National Commission on Indigenous Peoples (“NCIP”),
National Mapping and Resource Information Authority (NAMRIA), and relevant
stakeholders, shall prescribe the deforestation-free standard for covered products.

       The standard shall require proof that the product was legally produced, harvested,
processed, transported, and traded; was not derived from illegal logging or unlawful
forest conversion; and, where applicable, was not produced on land converted from
natural forest after the cut-off date to be fixed in the implementing rules.

        The cut-off date shall be prospective and shall not be earlier than the effectivity of
this Act, unless the applicable commodity already has an existing legally recognized
certification, licensing, or traceability system that can support an earlier verifiable date
without impairing vested rights or unduly excluding small producers.



                                              3
      The standard shall be risk-based and proportionate. It shall be stricter for products
with high deforestation risk and simplified for low-risk products, small-value
procurement, community-based producers, small farmers, cooperatives, and MSMEs,
without dispensing with the core requirement that public funds shall not support
deforestation-linked supply chains.

       SEC. 7. Certification, Verification, and Recognition System. – There is hereby
established a Deforestation-Free Procurement Verification System, to be administered by
the DENR and integrated into the Sustainable Public Procurement and Green Public
Procurement framework of the GPPB.

       The DENR shall determine the environmental and forestry criteria for certification
or verification. The DTI shall integrate deforestation-free criteria into its green product
validation and certification program for covered products, consistent with Republic Act
No. 12009. The GPPB shall incorporate the required certification, verification, and
contract clauses into standard procurement forms, manuals, and bidding documents.

        The system may recognize credible third-party certification, chain-of-custody
certification, government permits, foreign official documents, group certification,
community-based verification, or equivalent proof, subject to standards issued under this
Act. Recognition of private or foreign certifications shall not prevent the DENR or the
procuring entity from requiring additional proof where there is reasonable ground to
doubt compliance.

       SEC. 8. Traceability Requirements. – Covered products shall be traceable to their
source at a level sufficient to determine compliance with the deforestation-free standard.

       For timber, wood-based products, paper, pulp, furniture, and construction
materials, traceability shall include, where applicable, proof of lawful harvest or
plantation source, transport authority, processing authority, chain-of-custody
documentation, and other DENR-required documents.

       For agricultural or other forest-risk commodities, traceability shall include source
declarations and location information sufficient to show that the product was not
produced through unlawful forest conversion or encroachment into protected, forest,
mangrove, ancestral, or other ecologically sensitive areas.

      The implementing rules shall adopt practical traceability tiers, taking into account
the realities of smallholder production, cooperatives, community-based forest




                                            4
management, fragmented land records, and island-based supply chains in the
Philippines.

       SEC. 9. Deforestation-Free Procurement Registry. – The GPPB, through the
Philippine Government Electronic Procurement System, in coordination with the DENR,
DBM, DTI, and PS-DBM, shall establish and maintain a Deforestation-Free Procurement
Registry containing covered products, recognized certifications or verification
documents, accredited or recognized verification bodies, suppliers with verified covered
products, and suppliers suspended or blacklisted for violations under this Act.

       The Registry shall be interoperable, to the extent practicable, with existing
government databases on procurement, green products, forestry permits, business
registration, customs, geospatial information, and environmental compliance.

       SEC. 10. Phased Implementation. – Within one year from the effectivity of this Act,
the GPPB and DENR shall issue the initial list of covered products, standard bidding
clauses, verification templates, and guidance for procuring entities.

      Mandatory implementation shall begin with paper, pulp, timber, lumber,
plywood, furniture, fixtures, and wood-based construction materials procured by
national government agencies, GOCCs, GFIs, SUCs, and PS-DBM. LGUs and other
procuring entities shall be covered under a phased schedule, taking into account
procurement capacity, market availability, supplier readiness, and the need to avoid
disruption of essential public services.

       Additional forest-risk commodities and derivative products shall be included in
phases upon joint determination by the DENR, GPPB, DTI, and DA that the relevant
verification standards, market supply, and compliance mechanisms are reasonably
available.

       SEC. 11. Support for Local Producers, Cooperatives, and MSMEs. – The DENR,
DTI, DA, Cooperative Development Authority (CDA), DILG, and GPPB shall provide
technical assistance, simplified compliance pathways, model documents, training, and
group verification mechanisms for community-based forest management organizations,
indigenous peoples’ enterprises, small farmers, cooperatives, social enterprises, startups,
and MSMEs.

       No implementing rule shall impose a certification process that is unnecessarily
costly, exclusionary, or impracticable for local producers, provided that the minimum
deforestation-free requirement is maintained.

       Government agencies shall, where consistent with law, design procurement
specifications in a manner that encourages compliant local production and avoids
unnecessary reliance on imported certified products when equivalent local deforestation-
free products are available.

       SEC. 12. Exceptions. – A procuring entity may procure a covered product without
full deforestation-free certification only in cases of emergency procurement, disaster
response, urgent public health or safety needs, or temporary lack of verified market
supply, subject to written justification by the Head of the Procuring Entity.

        The justification shall state the necessity of the procurement, the efforts
undertaken to source compliant alternatives, the safeguards adopted to minimize
deforestation risk, and the transition measure to ensure future compliance. The
justification shall be submitted to the GPPB and DENR and posted in the procurement
record.

      Exceptions shall be narrowly construed and shall not be used to split contracts,
evade certification, favor a supplier, or defeat the policy of this Act.

       SEC. 13. Contract Conditions. – Every contract involving a covered product shall
contain a deforestation-free warranty stating that the product and its material inputs
comply with this Act and its implementing rules.

       The contract shall authorize the procuring entity, DENR, GPPB, COA, or their
authorized representatives to verify compliance, require additional documents, conduct
risk-based audits, inspect records, and recommend suspension, termination, blacklisting,
or other appropriate action where warranted.

       A material false statement, fraudulent certification, concealment of source,
substitution of non-compliant products, or refusal to submit to verification shall
constitute a substantial breach of contract.

        SEC. 14. Monitoring and Reporting. – All procuring entities shall submit annual
compliance reports to the GPPB and DENR, indicating their covered procurements,
certifications or verifications submitted, exceptions invoked, contracts terminated or
suspended, and suppliers reported for violations.

      The GPPB and DENR shall submit to Congress an annual report on the
implementation of this Act, including compliance rates, implementation gaps, market
readiness, supplier participation, enforcement actions, and recommendations for
expanding or adjusting the list of covered products.

       The COA may include compliance with this Act in its audit of procurement
transactions involving covered products.

       SEC. 15. Prohibited Acts. – The following acts are prohibited:

          (a) Any bidder, supplier, contractor, or service provider shall be prohibited
              from submitting false, fraudulent, misleading, expired, revoked, or
              materially incomplete deforestation-free documents; concealing the source
              of a covered product; substituting a compliant product with a non-
              compliant product; or refusing lawful verification under this Act.

          (b) Any public officer or employee shall be prohibited from knowingly
              accepting non-compliant covered products, waiving the requirements of
              this Act without legal basis, approving an unjustified exception, splitting
              contracts to avoid coverage, or unduly favoring a supplier in the
              implementation of this Act.

       SEC. 16. Penalties and Sanctions. – A bidder, supplier, contractor, or service
provider that violates this Act shall be subject to disqualification, contract termination,
forfeiture of performance security, recovery of payments, liquidated damages,
suspension, blacklisting, and other sanctions under Republic Act No. 12009 and its
implementing rules, without prejudice to civil, criminal, administrative, environmental,
customs, forestry, anti-graft, falsification, or perjury liability under existing laws.

        A public officer or employee who knowingly violates this Act shall be
administratively liable in accordance with civil service laws, Republic Act No. 12009,
Republic Act No. 3019, and other applicable laws, without prejudice to criminal or civil
liability when warranted.

       Where the violation involves illegal logging, unlawful forest conversion, protected
areas, ancestral domains, customs fraud, or environmental offenses, the matter shall be
referred to the appropriate enforcement agency for investigation and prosecution.

       SEC. 17. Implementing Rules and Regulations. – Within one hundred eighty days
from the effectivity of this Act, the DENR, GPPB, DBM, DTI, DA, DILG, COA, NCIP, and
other relevant agencies shall jointly issue the implementing rules and regulations.

      The implementing rules shall include the phased list of covered products,
deforestation-free standard, verification process, registry design, model bidding clauses,
contract conditions, traceability tiers, exception procedures, audit and reporting

        SEC. 18. Appropriations. – The amount necessary for the initial implementation

DA, DILG, GPPB, and other implementing agencies. Thereafter, such sums as may be
necessary shall be included in the annual General Appropriations Act.

       SEC. 19. Separability Clause. – If any provision of this Act is declared
unconstitutional or invalid, the other provisions not affected thereby shall remain in full
force and effect.

       SEC. 20. Repealing Clause. – All laws, decrees, executive orders, rules and
regulations, issuances, or parts thereof inconsistent with this Act are hereby repealed,
amended, or modified accordingly.

       SEC. 21. Effectivity. – This Act shall take effect fifteen days after its publication in
the Official Gazette or in a newspaper of general circulation.
