14                                        EXPLANATORY NOTE
15
16   The 1987 Philippine Constitution explicitly commits the State to recognize, promote, and protect
17   the rights of Indigenous Cultural Communities/Indigenous Peoples (ICCs/IPs) within the
18   framework of national unity and development. This constitutional guarantee was given statutory
19   form through Republic Act No. 8371, or the Indigenous Peoples' Rights Act of 1997 (IPRA),
20   particularly Section 15, which recognizes the right of ICCs/IPs to use their customary laws,
21   traditional justice systems, and conflict resolution mechanisms.
22
23   However, Section 15 of RA 8371 sets a clear and non-negotiable legal boundary: customary
24   practices and dispute resolution mechanisms must always be compatible with the national legal
25   system and internationally recognized human rights.
26
27   While customary law plays a vital role in community cohesion and dispute settlement, recent tragic
28   events highlight how hazardous trials by ordeal and dangerous traditional rituals place human lives
29   at grave risk.
30
31   A tragic illustration of this legislative gap occurred in Alabel, Sarangani Province. Two
32   indigenous farmers belonging to the Tagakaulo community—holding a long-standing land dispute
33   that failed to reach a settlement at the barangay level—agreed to undergo a traditional trial by
34   ordeal known as "Sulif" to resolve land ownership.
35
36   Under the Sulif ritual, disputing parties remain submerged underwater in a river, with the individual
37   remaining under longer declared the innocent or rightful party. Conducted at the Glamang River in
38   Alabel, the ritual resulted in catastrophic loss of life: both farmers experienced severe breathing
39   distress after remaining underwater for approximately 12 minutes. Both men subsequently
40   drowned—one declared dead on arrival at the Sarangani Provincial Hospital, and the other dying
41   shortly thereafter.
42
43   This heartbreaking incident in Sarangani underscores the urgent necessity of clear legislation:
44
45
46       1.   Human Rights and Bodily Integrity Precede Customary Ordeals: No customary law,
47            tribal agreement, voluntary consent, or community ritual can override the fundamental
48            constitutional rights to life, safety, and bodily integrity.
49
50       2.   Accountability for Facilitators and Authorities: Elders, leaders, spiritual guides,
51            organizers, or public officers who facilitate, direct, encourage, or tolerate rituals involving
52            physical danger, hazardous ordeals, or harm shall be held criminally and administratively
53            liable if such acts result in injury or death.
54
55       3.   Harmonization with RA 8371: It reinforces that while customary dispute resolution
56            remains protected under Section 15 of IPRA, any practice causing physical injury or loss
57            of life falls outside the legal protections of customary law and is subject to full prosecution
58            under penal law.
59
60   In view of the foregoing, the immediate passage of this bill is earnestly sought.

134     AN ACT PROHIBITING HAZARDOUS TRADITIONAL TRIALS BY ORDEAL AND
135        DANGEROUS RITUALS, PRESCRIBING PENALTIES FOR FACILITATORS,
136      ORGANIZERS, AND PUBLIC OFFICIALS, AND AMENDING FOR THE PURPOSE
137     RELEVANT PROVISIONS OF REPUBLIC ACT NO. 8371, OTHERWISE KNOWN AS
138               THE "INDIGENOUS PEOPLES' RIGHTS ACT OF 1997"
139
140         Be it enacted by the Senate and House of Representatives of the Philippines in Congress
141   assembled:
142
143   SECTION 1. Short Title.
144
145           This Act shall be known as the "Protection Against Hazardous Customary Ordeals
146   Act."
147
148   SECTION 2. Declaration of Policy.
149
150           It is the policy of the State to protect and promote the right to life, physical integrity, and
151   safety of all persons. While the State recognizes and respects the traditional justice systems and
152   customary laws of Indigenous Cultural Communities/Indigenous Peoples (ICCs/IPs) pursuant to
153   Section 15 of Republic Act No. 8371, such practices must at all times adhere to national legal
154   standards and human rights. No customary practice, agreement, or voluntary consent shall justify
155   acts that subject human life or physical well-being to grave peril.
156
157   SECTION 3. Prohibited Acts.
158
159           It shall be unlawful for any person or group to organize, conduct, order, facilitate, or require
160   participation in any trial by ordeal, hazardous ritual, or customary practice that poses a direct threat
161   to physical safety, bodily integrity, or human life, including but not limited to underwater
162   endurance tests, exposure to fire or hazardous substances, or physical tortures used as dispute
163   resolution mechanisms.
164
165   SECTION 4. Criminal Liability of Facilitators, Organizers, and Community Leaders.
166
167          Any elder, leader, spiritual guide, organizer, or individual who facilitates, directs,
168   encourages, or conducts any traditional trial by ordeal, hazardous ritual, or practice involving
169   physical danger or harm shall be held criminally liable as follows:
170
171
172      a. Resulting in Death or Serious Physical Injuries: The penalty of arresto mayor in its
173         maximum period to prisión correccional in its medium period.
174
175      b. Resulting in Less Serious Physical Injuries: The penalty of arresto mayor in its minimum
176         and medium periods.
177
178      c. Resulting in Slight Physical Injuries: The penalty of arresto menor in its maximum
179         period.
180
181   SECTION 5. Liability of Government Officials and Employees.
182
183           Any public officer, government official, or employee—whether elected, appointed, or
184   acting in an ex-officio capacity—who permits, authorizes, tolerates, participates in, or fails to
185   prevent the conduct of any hazardous ordeal, trial, or ritual within their jurisdiction or scope of
186   authority, despite prior knowledge or opportunity to intervene, shall face the following liabilities:
187
188
189      a. Criminal Liability: They shall be held liable as co-principals or accomplices under Section
190         4 and shall suffer the same criminal penalties prescribed therein.
191
192      b. Accessory Penalty: Upon conviction, they shall suffer the penalty of Perpetual Special
193         Disqualification from holding any public office, employment, or appointive position in the
194         government or any of its subdivisions, agencies, or instrumentalities, including government-
195         owned or controlled corporations (GOCCs).
196
197   SECTION 6. Civil Liability.
198
199           Conviction under this Act shall not bar the victim or the victim's heirs from recovering full
200   civil damages, including actual medical expenses, funeral expenses, loss of earning capacity, moral
201   damages, and exemplary damages.
202
203   SECTION 7. Non-Defense of Custom or Tradition.
204
205          Customary law, cultural tradition, community consent, or the voluntary consent of the
206   victim or the victim's legal guardian shall not constitute a defense or a mitigating circumstance in
207   any criminal prosecution under this Act.
208
209   SECTION 8. Inter-Agency Coordination and Cultural Sensitivity.
210
211           The National Commission on Indigenous Peoples (NCIP), in coordination with the
212   Department of Justice (DOJ), the Department of the Interior and Local Government (DILG), and
213   local government units (LGUs), shall establish educational and legal awareness programs to clarify
214   the constitutional boundaries of customary practices, ensuring that community traditions are
215   exercised without violating bodily integrity and human rights.
216
217   SECTION 9. Separability Clause.
218
219           If any provision or part of this Act is declared invalid or unconstitutional, the remaining
220   parts or provisions not affected thereby shall remain in full force and effect.
221
222   SECTION 10. Repealing Clause.
223
224           All laws, executive orders, rules, and regulations inconsistent with the provisions of this Act
225   are hereby repealed, amended, or modified accordingly.
226
227   SECTION 11. Effectivity.
228
229         This Act shall take effect fifteen (15) days after its publication in the Official Gazette or in a
230   newspaper of general circulation.
