24                                      EXPLANATORY NOTE
25
26          Up to 95% of the country's petroleum requirements are imported, rendering the
27   Philippines exceedingly vulnerable to the dictates of big transnational oil corporations.
28   These corporations control the importation, refining, and retailing of crude and
29   petroleum products in the world market, as well as the exploration, exploitation,
30   refining, retailing, and even re-exportation of our country's indigenous petroleum and
31   petroleum-based products. The worst implication of this system is that our severe
32   dependence on such corporations deprives us of the opportunity to become self-
33   sufficient.
34
35           These corporations, including Exxon-Mobil, Royal Dutch Shell, Chevron-
36   Texaco (its marketing arm is Caltex), British Petroleum (BP), and CFP-Total, have
37   almost complete control over the oil prices they collusively set worldwide. Even though
38   the largest oil-producing countries (Saudi Arabia, Iran, Venezuela, Mexico, and China)
39   control 90% of the world's oil reserves, these five transnational oil companies dictate
40   world prices because they control 60% of oil production and hold all levels of the market
41   through their vast global network of exploration and exploitation sites, partnerships with
42   nationalized oil companies, refineries, pipelines, tankers, and stations.
43
44           For the longest time, the Big Three—the local subsidiaries of Shell and Caltex
45   plus Petron—claim that they do not overprice. They justify their pricing by citing Dubai
46   oil prices exchanged over the spot market and Peso-Dollar exchange rate fluctuations.
47   Moreover, they deny the existence of a cartel, alleging that they merely follow the
48   movements of world oil prices. They maintain these claims even as they are minimally
49   affected by prices in world markets and even as they are able to trade in the same
50   currency between subsidiaries.
51
52          Both public perception and research reveal what is called “price asymmetry”:
53   whenever global prices rise, oil companies, especially the Big Three, are quick to hike
54   pump prices more than proportionately. However, when global prices fall, they decrease
55   prices slowly and less than proportionately. This pattern suggests collusion between oil
56   companies taking advantage of global price increases to boost profits—acts of price
57   manipulation that the government cannot check or prevent because it has relinquished
58   control over oil price regulation.
59
60          For example, on June 18, 2013, citing escalating world oil prices, oil firms
61   operating in the Philippines hiked prices of gasoline (₱1.05), diesel (₱1.45) and
62   kerosene (₱1.30) per liter, inclusive of the 12% value-added tax. Even when
63   international oil prices slumped in 2015, the corresponding downward adjustment of
64   local oil prices was hardly commensurate with the price movement in the international
65   oil market.
66
67         More recently, on February 22 and March 1, 2022, oil companies immediately
68   announced local oil price hikes citing increases in global oil prices due to “prospects of
69   a Russian invasion of Ukraine” and “fear of supply concerns across Europe and the
70   world”1—despite the fact that the Philippines does not buy oil directly from Russia. On
71   March 7, 2022, there was a big-time local oil price hike due to the “discussions to ban
72   Russian oil in the United States and Europe.”2 The actual ban was only announced by
73   the United States the following day.
74
75          Most tellingly, in contrast to the lowering of international oil prices from
76   September 30 to October 17, 2025, Filipino consumers continued to suffer from
77   consecutive local oil price hikes. From October 7 to November 10, 2025, the domestic
78   price of gasoline increased by ₱4.00 per liter, while diesel and kerosene prices increased
79   by P5.80 per liter and ₱3.60 per liter, respectively. These increases brought NCR pump
80   prices3 of gasoline to ₱50.00-71.82 per liter, diesel to ₱49.00-66.90 per liter, and
81   kerosene at ₱76.50-93.89 per liter. The following week, the Department of Energy
82   announced another gasoline and diesel price hike amounting to ₱1.20.
83

85   Dubai crude at US$55.4 per barrel, with exploration costs at US$3-4 per barrel,
86   production costs at US$7-8 per barrel, and royalties at US$8 per barrel, show that a
87   staggering US$35-37 per barrel (63-67%) is taken home as super profits by the

 89   expected to be raked-in from hidden transfer-pricing between company subsidiaries.
 90
 91          Because these subsidiaries under the same corporations are both the buyers
 92   (refiners in the downstream petroleum industry) and the sellers (producers in the
 93   upstream petroleum industry), they can fix and pad prices through hidden transfer
 94   pricing every step of the way, including the mere sale of petroleum and petroleum
 95   products from refiners to dealers. Estimates show that 67% of world trade in oil is done
 96   through term contracts, or transactions between subsidiaries of the same companies,
 97   bypassing exchanges over world markets.
 98
 99          Our dependence on imported petroleum and petroleum products has continually
100   depleted our foreign currency reserves, which has caused the further devaluation of the
101   Peso and has made importation more and more expensive. Even without factoring in
102   increases in the world demand for oil and the artificial effects of speculation, world oil
103   prices are unreasonably and unjustifiably high because they are under the control of
104   monopolistic transnational oil corporations.
105
106          Republic Act No. 8479, or the “Downstream Oil Industry Deregulation Act of
107   1998,” professed to “ensure a truly competitive market under a regime of fair prices”
108   through the liberalization and the deregulation of the local downstream oil industry. But
109   to begin with, R.A. 8479 has the wrong premise that the world pricing of petroleum and
110   petroleum products is fair and justifiable.
111
112           Hence, this deregulation law merely advocates fair pricing through the
113   “mediation” between big oil companies (refiners and importers) and relatively small
114   dealers, haulers, and LPG distributors, the opening of the market to new industry
115   participants nonetheless dependent on big transnational oil corporations, and the
116   relatively insignificant financial and operational support for small-scale gasoline station
117   retailers.
118
119           From the very beginning, R.A. 8479 was bound to fail to implement fair prices
120   since it ignored monopoly-pricing practices and cartel behavior by the giant
121   transnational oil corporations. Worse, deregulation has given transnational oil
122   corporations even more room to manipulate prices through automatic oil price hikes,
123   less transparency in pricing, profits, and operations, and virtually no accountability to
124   the consuming public.
125
126           Petroleum is a sensitive commodity, since its price directly affects the cost of
127   almost all other commodities and services, including essentials such as food, housing,
128   social services, as well as transportation. Deregulation has allowed oil price increases
129   to go unchecked. Deregulation has given transnational oil corporations even more
130   leeway to influence the country’s cost of living, livelihoods, business and commerce,
131   employment, and the National Budget. The detrimental effects of an unfair world set-
132   up dominated by giant, monopolistic, transnational oil corporations are magnified by
133   liberalization and deregulation.
134
135          There is a pressing need to interdict the hidden and unchecked transfer pricing
136   between oil company subsidiaries, including probable price padding in the sale of
137   petroleum and petroleum products between refiners and local subsidiaries, to protect
138   the majority of Filipinos from current runaway increases in oil prices.
139
140          But such state intervention can only be effective and truly beneficial if it is part
141   of a program to institutionalize national oil industrialization, so that local oil prices can
142   be brought down from the unreasonable and unjustifiable levels set by giant
143   transnational oil corporations, and prevented from further monopoly pricing and
144   manipulation.
145
146           This Bill therefore requires the institution of a centralized procurement system
147   of all imported crude oil and refined petroleum products, which includes the creation of
148   buffer supplies to cushion consumers against drastic increases in petroleum prices.
149
150         This measure was first and originally filed during the 16th Congress by the
151   Makabayan bloc led by Bayan Muna Reps. Neri Colmenares and Carlos Zarate as HB
152   176. The Bill was also refiled during the 17th Congress as HB 3678.
153
154          Immediate passage of this measure is earnestly sought.

183   SECTION 1. Short Title. – This Act shall be known as the “Centralized Petroleum
184   Procurement Act.”
185
186   SEC. 2. Declaration of Policy. –
187
188   I. Social Justice
189
190   Article II, Section X of the 1987 Constitution states: “The State shall promote social
191   justice in all phases of national development.”
192
193   Article II, Section XIX of the 1987 Constitution states: “The State shall develop a self-
194   reliant and independent national economy effectively controlled by Filipinos.”
195
196   II. National Economy and Patrimony
197
198   Article XII, Section I of the 1987 Constitution states: “The goals of the national
199   economy are a more equitable distribution of opportunities, income, and wealth; a
200   sustained increase in the amount of goods and services produced by the nation for the
201   benefit of the people; and an expanding productivity as the key to raising the quality of
202   life for all, especially the under-privileged.”
203
204   Article XII, Section XIII of the 1987 Constitution states: “The State shall pursue a trade
205   policy that serves the general welfare and utilizes all forms and arrangements of
206   exchange on the basis of equality and reciprocity.”
207
208   Article XII, Section XIV of the 1987 Constitution states: “The State shall regulate or
209   prohibit monopolies when the public interest so requires. No combinations in restraint
210   of trade or unfair competition shall be allowed.”
211
212   Article XII, Section XVIII of the 1987 Constitution states: “The State may, in the
213   interest of national welfare or defense, establish and operate vital industries and, upon
214   payment of just compensation, transfer to public ownership utilities and other private
215   enterprises to be operated by the Government.”
216
217   Consistent with the foregoing, petroleum and petroleum products, being vital to national
218   security and their supply at reasonable prices being essential to the general welfare, it
219   is hereby declared to be the policy of the State that the act and business of importing,
220   exporting, re-exporting, distributing, and selling at wholesale of crude oil, gasoline,
221   kerosene, gas, and other refined petroleum products, as well as operations and activities
222   of natural and juridical persons, firms, and entities engaged in the petroleum industry
223   shall be carried out in a manner consistent with the public interest, so as to attain the
224   following objectives and purposes:
225
226      a. To assure that the indigenous petroleum and petroleum-based resources shall be
227         primarily for the benefit of the general welfare;
228
229      b. To assure that the country shall have a proper, adequate, and continuous supply
230         of crude oil and refined petroleum products under the most economic and
231         competitive terms possible, considering all available sources of supply;
232
233      c. To assure the public of reasonable prices for petroleum products and to prevent
234         monopoly, combinations in restraint of trade, unfair competition, and economic
235         domination; and
236
237      d. To minimize the cost and the outflow of foreign exchange involved in the
238         operations of the petroleum industry.

240   SEC. 3. Coverage. – This Act shall apply to all natural and juridical persons, firms, and
241   entities engaged in any and all activities of the petroleum industry, as well as persons
242   or companies directly importing or exporting crude or refined petroleum products for
243   their own use or for the use of other natural and juridical persons, firms, and entities
244   engaged in any and all activities of the petroleum industry.
245
246   These activities include the act and business of importing, exporting, re-exporting,
247   storing, distributing, and selling both at wholesale and retail of crude oil, gasoline,
248   kerosene, gas, and other refined petroleum products.

250   SEC. 4. Definition of Terms. –
251
252      a. “Exchange” shall mean the Philippine National Oil Company-National
253         Petroleum Exchange Corporation (PNOC-NPEC).
254
255      b. “Logistics Company” shall mean the PSTSC or the PNOC (Philippine National
256         Oil Company) Shipping, Transport, and Storage Corporation.
257
258      c. “Petroleum” shall refer to the naturally occurring mixture of compounds of
259         hydrogen and carbon with a small proportion of impurities and shall include any
260         mineral oil, petroleum gas, hydrogen gas, bitumen, asphalt, mineral wax, and all
261         other similar or naturally-associated substances, with the exception of coal, peat,
262         bituminous shale and/or other stratified mineral fuel deposits;
263
264      d. “Crude oil” or “oil” shall be used interchangeably with “petroleum”.
265
266      e. “Petroleum products” shall only refer to liquefied petroleum gas (LPG),
267         naphtha, gasolines, kerosenes, automatic/industrial diesel oils, and
268         industrial/residual fuel oils.

270      f. “Plan” shall refer to the Centralized Petroleum Procurement Plan.

272   SEC. 5. Creation of the National Petroleum Exchange Corporation. – There is
273   hereby created the National Petroleum Exchange Corporation (NPEC), hereafter
274   referred to as the Exchange, with the primary objective to enable the country to get the
275   best prices, and adequate and stable supplies for its requirements of crude oil and
276   petroleum products. The National Petroleum Exchange Corporation shall be a
277   subsidiary company of the Philippine National Oil Company (PNOC), making
278   Philippine National Oil Company-National Petroleum Exchange Corporation (PNOC-
279   NPEC) the company’s formal corporate name.
280
281   SEC. 6. Board of Directors of the Exchange. – The Board shall be composed of a
282   Chairperson and six (6) Directors to be appointed by the Board of Directors of the
283   Exchange’s mother corporation, the Philippine National Oil Company (PNOC). The
284   Chairperson and the Board Directors shall be natural-born citizens and residents of the
285   Philippines; persons of good moral character; at least thirty-five (35) years of age; of
286   recognized competence in any of the following fields: energy, law, economics, finance,
287   commerce and industry, and management; or the transportation and consumer sectors;
288   and with at least five (5) years of actual and distinguished experience in their respective
289   fields of expertise or sectors: Provided, That of the six (6) Directors of the Board, at
290   least one (1) shall have had (10) years experience in the fields of management,
291   economics, or commerce and industry; one (1) shall be a certified public accountant
292   with at least ten (10) years experience in active practice; and one (1) shall be a sector
293   leader who has been active for at least ten (10) years in the transportation, consumer, or
294   industrial sector.
295
296   The term of office of the Chairperson and the Board Directors shall be four (4) years:
297   Provided, That for the first appointees, the Chairperson shall hold office for four (4)
298   years, one Director shall hold office for four (4) years, another two for three (3) years,
299   and the remaining two Directors shall hold office for two (2) years: Provided further,
300   That for subsequent appointees, the successor of each Director shall have the same
301   tenure as his predecessor: Provided further, That appointment to any vacancy shall only
302   be for the unexpired term of the predecessor: Provided finally, That in no case shall any
303   member serve for more than four (4) years in the Board.
304
305   Within three (3) months from the creation of the Exchange, the Chairperson shall submit
306   for the approval by the PNOC Board of Directors the new organization structure and
307   plantilla positions necessary to carry out the powers and functions of the Exchange.

309   No person who has worked within four (4) years immediately prior to his appointment
310   or is working in any firm engaged in the petroleum industry or any other entity whose
311   main business is directly or indirectly related to any such firm shall be appointed to the

313   any interest whatsoever, except for the Chairperson of the NPSTSC, either as investor,

316   processing, refining, storing, marketing, distributing, and selling both at wholesale and

319
320   The presence of at least four (4) members of the Board shall constitute a quorum and

322   for the adoption of any rules, rulings, orders, resolutions, decisions, or other acts of the
323   Board in the exercise of its functions.
324
325   Former Chairpersons and members of the Board are prohibited from working directly
326   or indirectly in any firm engaged in the petroleum industry or any other entity whose
327   main business is directly or indirectly related to any such firm within one (1) year from
328   the time they have ceased to be officially connected with the Exchange.

330   The five (5) appointed Board Directors shall devote their full time to the Board and shall
331   not accept any other employment while sitting in the Board.

333   SEC. 7. Jurisdiction, Powers, Functions, and Duties of the Exchange. – The
334   Exchange shall centralize and strictly regulate the movement of petroleum and
335   petroleum products at the stages of importation, exportation, re-exportation,
336   distribution, storage, and selling both at wholesale and retail of crude oil, gasoline,
337   kerosene, gas, and other refined petroleum products.
338
339   For this purpose, the general jurisdiction, powers, functions, and duties of the Exchange
340   shall include the following:
341
342      a. Instituting a centralized procurement system, which shall take over all
343         importation of crude oil and petroleum products and shall be the sole supplier of
344         crude and petroleum for the local markets—for all local oil subsidiaries,
345         wholesalers, retailers, as well as to all natural and juridical persons, firms, and
346         entities engaged in the act and business of importing, marketing, distributing,
347         and selling both at wholesale and retail crude oil and petroleum products for their
348         own use or for the use of other natural and juridical persons, firms, and entities
349         engaged in any and all activities of the petroleum industry;
350
351      b. Determining and supplying the country’s total annual requirement for crude oil,
352         gasoline, diesel, kerosene, fuel oil, liquefied petroleum gas, and other oil
353         products;
354
355      c. Determining and sourcing the most economic, stable, and adequate foreign
356         sources of crude oil and petroleum products based on the following criteria:
357
358            i.   Ownership of petroleum suppliers: shares of small private investors,
359                 national governments, and transnational oil corporations and the
360
361           ii.   Inclusion of mutually advantageous commodity swaps and/or foreign
362                 exchange arrangements in bilateral petroleum trade agreements; and
363
364      d. Ensuring that locally extracted crude oil, locally refined petroleum, and locally
365         processed petroleum products primarily and singularly serve the country’s local
366         petroleum requirements.

373   SEC. 9. Centralized Petroleum Procurement Plan. – Complete procurement of 100%
374   of the national petroleum needs, as determined by the NPEC, shall be effected over a
375   period of not less than four (4) years and not more than five (5) years, for which each
376   year shall cover 20% to 25% more than the previous year of the national petroleum
377   needs.
378
379   The pace and the time frame of the Centralized Petroleum Procurement Plan shall be
380   based on the following criteria:
381
382      a. Total national demand forecasts on crude oil and petroleum products, on a
383         weekly, monthly, quarterly, and yearly basis, to be organized and undertaken by
384         the Exchange, which shall be based on the requirements of the local petroleum
385         marketing companies, independent demand projections, and demand forecasts
386         by the Exchange;
387
388      b. Actual total national requirements on crude oil and petroleum products, for
389         previous months, quarters, and years, to be organized and undertaken by the
390         Exchange, which shall be based on actual sales of local petroleum marketing
391         companies and separate investigations by the Exchange;
392
393      c. Actual total importations of crude oil and petroleum products, for previous
394         months, quarters, and years, to be organized and undertaken by the Exchange,
395         which shall be based on the records of the Bureau of Customs, actual sales of
396         local petroleum marketing companies, and separate investigations by the
397         Exchange;
398
399      d. Effective control of the Exchange over shipping, transport, and storage facilities
400         of crude oil and petroleum products, as detailed under Section 12 of this Act;
401
402      e. Levels of the sources of funding, as delineated under Section 17 of this Act;
403
404      f. Efficacy of Commodity Swaps and other bilateral trade negotiations between
405         PNOC-NPEC and state-owned oil companies from oil-producing countries as
406         delineated under Sections 10-11 of this Act; and
407
408      g. Forecasts, feasibility studies, and planning sessions by the Board of the
409         Exchange.
410
411   For the first year upon the effectivity of this Act, the Exchange shall utilize the shipping,
412   transport, and storage facilities of Petron Corporation, as renationalized under Section
413   13 and 14 of this Act, and of the PNOC Shipping and Transport Corporation (PSTC),
414   and shall progressively augment such facilities and capabilities, for the succeeding years
415   of the Centralized Petroleum Procurement Plan, as delineated under the provisions of
416   Section 12 of this Act.
417
418   The ability of the Exchange to procure a surplus of imported petroleum over the actual
419   total national requirements, as delineated under Section 15 of this Act as the Buffer
420   Supply, marks the last period of the Centralized Petroleum Procurement Plan.
421
422   At the conclusion of the Plan, the National Petroleum Exchange Corporation shall be
423   the sole source of the country’s total requirement for petroleum and refined petroleum
424   products. All petroleum marketing companies, petroleum dealers and traders, and all
425   other importers of petroleum and refined petroleum products shall thereby be prohibited
426   to procure by themselves or through other natural and juridical persons, firms, and
427   entities, through importation or any such transportation, shipment, and freight activity,
428   petroleum and refined petroleum products. They shall likewise be prohibited from
429   exporting or re-exporting crude oil and petroleum products within the period of the plan,
430   from which time they will be required to apply for exporting and re-exporting licenses
431   from the Exchange.
432
433   Violations of these provisions shall be dealt with by the penalties under Section 19 of
434   this Act.

436   SEC. 10. Bilateral Negotiations between PNOC-NPEC and state-owned oil
437   companies from oil-producing countries. – When bidding out supply contracts to
438   prospective exporters of crude oil and refined petroleum products, the Exchange shall
439   prioritize, and if possible, restrict supply contracts to 100% state-owned oil companies
440   from oil-producing countries, which have the least influence from transnational oil
441   corporations.
442
443   The Exchange shall avoid dealing with state-owned oil companies with strategic
444   linkages with transnational oil corporations and shall, by policy, include in such
445   contracts trade agreements involving non-petroleum products. These trade agreements
446   include, but are not limited to, commodity swaps as delineated under Section 11 of this
447   Act.
448
449   The Exchange shall minimize, as much as possible and within the limits of annual
450   appropriations under Section 17 of this Act, cash outlays for performing its procurement
451   activities.
452
453   SEC. 11. Commodity Swaps and other bilateral trade and currency agreements. –
454   When bidding out supply contracts to prospective importers of crude oil and refined
455   petroleum products, the Exchange shall, as a matter of policy and especially in trade
456   with state-owned oil companies from oil-producing countries, pursue commodity swaps
457   where the Exchange shall push for special terms in supply contracts of crude oil and
458   refined petroleum products in exchange for equally favorable concessions to exporting
459   countries.
460
461   Such swaps shall include the trade of products without the exchange of foreign
462   currencies to minimize the impact of fluctuations in the U.S. Dollar-Philippine Peso
463   exchange rate on the price of oil. An alternative practice shall include the Exchange
464   purchasing petroleum imports using the Philippine Peso, which oil-exporting countries
465   can subsequently use to pay for their imports from the Philippines. These trade practices
466   shall be subject to actual negotiations.
467
468   The Exchange, in consultation with the Secretary of the Department of Trade and
469   Industry (DTI), is tasked to spearhead activities relating to commodity swaps and other
470   bilateral trade agreements. It shall conduct studies on which petroleum exporting
471   countries would such arrangements be most feasible before commencing with the
472   bidding and negotiations.
473
474   The Exchange, in consultation with the DTI Secretary, shall likewise explore previously
475   untapped trade partners for crude oil and refined petroleum products for which
476   commodity swaps and other bilateral trade agreements may be feasible.
477
478   SEC. 12. Shipping, Transport and Storage. – For the Exchange to centralize and
479   strictly regulate the movement of petroleum and petroleum products at shipping,
480   transport, and storage, as well as enable it to get the best shipping, transport, and storage
481   prices for its imported crude oil and petroleum products, Petron Corporation, as
482   renationalized under Sections 13 to 14 of this Act, with the PNOC Shipping and
483   Transport Corporation (PSTC), another subsidiary company of the Philippine National
484   Oil Company, are hereby tasked to fulfill, in the first year upon the effectivity of this
485   Act, the shipping and transportation needs of the Exchange. Any and all deficiencies
486   in the ability of Petron and the PSTC to fulfill the shipping and transport needs of the
487   Exchange shall be resolved by the Exchange by acquiring, leasing, or renting private
488   shipping and transport facilities, through supplementary appropriations.
489
490   To respond to the storage needs of the Exchange, the mandate of the PNOC Shipping
491   and Transport Corporation is hereby extended to include storage, besides shipping and
492   transport. The PSTC is thus renamed as the PNOC Shipping, Transport, and Storage
493   Corporation (PSTSC), hereon referred to as the Logistics Company.
494
495   To effectively empower the Exchange, together with the use of the oil depots and
496   storage facilities of Petron Corporation, the newly established PSTSC shall hereby
497   acquire majority equity control over vital, national oil storage facilities through the
498   purchase of stock. PSTSC shall initially acquire, through supplementary appropriations
499   and succeeding general appropriations, majority stake in the top three (3) to five (5) oil
500   depots in the country. PSTSC shall acquire more stock in these corporations and shall
501   likewise take control of, lease, or rent other vital oil storage facilities as the need arises
502   for the Exchange and as is feasible according to the Centralized Petroleum Procurement
503   Plan under Section 8 of this Act.
504
505   The Logistics Company shall initially attempt to buy back the shares at the Philippine
506   Stock Exchange. If the minimum 51% equity from the involved corporations is not
507   purchased, the Exchange shall cause the expropriation, subject to just and fair
508   compensation, of shares from foreign investors or from local investors, or
509   whoever/whichever has possession/ownership/control/custody thereof.
510
511   SEC. 13. Transitory Provision. – Upon the effectivity of this Act, the Philippine
512   National Oil Company (PNOC) shall, upon the first year of effectivity of this Act,
513   reacquire equity in Petron Corporation by at least 11% of its total subscribed stocks.
514   PNOC shall initially buy back the shares at the Philippine Stock Exchange. If the
515   minimum 11% is not repurchased, the PNOC shall cause the expropriation, subject to
516   just and fair compensation, of Petron stocks.
517
518   For the first year, upon the effectivity of this Act, Four Billion Pesos (₱4,000,000,000)
519   shall be appropriated by the National Government for this initial repurchase of Petron
520   stocks. Earnings from the stake of PNOC in Petron Corporation shall be utilized as the
521   secondary funding source for this stock repurchase.

523   SEC. 14. Full Nationalization of Petron Corporation. – The PNOC shall reacquire
524   100% ownership of Petron Corporation over a period of four (4) years. Reacquisition
525   costs shall mainly be funded by the National Government, through annual budget
526   appropriations. The secondary source for the full repurchase shall be the expanding
527   earnings of PNOC from its increasing stake in Petron Corporation over the period of
528   reacquisition.
529
530   To achieve this objective, the PNOC is hereby required to aggressively and consistently
531   improve the operations and income of Petron Corporation in line with the policies of
532   this Act. The eventual corporate merger of the PNOC and Petron Corporation shall be
533   a matter of policy.

535   SEC. 15. Buffer Supply. – Once the Exchange has reached procurement levels where
536   100% of the previous year’s actual total importation is already being procured for the
537   current year, the Exchange shall procure greater volume than the present year’s total
538   national demand or importation forecasts, if warranted by the availability of funds. Any
539   surplus inventories from present year’s petroleum procurement shall serve as stock for
540   the Buffer Supply of the Exchange.
541
542   Buffer supplies shall thereon be maintained and expanded during periods of relatively
543   low world prices of oil, lower levels of consumption, and during periods where
544   commodity swaps and bilateral trade negotiations bear extraordinary levels of supply.
545
546   These supplies shall be withdrawn during periods of extremely high global prices of oil
547   and during shortages in order to delay any necessary price increases and bridge the gap
548   between import levels and actual domestic demand.
549
550   The Exchange shall set minimum Buffer Supply levels at the conclusion of the
551   Centralized Petroleum Procurement Plan. At this point, the Exchange shall set a
552   program to progressively increase the minimum Buffer Supply level until a suitable
553   floor level can be maintained. Minimum Buffer Supply levels and subsequent
554   modifications shall be set by the Board of the Exchange.
555
556   When Buffer Supply minimum floor levels have been maintained for at least five (5)
557   years, extraordinary gains from existing inventories due to extremely large increases in
558   global prices can be plowed back, by recommendation of the Board of the Exchange,
559   into the funding resources of the Exchange.

728   SEC. 18. Petroleum Security Procedures. – The Exchange shall draw up plans to cope

730   or inaccessible, and enter into such preliminary negotiations or arrangements with
731   possible alternative sources as may be necessary to assure a stable, adequate, and

735   SEC. 19. Penalties for Violations. –
736
737      a. Any person who violates any provision of this Act or any order, decision, ruling,
738         or regulation of the Council shall, upon conviction, be sentenced for a period of
739         not less than six months and not more than five years and a fine of not less than
740         ten thousand pesos and not more than fifty thousand pesos. If the offender is a
741         corporation, partnership, or juridical person, the penalty shall be imposed on the
742         officer or officers responsible for permitting or causing a violation by the
743         corporation, partnership, or juridical person of the provisions of this Act;
744
745      b. If the offender is a government official or employee, the maximum penalty
746         prescribed in paragraph (a) shall be imposed and the offender shall suffer the
747         additional penalty of perpetual disqualification from public office without
748         prejudice to any administrative action against him;
749
750      c. If the offender is a member of the PNOC-National Petroleum Corporation or any
751         of its attached agencies and companies, and the commission of the offense is
752         attended by clear abuse of discretion on his part, or by any corrupt practice
753          defined in Republic Act No. 3019, otherwise known as the “Anti-Graft and
754          Corrupt Practices Act,” or other similar irregularity, the penalty imposed shall
755          be a fine of one hundred thousand pesos and imprisonment of not less than ten
756          years. A similar penalty shall be imposed upon any private person, whether in
757          the government service or not, who induces, aids, or abets the offender in the
758          commission of the offense;
759
760      d. It shall be an offense, penalized as provided in paragraph (b) or (c) above,
761         whichever may be applicable, for any person serving in the PNOC-National
762         Petroleum Corporation or any of its attached agencies and companies, either as
763         a Chairperson, Member, or as a member of the staff o directly or indirectly be
764         employed by, or to receive any compensation from, or to have any direct or
765         indirect financial interest in, any firm engaged in the business of exploring,
766         exploiting, importing, exporting, re-exporting, shipping, transporting,
767         processing, refining, storing, marketing, distributing, and selling both at
768         wholesale and retail any petroleum resource; and
769
770      e. Any alien violating this Act shall, in addition to the penalty herein provided, be
771         deported after service of sentence and shall not be permitted reentry into the
772         Philippines.
773
774      f. All monetary penalties shall directly accrue to the PNOC-National Petroleum
775         Corporation.
