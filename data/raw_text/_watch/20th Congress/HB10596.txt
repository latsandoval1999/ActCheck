                                   EXPLANATORY NOTE

“Humihinga pa ba?”

Those were the first words uttered by the mother of Chris Lorenz Fabian when she
arrived at his school in Tacloban. By then, she already knew that her son had been shot.
What she did not yet know was whether he was still alive.

A mother who knew her son as someone who would not fight back even when he was
bullied, even though he was physically capable of defending himself, was suddenly
confronted with a reality no parent should ever have to face.

Chris Lorenz Fabian, Ayessa Nicole Dazo, and Joyancee Separa lost their lives in the
school shooting in Tacloban, a tragedy that shocked not only the families of the victims
but our whole nation.

In the days that followed, the tragedy drew widespread public attention. Public officials,
members of the media, and concerned citizens reached out to the grieving families. But
amidst that attention, Christopher's mother acknowledged a reality she had already come
to accept: today, their story is at the height of public attention and a lot of people
are reaching out; but when the public moves on, she knows that attention will fade,
and people will eventually forget.

The families, however, will not.
Long after the headlines disappear, they will continue to live with the empty seats at their
tables, the unanswered questions, and the permanent absence of their children.

It is in this context that this measure is proposed.

No law can bring Chris Lorenz, Ayessa Nicole, and Joyancee back. No amendment to our
criminal laws can undo what happened inside that school. But the deaths of these victims
must compel us to examine whether our laws adequately respond when children commit
acts involving the gravest forms of violence.

Republic Act No. 9344, otherwise known as the “Juvenile Justice and Welfare Act of 2006,”
as amended by Republic Act No. 10630, established a juvenile justice system founded on
restorative justice, diversion, intervention, and rehabilitation. These principles remain
essential. Children in conflict with the law must continue to be treated in a manner that
recognizes their age, capacity for change, dignity, and potential for rehabilitation.

But compassion and rehabilitation cannot mean the absence of accountability.

The State has a fundamental duty to protect children, including children in conflict with
the law. It has an equally solemn duty to protect other children, families, and communities
from violence, to recognize the suffering of victims, and to ensure that the justice system
provides an appropriate response to the gravity of an offense.

This measure therefore seeks to strengthen and recalibrate the juvenile justice
framework while preserving its fundamental child-appropriate safeguards. It provides
that children twelve (12) years of age or under at the time of the commission of an offense
shall remain exempt from criminal liability and shall instead undergo appropriate
intervention. Children above twelve (12) but below eighteen (18) years of age may be
held criminally liable, subject to child-appropriate proceedings, with diversion,
restorative justice, intervention, and rehabilitation continuing to be prioritized whenever
legally permissible.

The bill likewise provides a more appropriate framework for heinous crimes. Where a
child above twelve (12) but below eighteen (18) years of age commits such an offense,
special rules on custody and disposition shall apply. For persons found guilty by final
judgment of heinous crimes committed while minors, transfer to the appropriate regular
penal facility shall be mandatory upon reaching eighteen (18) years of age, subject to
appropriate transition measures and continued access to rehabilitation programs.

The measure also recognizes that responsibility cannot rest solely upon the child when
adults knowingly contribute to juvenile offending. It accordingly imposes liability upon
parents, guardians, or persons exercising parental authority who knowingly encourage,
direct, compel, profit from, or induce a child to commit an offense; whose gross negligence
or willful failure to exercise reasonable supervision substantially contributes to the
commission of an offense; or who unlawfully assist a child in evading lawful custody or
proceedings. Safeguards are retained to ensure that no parent or guardian is punished
merely by reason of parenthood and that liability must be established beyond reasonable
doubt.
This bill does not seek to abandon rehabilitation as the foundation of juvenile justice.
Neither does it proceed from the belief that every child who commits an offense is beyond
reform. Rather, it recognizes that rehabilitation and accountability need not be opposing
principles. A justice system can believe in the capacity of a child to change while still
recognizing the gravity of the harm inflicted upon another human being.

Most importantly, this measure asks that we remember the people behind the cases that
eventually disappear from our headlines.

Chris' mother understands that the attention surrounding their tragedy will not last
forever. She also understands that changing the law will never return the lives that were
taken.

But their deaths need not be without consequence or meaning.

If this tragedy can compel us to examine the gaps in our laws, strengthen accountability
for the most serious offenses, better protect our children and communities, and prevent
another family from asking whether their child is still breathing, then the lives lost will
have left behind something more enduring than a headline.

We cannot bring them back. But we can remember them through action, and we
can give meaning to their loss by ensuring that what happened to them is not
simply forgotten when the public attention fades.

In view of the foregoing, ug sa ngalan sa SERBISYONG TINUORAY, the undersigned
respectfully requests the immediate passage of this measure.

SECTION 2. Section 6 of Republic Act No. 9344, as amended, is hereby amended to read
as follows:

        “SEC. 6. Minimum Age of Criminal Responsibility. — A child twelve
        (12) years of age or under at the time of the commission of the offense
        shall be exempt from criminal liability. However, the child shall be
        subjected to an intervention program pursuant to Section 20 of this Act.

        A child above twelve (12) years but below eighteen (18) years of
        age shall be subject to criminal liability and be proceeded against in
        accordance     with     this   Act     and    applicable     procedural
        rules: Provided, That diversion, restorative justice, and child-
        appropriate proceedings shall be prioritized when legally permissible.

        Exception      for    Heinous      Crimes. — Notwithstanding         the
        foregoing, when the offense committed is a heinous crime, the
        child above twelve (12) years of age but below eighteen (18) years
        of age shall be proceeded against in accordance with this Act, subject to
        the special rules on custody, disposition, and transition to adult penal
        custody as provided under this Act and its amendments.

        The exemption from criminal liability herein established does not include
        exemption from civil liability, which shall be enforced in accordance with
        existing laws.”**

For purposes of this Act, “heinous crimes” shall have the meaning and coverage
provided under existing laws and jurisprudence, unless otherwise defined by Congress
in a separate enactment.

SECTION 3. Section 20 of Republic Act No. 9344, as amended, is hereby amended to read
as follows:

        “SEC. 20. Children Below the Age of Criminal Responsibility. – If it has
        been determined that the child taken into custody is fifteen (15) years old
        or below, the authority which will have an initial contact with the child,
        in consultation with the local social welfare and development officer, has
        the duty to immediately release the child to the custody of his/her parents
        or guardian, or in the absence thereof, the child’s nearest relative. The
        child shall be subjected to a community-based intervention program
        supervised by the local social welfare and development officer, unless the
        best interest of the child requires the referral of the child to a youth care
        facility or ‘Bahay Pag-asa’ managed by LGUs or licensed and/or
        accredited NGOs monitored by the DSWD.

                                             XXX

        THIS PROVISION SHALL NOT APPLY WHEN THE OFFENSE CHARGE IS
        A HEINOUS CRIME, AS PROVIDED IN THIS ACT.”

SECTION 4. Section 20-D of Republic Act No. 9344, as amended, is hereby amended to
read as follows:

        “SEC. 20-D. Criminal Liability of Parents, Guardians, or Persons
        Exercising Parental Authority; Duties; Presumptions. — The parents,
        legal guardians, or persons exercising parental authority over a child in
        conflict with the law (CICL) shall have the duty to:
        (a) exercise reasonable supervision, guidance, and care;
        (b) cooperate with and ensure the child’s attendance and compliance
        with diversion, intervention, and rehabilitation programs; and
        (c) appear when required by the court, prosecutor, barangay authorities,
        or social welfare officer.

        (1) Criminal Liability. — Without prejudice to civil liability and other
        applicable laws, a parent, legal guardian, or person exercising substitute
        parental authority having custody of a child in conflict with the law
        (CICL) shall incur criminal liability when:

        (a) He/she knowingly encourages, directs, commands, compels,
        profits from, or otherwise induces the child to commit an offense; or

        (b) Through gross negligence or willful failure to exercise reasonable
        parental     authority   and      supervision,    he/she substantially
        contributes to the commission of the offense by the child, as determined
        by the court; or

        (c) He/she harbors, conceals, or assists the child to evade lawful
        custody or proceedings, without lawful justification.

        (2) Penalty. — The penalty shall be prision correccional in its
        minimum to medium periods and a fine of not less than Fifty
        Thousand Pesos (₱50,000.00) but not more than Two Hundred
        Thousand Pesos (₱200,000.00), without prejudice to prosecution
        under the Revised Penal Code or special laws when applicable.

        (3) Due Process Safeguards. — No parent/guardian shall be held liable
        under this Section solely by reason of parenthood or guardianship. The
        prosecution must prove beyond reasonable doubt the specific acts or
        omissions under paragraphs (a), (b), or (c), and their substantial
        contribution to the commission of the offense.

        (4) Non-Application to Victims of Abuse. — Where the
        parent/guardian is shown to be a victim of domestic violence, coercion,
        or intimidation materially preventing supervision or reporting, such
        circumstance shall be considered in determining liability.”

SECTION 5. New Section on Mandatory/Discretionary Transfer to Regular Penal
Custody Upon Reaching Eighteen (18) Years Old. —

A new section is hereby inserted in Republic Act No. 9344, as amended, to read as follows:

        “SEC. 43. Transition to Adult Penal Custody Upon Reaching Eighteen
        (18) Years of Age. —
        (a) Coverage. — This Section applies to a person who:
        (1) committed an offense while below eighteen (18) years of age; and
        (2) is found guilty by final judgment; and
        (3) is serving a disposition, rehabilitation program, or sentence in a youth
        facility, Bahay Pag-asa, IJISC, or other child-appropriate facility pursuant
        to this Act or court order.

        (b) Mandatory Transfer for Heinous Crimes. — If the person was
        found guilty of a heinous crime, the person shall, upon reaching
        eighteen (18) years of age, be mandatorily transferred to the regular
        penal facility under the Bureau of Corrections or Bureau of Jail
        Management and Penology, as appropriate, to serve the remainder of the
        penalty: Provided, That the court shall ensure appropriate transition
        measures, security classification, and access to rehabilitation programs
        suited to young adult offenders.

        (c) Discretionary Transfer for Non-Heinous Crimes. — If the person
        was found guilty of a non-heinous offense, the court, upon motion or
        motu proprio and after hearing, may order transfer to a regular penal
        facility upon reaching eighteen (18) years of age, considering:
        (1) the person’s conduct and progress reports;
        (2) risk to public safety;
        (3) victim safety and reparation compliance;
        (4) the feasibility of continued rehabilitation in a youth or youth-oriented
        facility; and
        (5) recommendations of the social worker and the multi-disciplinary
        team.

        (d) Implementing Custodial Arrangements. — The Department of
        Social Welfare and Development (DSWD), the Department of the Interior
        and Local Government (DILG), the Bureau of Corrections (BuCor), the
        Bureau of Jail Management and Penology (BJMP), and the Juvenile Justice
        and Welfare Council (JJWC) shall issue joint protocols to ensure safe,
        orderly transition and continuity of rehabilitation programming.”**

SECTION 6. Conforming Amendment; Harmonization with the 2019 Supreme
Court Revised Rule on Children in Conflict with the Law. —

All courts, prosecutors, law enforcement agencies, local government units, and concerned
agencies shall, to the maximum extent consistent with this Act, implement this Act in
a manner that aligns procedures, terminology, and child-appropriate safeguards with
the 2019 Supreme Court Revised Rule on Children in Conflict with the Law, including
the serious-crimes handling and commitment procedures reflected in the Rule and in the
statutory IJISC framework.
Provided, That      such     procedural    alignment      shall not be  construed     to:
(a) diminish or negate the policy choice under this Act on mandatory transfer upon
reaching eighteen (18) years old for persons found guilty of heinous crimes; or
(b) bar Congress from setting substantive standards on criminal responsibility and
custodial disposition consistent with the Constitution.
SECTION 7. Implementing Rules and Regulations. — Within ninety (90) days from
effectivity, the JJWC, in coordination with the DSWD, DOJ, DILG, DepEd, BuCor, BJMP, and
other concerned agencies, shall promulgate the IRR to implement this Act, consistent with
the juvenile justice framework under RA 9344 (2006), as amended, including the Bahay
Pag-asa and IJISC mechanisms under RA 10630 (2013) and the relevant judicial
procedures under A.M. No. 02-1-18-SC (2019).

SECTION 8. Separability Clause. — If any provision of this Act is declared
unconstitutional or invalid, the other provisions not affected thereby shall remain in full
force and effect.

SECTION 9. Repealing Clause. — All laws, presidential decrees, executive orders, rules
and regulations, memorandum circulars, and other issuances or parts thereof
inconsistent with this Act are hereby repealed or modified accordingly.

SECTION 10. Effectivity. — This Act shall take effect fifteen (15) days after its publication
in the Official Gazette or in a newspaper of general circulation.
