PROVISIONS OF THE NATIONAL INTERNAL REVENUE CODE (NIRC) OF 1997
OR REPUBLIC ACT NO. 8424, AS AMENDED

                                    EXPLANATORY NOTE

        According to a 2022 World Bank report, the Philippines ranked 15th out of 63
countries in income inequality, having one of the highest income inequality rates in
East Asia.1 In the country, the top 1% of income earners contribute 17% of the national
income while the bottom 50% capture only 14%.2 According to the 2023 Forbes
Philippines’ 50 Richest list, the P3.95 trillion net worth of the country’s 50 richest
reported in 2022 increased to P4.44 trillion in 2023, marking a 12.5% increase and
reflecting an even widening income gap.

       The 2022 World Bank report emphasized how income inequality is perpetuated
by the intergenerational cycle of poverty.3 For instance, it was found that families with
limited financial resources and lower educational backgrounds have significantly less
access to antenatal care (ANC) and postnatal care (PNC) compared to the national
average, resulting in elevated rates of stunting, underweight, and wasting among them
compared to wealthier counterparts.4 At the same time, while students of more affluent
households are able to invest heavily on education, with a per capita expenditure on
education more than 22 times that of the poorest households, children of lower-income
households face reduced enrollment rates and are less likely to be placed in the age-
appropriate grades.5 Clearly, the limited access to health care, education, and
opportunities for skill development diminishes the opportunities for upward mobility,

        Government interventions are therefore necessary to address the rising gap in
inequality brought about by systemic failures, including having a regressive taxation
system. The country’s current taxation system include consumption taxes, such as the
value-added tax (VAT) and excise taxes, that more significantly impact smaller
incomes, thus widening the gap between rich and poor, and the marginalized and the
elite.6

       At present, a common practice among governments across Asia to reduce
public budget deficits is to enforce austerity measures, such as spending cuts,
regressive tax increases, or both.7 However, at a time when state provision of
healthcare, education, infrastructure, and other basic public goods are crucial to
sustain the lives and livelihoods of the people, these anti-people taxes and austerity
measures would simply be unacceptable.

        This bill proposes the imposition of an annual Ultra-Wealth Tax on the country’s
billionaires or the ultra-rich whose net taxable assets exceed one billion pesos
(P1,000,000,000). The rates of the ultra-wealth tax shall be as follows: 1% for wealth
above P1 billion; 2% for wealth above P2 billion; and 3% for wealth above P3 billion.
Considering the limited number of billionaires whose net worth significantly exceed the
net worth of the bottom 50% Filipino income earners, this proposed ultra-wealth tax
shall only target a small number of taxpayers, unlike in the case of consumption taxes
that affect a majority of the population, heavily impacting upon the poor.

       To ensure that the revenues generated from the ultra-wealth taxes are directed
to the necessary government programs that benefit the general public, this bill
proposes for the exclusive use of the revenues for the following items of public
expenditure: public health services nationwide, education, local government support
fund, social protection and employment, and housing.

       In the 20th Congress, similar bills were filed by Representatives Percival
Cendaña, Jose “Chel” Diokno, Dadah Ismula, Kaka Bag-Ao, Antonio Tinio, Sarah
Elago, Renee Co, and Raymond Mendoza. This bill adopts several provisions from
the aforementioned bills.

        Early approval of this measure is earnestly sought.

171          SEC. 7. Wealth-Tax Unit. – Within 180 days from the effectivity of this Act, the
172   BIR shall create a Wealth-Tax Unit composed of specialized revenue officers and
173   government appraisers who shall review and assess the Self-Assessment Statement
174   of Wealth filed and the wealth tax paid by the billionaires. The Unit shall be authorized
175   to conduct lifestyle audits, asset tracing, and to access domestic and international
176   financial records to verify the accuracy of wealth declarations. The Unit shall
177   investigate and recommend the filing of charges for avoidance, misdeclaration, non-
178   declaration of assets, and other offenses, if any.
179
180         SEC. 8. Penalties. – Any willful misdeclaration or non-declaration of assets shall
181   be subject to the following penalties, with full respect for due process:
182
183             A. A monetary penalty of not less than 20% of the value of the undeclared
184                or misdeclared assets;

186             B. Expropriation or confiscation of the undeclared assets in cases of
187                deliberate concealment or fraud; and
188
189             C. Where appropriate, the filing of criminal charges for tax evasion or
190                related offenses.
