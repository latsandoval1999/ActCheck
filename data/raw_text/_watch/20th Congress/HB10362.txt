                        EXPLANATORY NOTE

     Artificial Intelligence (AI) is no longer a future technology. It is
already reshaping economies, labor markets, education systems, public
governance, media, finance, healthcare, communications, and everyday life
at an unprecedented speed. In the Philippines, AI systems are now widely
used in schools, offices, call centers, banks, hospitals, online platforms,
content creation, transportation, customer service, and government
operations.

     The rapid expansion of AI in the country is already measurable. The
Philippine AI Report 2025 found that more than ninety-two percent (92%)
of surveyed Philippine organizations have adopted AI in some capacity,


                                     i
while more than half (54%) have already used generative AI tools for over a
year. Executive-level AI adoption and deployment continue to accelerate
across industries. Meanwhile, studies indicate that Filipinos are among the
most active users of generative AI tools globally.1

       The Philippine AI market itself is projected to expand rapidly in the
coming years. Industry studies estimate that the Philippine generative AI
market may grow from approximately USD 292 million in 2025 to more than
USD 1.2 billion by 2034, driven by the country’s highly digital population,
expanding internet access, and increasing demand for automation in
business process outsourcing, finance, education, and creative industries. 2

       Despite the speed and scale of AI adoption, the Philippines still has
no comprehensive national law governing the responsible development,
deployment, accountability, oversight, and regulation of AI systems.
Existing laws such as the Data Privacy Act of 2012, the Cybercrime
Prevention Act of 2012, and the Philippine Innovation Act address only
certain aspects of AI-related concerns. At present, there is no unified
national framework that establishes safeguards against harmful AI
systems, protects workers from AI-driven displacement, guarantees
transparency in automated decision-making, promotes AI literacy,
establishes accountability standards, or creates institutional mechanisms
for AI governance. This legal and regulatory gap leaves the public
vulnerable to the risks of unregulated AI deployment.

       AI technologies create enormous opportunities for economic growth,
innovation, scientific advancement, improved public services, and increased

       Without clear national policies, the Philippines risks being
technologically unprepared, economically vulnerable, and institutionally
incapable of addressing the profound social and economic consequences of
AI-driven transformation.

       The absence of governance standards also creates uncertainty for
businesses, educational institutions, innovators, workers, investors, and
government agencies seeking responsible pathways for AI adoption.
Without clear safeguards and accountability mechanisms, public trust in AI
technologies may weaken, while irresponsible or harmful AI deployment
may proliferate unchecked.

       The Constitution recognizes the vital role of science and technology in
nation-building and directs the State to prioritize research, development,
and innovation for national progress. This constitutional mandate also
requires the State to ensure that technological development is ethical,
inclusive, human-centered, rights-based, and consistent with democratic
values.

(2) promote AI literacy, institutional readiness, responsible innovation,

and democratic safeguards; (3) establish rights relating to high-impact AI

that strengthens democracy, public trust, labor rights, human dignity, and
inclusive national development.

14   Section 3. Definition of Terms. – As used in this Act:

15     a. “Advisory Council” refers to the Multi-Stakeholder Advisory Council
16        created under this Act.

17     b. “Artificial Intelligence” or “AI” refers to machine-based systems
18        capable of generating outputs such as predictions, recommendations,
19        decisions, or content that influence physical or virtual environments
20        through learning, reasoning, inference, pattern recognition,
21        adaptation, or similar computational processes commonly associated
22        with human intelligence.

23     c. “Artificial Intelligence System” or “AI System” refers to a machine-
24        based system that, for explicit or implicit objectives, infers, from the
25        input it receives, how to generate outputs such as predictions,
26        recommendations, decisions, or content that may influence physical
27        or virtual environments, and which may operate with varying levels
28        of autonomy and adaptiveness.

29     d. “AI Governance” refers to the laws, policies, standards, safeguards,
30        institutions, and practices of the government and private entities that
31        guide the development, deployment, use, oversight, and
32        accountability of AI systems.


                                          2
1    e. “AI Infrastructure” refers to the physical and digital systems that
2       support the development, training, deployment, operation, storage,
3       maintenance, or use of AI systems, including computing systems,
4       cloud services, networks, data systems, and related facilities.

5    f. “AI Impact Assessment” refers to a structured evaluation conducted
6       before or during the deployment of AI systems to identify, analyze,
7       and mitigate potential risks to individuals, communities, democratic
8       institutions, labor, and the environment.

9    g. “AI Literacy” refers to the knowledge, skills, and critical
10      understanding necessary to safely, responsibly, and effectively
11      evaluate, use, and interact with AI systems.

12   h. “Algorithmic Impact Assessment” refers to an evaluation of the
13      societal effects and risks of automated decision-making systems,
14      particularly those used in public services.

15   i. “Automated Decision-Making” refers to any process by which an AI
16      system makes or substantially influences decisions affecting
17      individuals or groups without or with little meaningful human
18      involvement.

19   j. “Data Center” refers to a facility or physical infrastructure used to
20      house computer systems, servers, storage systems, networking
21      equipment, and related technologies for the processing, storage,
22      management, or transmission of digital data, including data used for
23      AI systems.

24   k. “High-Impact AI System” refers to an AI system that, by reason of its
25      purpose, scale, or manner of deployment, may significantly affect the
26      rights, safety, livelihood, opportunities, access to essential services, or
27      wellbeing of individuals or communities.

28   l. “Just Transition” refers to a a set of principles ensuring that the shift
29      to AI-driven economies protects workers, shares economic benefits
30      fairly, and respects environmental limits. It guarantees that no
31      person, group, or community is left behind or harmed by rapid
32      automation and technological changes.



                                         3
1       m. “Regulatory Sandbox” refers to a regulatory approach, typically
2          summarized in writing and published, that allows live, time-bound
3          testing of innovation under a regulator’s oversight.

4       n. “Synthetic Media” refers to text, audio, image, video, or other media
5          wholly or substantially generated or manipulated through AI

15                                  CHAPTER II

16             ARTIFICIAL INTELLIGENCE BILL OF RIGHTS

17   Section 5. Rights Relating to Artificial Intelligence Systems. Every
18   person affected by AI systems or automated decision-making has the
19   following rights:

20      a. Right to Transparency. Every person has the right to know when a
21         high-impact AI system or automated decision-making is being used
22         on or about them, including the nature and purpose of such system
23         and what it is used for, which must be communicated in plain and
24         clear language.

25      b. Right to Information. Every person has the right to receive complete
26         information regarding the basis of automated decision-making,
27         including the principal factors and considerations that materially
28         influenced the decision, subject to reasonable protection of trade
29         secrets and proprietary information.

30      c. Right Against Discriminatory Automated Decisions. Every person has
31         the right to equal treatment regardless of race, ethnicity, religion, sex
32         characteristics, gender identity or expression, sexual orientation,
33         ability, age, socioeconomic status, or any other protected
34         characteristic under existing laws, in all automated decision-making.

                                           4
1      d. Right to Human Review. Every person has the right to request
2         meaningful human review of automated decision-making, which must
3         be conducted by a person with sufficient authority and competence to
4         change, reverse, or confirm the decision.

5      e. Right to Privacy and Data Protection. Every person has the right to
6         protection of personal information, including sensitive personal
7         information, consistent with the Data Privacy Act of 2012 and any
8         applicable AI-specific data governance rules issued by the
9         Department of Information and Communications Technology (DICT)
10        and the National Privacy Commission (NPC).

11     f. Right to be Forgotten. Every person has the right to demand the
12        permanent deletion of their personal data from all training datasets,
13        storage repositories, and operational parameters of an artificial
14        intelligence system, requiring the data controller to completely erase
15        the data or provably eliminate its influence through machine
16        unlearning or model retraining, unless retention is strictly required
17        by law.
18
19     g. Right to Safety. Every person has the right to protection from AI
20        systems that pose unreasonable or foreseeable risks to a person’s life,
21        health, or wellbeing.

22     h. Right to Disclosure of Synthetic Media. Every person has the right to
23        know when they are exposed to synthetic media. Any synthetic media
24        that materially or deceptively alters or realistically depicts the image
25        or likeness of a person or any statement, event, or circumstance in a
26        manner likely to mislead the public must contain a clear and
27        conspicuous disclosure that it is AI-generated or AI-manipulated.

1                                   CHAPTER VI

2             MONITORING AND ENFOREMENT MECHANISMS

3    Section 20. Prohibited Practices. An employer must not impose
4    performance standards that are unreasonable, opaque, or based solely on
5    AI-generated outputs without meaningful human evaluation. An employer
6    must not impose disciplinary action on the basis of errors caused by an
7    employer-mandated AI system where adequate training, supervision, or
8    safeguards were not provided. Finally, an employer may not use an AI
9    system to evade obligations under labor laws, including obligations relating
10   to security of tenure, minimum wage, employee benefits, and the right to
11   self-organization.

12   The use of AI systems for the following purposes is prohibited:

13      a. Identity theft;

14      b. Fraud and deceptive impersonation;

15      c. Exploitation or abuse of children;

16      d. Unlawful surveillance;

17      e. The creation or dissemination of deceptive synthetic media intended
18         to facilitate fraud, cause public harm, unlawfully manipulate the
19         image or likeness of a person, or infringe upon the rights of others;
20         and

21      f. Other unlawful uses.

22   Section 21. Penalties. – Any person who willfully or knowingly does or
23   who shall aid, permit, or cause to be done any of the prohibited acts will
24   suffer the following penalties:

25      a. Imprisonment of not less than six months or more than six years;

26      b. Accessory penalty of temporary or perpetual absolute disqualification
27         from public office if the offender is a public official at the time of the
28         commission of the offense; and

29      c. Deportation if the offender is an alien.

1    Section 22. Civil Liability and Access to Remedies. Any person who
2    suffers injury or damage arising from the negligent, reckless,
3    discriminatory, deceptive, or unlawful use of an AI system may institute the
4    appropriate civil action under the Civil Code and existing laws. The action
5    shall be brought against the person or entity that deployed, operated, or
6    used the AI system. In addition, it may also be brought against the person
7    or entity that developed or provided the AI system if:

8       a. The AI system was designed, trained, marketed, or offered principally
9          for a use prohibited under Section 20 of this Act or under other penal
10         laws;

11      b. The developer or provider had actual knowledge of a specific unlawful
12         use of its AI system and failed, within a reasonable period, to take
13         reasonable and proportionate measures to prevent its continuation or
14         recurrence;

15      c. The developer or provider failed to create or implement safeguards in
16         the AI system that would be proportionate to the gravity and
17         likelihood of injury and damage; or

18      d. The injury or damage is traceable to a defect in the AI system, or a
19         capability of the AI system or to the failure of the AI developer or
20         provider to comply with the provisions of this Act or the standards
21         issued by the DICT.

22   Where a complainant for any case establishes reasonable grounds to believe
23   that a high-impact AI system materially contributed to an adverse decision
24   affecting them, the court or administrative agency may require the
25   respondent to disclose relevant records, documentation, audit logs, or other
26   information necessary to determine compliance with this Act. Nothing in
27   this Act shall prevent a competent court or government agency from
28   ordering an independent technical audit or expert examination to determine
29   whether an AI system contributed to the alleged injury or damage.

30   Any person found liable under this Section shall be subject to the
31   appropriate remedies under existing laws, including actual, moral, nominal,
32   temperate, or exemplary damages, injunctive relief, and other appropriate
33   relief.

1    Section 23. Violation of Juridical Entities. – If the violation is
2    committed by a corporation, partnership, association, or other juridical
3    entity, the responsible directors or officers thereof shall be held criminally
4    or solidarily liable, in addition to possible sanctions from relevant

6                                  CHAPTER VII

7                              FINAL PROVISIONS

8    Section 24. Appropriations. The amount necessary for the initial
9    implementation of this Act is charged against the current appropriations of
10   the DICT. Thereafter, such amounts as may be necessary for the continued
11   implementation of this Act must be included in the annual General
12   Appropriations Act.

13   Section 25. Implementing Rules and Regulations. Within one hundred
14   eighty (180) days from the effectivity of this Act, the DICT, in coordination
15   with relevant government agencies and stakeholders, will issue the rules
16   and regulations necessary for the effective implementation of this Act.
17   Failure to issue the said rules and regulations will not prevent the
18   effectivity of this Act.

19   Section 26. Separability Clause. If any part or provision of this Act is
20   declared unconstitutional or invalid, the remaining provisions remain in full
21   force and effect.

22   Section 27. Repealing Clause. All laws, executive orders, presidential
23   decrees, rules, and regulations, or parts thereof, inconsistent with this Act
24   are hereby repealed or modified accordingly.

25   Section 28. Effectivity. This Act takes effect fifteen (15) days after
26   publication in the Official Gazette or in at least two (2) newspapers of
27   general circulation, whichever comes earlier.
