25                                      EXPLANATORY NOTE
26
27           Under the Arroyo administration, the Armed Forces of the Philippines (AFP)
28   gained international notoriety for its alleged involvement in a significant number of
29   extrajudicial killings and involuntary disappearances of citizens, many of whom were
30   activists opposed to the administration's policies and programs. Such gross violations
31   drew the attention not only of human rights organizations such as Amnesty International
32   (AI) and Human Rights Watch (HRW), but also the United Nations Special Rapporteur
33   on Summary and Extrajudicial Killings, Philip Alston, and even the United States
34   Senate Defense Committee led by Sen. Barbara Boxer. All acknowledged a link
35   between human rights violations and the AFP's counterinsurgency program, then known
36   as Oplan Bantay Laya. Many noted that the so-called “climate of impunity” enabled
37   perpetrators to commit violations on such a scale.
38
39           The passage of the years and changing of administrations saw only the worsening
40   of the human rights climate in the country. The HRW noted that, under the Aquino
41   administration, “several key institutions, including law enforcement agencies and the
42   justice system, remain weak and the military and police commit human rights violations
43   with impunity” (Human Rights Watch World Report 2011). Until now, only a few
44   military and police personnel have been criminally charged, least of all convicted, for
45   human rights violations.
46

 1           Duterte—who made no secret of his contempt for human rights and human rights
 2   defenders with statements such as “I don’t care about human rights”—has been
 3   repeatedly scored for systemic violations of the people’s rights, state terror, violence,
 4   and repression under his reign by the United Nations and local and international rights
 5   monitors. Under his administration’s bloody anti-illegal drugs campaign, thousands of
 6   civilians, including minors, were killed by state forces, particularly by members of the
 7   Philippine National Police (PNP). Worse, only three policemen were convicted by local
 8   courts for murdering Kian delos Santos, a minor. The majority of perpetrators and
 9   masterminds behind the anti-people war on drugs campaign still remain at large without
10   any case filed against them.
11
12          Furthermore, laws passed during President Duterte’s term, such as the Anti-
13   Terrorism Act, the establishment of the National Task Force to End Local Communist
14   Armed Conflict, and red-tagging as a state policy, only foreshadow the country
15   becoming a killing field in the coming years—a figurative one, with the death of human
16   rights protection, and a literal killing field, with state agents and paramilitaries
17   emboldened to threaten, harass, then kill citizens.
18
19          The government should use every tool at its disposal to promote respect for
20   human rights and end impunity, particularly within the ranks of the military and the
21   police. The national budget is one such tool. Experience shows that the release or non-
22   release of funds can be a powerful instrument for promoting reforms and modifying
23   behavior in the ranks of government. This Bill proposes the introduction of a “no
24   impunity provision” in our laws, one that would link the release of operational funds of
25   units of the AFP and the PNP to their compliance with human rights standards.
26
27           Such a provision will encourage the AFP and PNP chain of command to ensure
28   that their units uphold and respect human rights at all times in the discharge of their
29   duties, even during counterinsurgency operations. Furthermore, it will encourage AFP
30   commanders and PNP chiefs to promptly identify perpetrators and make sure that the
31   proper charges are filed. It also underscores the responsibility of the Secretary of
32   National Defense and the Secretary of Interior Local Government in ensuring that
33   charges are filed against individual perpetrators. For its part, the Commission on
34   Human Rights is given the role of vetting complaints and weeding out spurious
35   allegations.
36
37          The proposed amendment draws some inspiration from the Leahy Law in the
38   United States, a provision in US foreign aid appropriations legislation introduced by
39   Senator Patrick Leahy that prohibits the granting of US military aid to foreign security
40   forces units with a known track record of gross human rights violations.

25   Be it enacted by the Senate and House of Representatives of the Philippines in Congress
26   assembled:
27
28   SECTION 1. State Policies. The State shall value the dignity of every human being
29   and guarantee full respect for human rights. Civilian authority shall always be supreme
30   over the military, and the Armed Forces of the Philippines and the Philippine National
31   Police shall stand as the protector of the people and the State. The State shall therefore
32   take steps to ensure that mechanisms designed towards the genuine exercise of civilian
33   supremacy and the full promotion, protection, and realization of human rights are in
34   place.
35
36   SEC. 2. No impunity provision. Upon a determination by the Commission on Human
37   Rights (CHR) of credible evidence that a unit of the Armed Forces of the Philippines or
38   the Philippine National Police has committed a gross violation of human rights, the
39   maintenance and other operating expenses of the unit concerned shall be withheld until
40   the CHR determines and reports to the President of the Philippines that the Secretary of
41   the Department of National Defense and the Secretary of the Department of the Interior
42   Local Government have taken effective measures to identify the responsible member or
43   members of the unit and ensured that the appropriate charges are filed.
44
45        The determination provided for in this Section may be done motu proprio by the
46   CHR initiated either through a complaint filed by aggrieved party or by any concerned

 2   be required in the filing of the complaint, after which, the CHR shall make the
 3   determination within five (5) calendar days.
 4
 5   SEC. 3. Implementing Rules and Regulations. The CHR, upon consultation with
 6   human rights advocates and organizations, shall promulgate, within fifteen (15) days
 7   from the effectivity of this Act, the rules and regulations necessary for its
 8   implementation.
 9
10   SEC. 4. Repealing Clause. All laws, decrees, resolutions, orders, or ordinances or
11   parts thereof inconsistent with this Act are hereby repealed, amended, or modified
12   accordingly.
13
14   SEC. 5. Separability Clause. If any provision of this Act is declared unconstitutional,
15   the same shall not affect the validity and effectivity of the other provisions hereof.
16
17   SEC. 6. Effectivity. This Act shall take effect fifteen (15) days after its publication in
18   the Official Gazette or in one newspaper of general circulation.
