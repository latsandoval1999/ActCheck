 AN ACT TO ENSURE GENDER-RESPONSIVE AND INCLUSIVE PROTOCOLS
AND PROGRAMMING TO ADDRESS THE GENDER-DIFFERENTIATED NEEDS
    OF WOMEN DURING EMERGENCIES INCLUDING PUBLIC HEALTH
  CONCERNS, PANDEMICS, AND NATURAL AND MAN-MADE DISASTERS

                              EXPLANATORY NOTE

       The Philippines faces various crises annually that affect millions of Filipinos
annually. Arguably, the most significant and costly event of the 21st century, the
COVID-19 pandemic took over the world and drastically changed the global social
landscape as we know it. The COVID-19 pandemic became an invisible and vicious
threat that brought harm and fear to millions of Filipinos worldwide and tested the
capabilities of the government to respond to rising global trends at such magnitude.

        State policies aimed to combat the spread of the virus heavily focused on
isolation and limitation of social interactions. Community Quarantine protocols became
the government’s primary solution both in response to the pandemic and in response
to the people’s needs. These restrictive mechanisms has changed the Filipino people’s
view on how social and public services are given and attained, with many experiencing
the increased gap and injustice between social classes and sectors in the society. The
unprecedented time brought out a new reality for Filipinos, along with a new set of
challenges and threats that exacerbated existing social inequalities.

       As one of the most heavily affected sectors at the time, the women’s sector
experienced issues attributed to the vastly changing times and environment on top of
an already aggravated state of affairs. A study conducted by the International Public
Policy Observatory stated that the impact of COVID-19 on gender-based violence in
the Philippines is ‘One of the most insidious consequences of the pandemic’. On the
first four (4) months of the Community Quarantine alone, 4,260 cases of violence
against women and children (VAWC) were reported to the Philippine National Police
(PNP), of which 2,183 were committed against women. 1

       As stunning as it may be, the Philippine Commission on Women (PCW) declared
that the number may even be higher as many cases may not have even be reported
due to the restricted mobility of people that disabled women to seek help from the
authorities. An independent regional study conducted by UN Women, UN Population
Fund and Quilt.ai, an internet analytics company, gave further credence to the PCW’s
assertion. This study found a spike in online searches related to violence against
women in the Philippines and seven other Asian countries with searches relating to
physical violence against women rising by 63% in the Philippines, and help-seeking
queries increasing by 10%. 2

      Furthermore, State response to the onset of gender-based violence (GBV) can
even be hampered or limited by restrictive measures in place, such as that of the
Community Quarantine. The pandemic also disrupted GBV survivors’ access to legal
remedies and conciliatory processes, rendering it unenforceable due to the
circumstances that may affect the implementation of the government’s measures
against a public health crisis.

        As an effort to curtail these unfortunate incidences, this measure seeks to
institutionalize and ensure a gender-responsive and inclusive protocol and
policymaking in the Philippines. As an effort to curtail these unfortunate incidences,
this measure seeks to institutionalize and ensure a gender-responsive and inclusive
protocol and policymaking in the Philippines and guarantee the meaningful
participation of women and vulnerable sectors in decision-making processes. Through
this framework, the State affirms its commitment to equitable, rights-based, and
inclusive governance.



        In view of the foregoing, the immediate passage of this bill is earnestly sought.

 7   Sec. 2. Declaration of P olicy . – The State hereby affirms its role as the primary
 8   duty-bearer in ensuring the enjoyment and fulfillment of all human rights during
 9   natural and other disasters and emergencies, including a pandemic, especially the
10   right to life, which permits no derogation even during emergencies, and the right to
11   health, so that every individual shall enjoy the highest attainable level of health. It
12   affirms its Constitutional duties to guarantee the equality of men and women to the
13   enjoyment of their human rights, with focus on the differentiated needs of, and impact
14   of emergencies on, women, prohibit discrimination, and create an enabling
15   environment under the “new normal” that guarantees the human rights of women. It
16   acknowledges its corresponding duties relating to women's rights to participation and
17   representation, protection from gender-based violence during emergency situations,
18   sexual, reproductive, and mental health, and economic empowerment of women. It
19   adopts a zero-tolerance policy towards gender-based violence, abuse, and
20   exploitation, which is rooted in gender inequality and discrimination. The
21   implementation of approaches and strategies relevant to disaster risk reduction,
22   emergency management must integrate human rights and gendered lenses. It is
23   fundamental that policies, plans, programs, actions, conduct, and results are non-
24   discriminatory and that no one is left behind. Towards ensuring gender equality, the

 5   situation.

10   Sec. 5. Hum anitarian P rinciples Guiding Em ergency P rogram m ing and
11   M anagem ent to Address Gender-Differentiated Needs of W om en I ncluding
12   At-risk I ndividuals and Groups During Em ergencies. - The following principles
13   shall ground, inform, and guide the formulation and implementation of policies, plans,
14   programs and other appropriate measures, including affirmative actions, in managing
15   emergencies:
16
17      (a) A survivor-centered approach which creates a supportive environment in which
18          survivors' rights and wishes are respected, their safety is ensured, and they are
19          treated with dignity and respect;
20      (b) A human rights-based approach that seeks to analyze and address the root
21          causes of discrimination and inequality to ensure that everyone has the right
22          to live with freedom and dignity, safe from violence, exploitation and abuse, in
23          accordance with principles of human rights law;
24      (c) A community-based approach that ensures that affected populations are
25          engaged actively as partners in developing strategies related to their protection
26          and the provision of humanitarian assistance. This approach involves direct
27          involvement of women, girls, and other at-risk groups at all stages in the
28          humanitarian response, to identify protection risks and solutions, and build on
29          existing community-based protection mechanisms;
30      (d) The humanitarian principles of humanity, impartiality, independence and
31          neutrality which should underpin the implementation of the Minimum Standards
32          and are essential to maintaining access to affected populations and ensuring
33          an effective humanitarian response;
34      (e) An equity approach that prioritizes the allocation of interventions and support
35          that considers the intersectionality of women's circumstances that are affected
36          disproportionately because of the multiple disadvantaged identities they
37          possess (e.g. indigenous and poor woman);
38      (f) The "do no harm" approach which takes into account all measures necessary
39          to avoid exposing people to further harm as a result of the actions of
40          humanitarian actors;
41      (g) The Principles of Partnership which comprise a framework for all actors in the
42          humanitarian space to follow principles of equality, transparency, a results-
43          oriented approach, responsibility, and complementarity;
44      (h) Taking into account the best interests of the child wherein child and adolescent
45          girl survivors of sexual abuse have the right to have their best interests
46          assessed and determined and taken as a primary consideration in all decisions
47          that affect them; and
 1      (i) Rights-based policing wherein human rights principles and practices are taught
 2          and applied at all levels, in policy, doctrinal, operational and administrative
 3          functions of the police organization.

16   Sec. 7. National P reparedness and R esponse P lan to Address the Gender
17   differentiated Needs of W om en during an Em ergency. - A coherent,
18   appropriate, efficient, developmental, evidence-based, and survivor-centered national
19   program against GBV and addressing gender-differentiated needs of women especially
20   from marginalized sectors shall be developed to inform, direct, and coordinate its
21   implementation at all levels and to ensure accessibility, availability, and quality of
22   services at the community level.
23
24   For this purpose, a Task Force, to be co-headed by the National Disaster Risk
25   Reduction and Management Council (NDRRMC) and the Philippine Commission on
26   Women (PCW), in coordination with the IATF, Climate Change Commission, and
27   Department of the Interior and Local Government, Department of Health, Department
28   of Social Welfare and Development, and Department of Education, shall come up with
29   said National Plan within six (6) months from passage of this Act, to be cascaded to
30   the local government units where local counterparts may be established, and must be
31   automatically integrated into the national strategy to manage the emergency. This
32   plan shall be subjected to review and improvement every two (2) years. It shall
33   address GBV and gender-differentiated needs at all stages of emergency
34   management, and anticipating emergency-related events and factors that might
35   impede the functioning of legal, medical, social, remedial, and other protection
36   measures and mechanisms, to include, but not limited to:
37
38      (a) Reviewing lessons based on the responsiveness, preparedness, and impact of
39          previous interventions from the COVID-19 pandemic to address the specific
40          barriers and institutional obstacles faced by women including at-risk individuals
41          and groups during emergencies that result in GBV, lack of access to sexual,
42          reproductive, and mental health services, and discontinued education, taking
43          into account intersecting vulnerabilities and the multiple burdens they
44          experience, and addressing stigma and discriminatory practices, attitudes, and
45          behaviors resulting in GBV;
46      (b) Gathering, managing, and utilizing data concerning the risks and incidence of
47          online and offline GBV, limited access to sexual, reproductive, and mental
 1          health services, and discontinued education to inform policies and programs to
 2          achieve gender inclusive responses and address gaps and lapses;
 3      (c) Providing prompt, accurate, disaggregated data by age, gender, ethnicity, and
 4          special needs, accessible, up-to-date, comprehensible, science-based, and
 5          transparent data on women during emergencies;
 6      (d) Developing protocols and guidelines of conduct relating to the delivery of
 7          service to women in the context of emergencies, providing access to essential
 8          services and government social protection and safety nets, including water,
 9          hygiene, and sanitation (WASH), food and nutrition security;
10      (e) Providing capacity-building for service providers, such as but not limited to
11          frontline workers, volunteers, and governmental workers, on early identification
12          and mitigation by building on their gendered analyses skills, strengthening
13          nationwide helplines, and community-level referral mechanisms to ensure they
14          are functional to prevent and respond to cases including chain management
15          and service provision plans for these services to stay open during the outbreak;
16      (f) Providing a clear channel of communication with GBV service providers and
17          actors as well as women's organizations, GBV and Sexual and Reproductive
18          Health watch groups, and local GBV service providers and survivor-centered
19          referral systems through the utilization of technology;
20      (g) Assisting in the planning of national, local and community units by developing,
21          identifying, and sharing best practices;
22      (h) Providing technical assistance, training, and consultation to improve
23          preparedness and response capabilities, with focus on the needs of women and
24          at-risk individuals and groups, including to support the achievement of
25          evidence-based benchmarks and objective standards to monitor, assess and
26          measure levels of preparedness, response and recovery;
27      (i) Improving systems of public, private, and civil society collaboration and
28          partnership to formulate, and address gaps and inefficiencies in the policies,
29          plans, and programs to address GBV during emergencies, including systems of
30          global cooperation; and
31      (j) Carrying out other duties related to preparedness, response, and recovery
32          activities for women, as the Task Force may deem appropriate.
