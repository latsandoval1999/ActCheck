                                  EXPLANATORY NOTE

        The 1987 Constitution guarantees equal access to opportunities for public service and
prohibits political dynasties. A public office is a public trust and democratic governance
requires open and genuine political competition. However, Congress has yet to pass a law that
enacts this Constitutional provision, and thus political dynasties continue to dominate elective
positions across the country.

       This dominance has a negative effect on social justice and democratic inclusion. The
concentration of power in a select number of political families pose significant barriers to
meaningful participation in governance and electoral competition for most citizens.

       Nationwide, 113 out of 149 city mayors belong to political families, 71 of 82
provincial governors are members of political dynasties, and approximately eighty percent
(80%) of members of the House of Representatives come from political clans. This
dominance limits voter choice to candidates drawn from a small number of entrenched
families, weakening genuine electoral competition and accountability.

        As defined by the Congressional Policy and Budget Research Department (CPBRD)
Policy Note CN2024-04, a political dynasty is a family in which two or more members secure
elective public office in at least two elections, simultaneously or in succession. Electoral
success becomes a matter of having the correct surname rather than possessing the necessary
qualifications.

          While it is recognized that not all members of political families are unqualified or
ill-intentioned, the repeated occupation of elective positions by members of the same family
tends to consolidate political influence, name recognition, and access to public resources.
Over time, this consolidation discourages qualified challengers and narrows the range of
meaningful choices available to the electorate.

        Research conducted by the Ateneo School of Government shows that dynastic
dominance is deeply embedded in local political and economic structures. The presence of
political dynasties is correlated with poorer development outcomes and higher levels of
poverty. These findings show that dynastic dominance inhibit accountability necessary for
good governance.

       Political dynasties exist in many democracies, but their prevalence and impact are not
as regressive as what is seen in the Philippines. In most established Western democracies,
such as Germany, Canada, and the United States, dynastic politicians account for a relatively

small share of elected officials, often estimated at five to ten percent of national legislatures.
While prominent political families exist in these countries, electoral competition remains
largely open, and political parties play a stronger role in candidate selection.

        In Japan, dynastic politics is more pronounced, with studies showing that more than
one-fourth of members of the national legislature belong to political families. Even so, strong
party institutions and bureaucratic systems help limit the concentration of power at the local
level. In Indonesia, political dynasties have grown following decentralization and direct local
elections, particularly at the provincial and municipal levels, but their overall reach remains
less extensive than in the Philippines.

        To comply with the Constitutional mandate and halt the further entrenchment of these
political dynasties, this measure proposes a ban on candidates related to other candidates or
incumbents, in any local or national elective post, to whom they are related to up to the
second degree of consanguinity or affinity.

       This prohibition recognizes the nature of Filipino families, where in-laws and
extended relations are considered part of the family and partake in the efforts and rewards of
family ventures, including dynastic politics. The prohibition targets where the transfer and
sharing of power is most pronounced and most effective, while preserving voter choice.

       This bill seeks compliance with the Constitution in its letter and spirit to foster
genuine democratic participation.

       In view of the foregoing, the immediate passage of this bill is earnestly sought.

                           AN ACT
DEFINING AND PROHIBITING POLITICAL DYNASTIES, ESTABLISHING THE
NECESSARY MECHANISMS TO PREVENT AND ELIMINATE THE
PROLIFERATION THEREOF, AND PROVIDING PENALTIES FOR VIOLATIONS
AND FOR OTHER PURPOSES

Be it enacted by the Senate and House of Representatives of the Philippines in Congress
assembled:

       SECTION 1. Short Title. – This Act shall be known as the “Anti-Political Dynasty Act.”

        SEC. 2. Declaration of Policy. – The State shall guarantee equal access to opportunities
for public service and prohibit political dynasties as may be defined by law. To this effect, the
State shall define political dynasties and provide effective and accountable mechanisms in the
prohibition thereof.

       SEC 3. Definition of Terms. – As used in this Act, the following terms shall mean:

               a) Political dynasties - refer to family members running for or holding public
                  office, among national or local elective offices, as well as any and all other
                  elective offices or positions that may be created by law, that has the potential
                  or actual effect of concentrating, consolidating, or perpetuating political
                  power of the family.

               b) Candidate - refers to any person aspiring for or seeking an elective public
                  office, who has filed a certificate of candidacy by himself or herself, or
                  through an accredited political party aggroupment, or coalition of parties. Any
                  organized group of persons which has registered with the Commission on
                  Elections (COMELEC) not later than ninety (90) days before the election
                  stating its desire to participate in the party-list system as a national, regional
                  or sectoral party or organization or a coalition of such parties or organizations,
                  attaching thereto its constitution, by-laws, shall likewise be considered as a
                  candidate.




                                                                                                  3
               c)​ Family members - refer to those who are related to the second civil degree of
                   consanguinity or affinity including common law wife or husband or partner.

               d)​ National elective offices - refer to the offices of the President, Vice-President,
                   Senators, and Members of the House of Representatives.

               e)​ Local elective offices - refer to the offices of provincial, city, municipal, and
                   barangay officials.

               f)​ Spouse - refers to the legal or common-law wife or husband or partner.

       SEC. 4. Prohibition Against Political Dynasties. – The following acts are prohibited:

               a)​ No person related within the second (2nd) degree of consanguinity or affinity
                   to an incumbent national elective official shall be allowed to hold or run,
                   simultaneously or successively, for any national elective office.

               b)​ No person related within the second (2nd) degree of consanguinity or affinity
                   to an incumbent local elective official shall be allowed to hold or run,
                   simultaneously or successively, for any local elective office.

               c)​ The simultaneous running of persons who are related within the second (2nd)
                   degree of consanguinity or affinity for any national elective office, including
                   nominees of a party-list group, is likewise prohibited.

               d)​ The simultaneous running of persons who are related within the second (2nd)
                   degree of consanguinity or affinity for any local elective office is likewise
                   prohibited.

        Succession or appointment to an elective office in any manner provided by law, whether
in a temporary or permanent capacity, shall be deemed as holding an elective office.

         SEC. 5. Declaration of Family Relationship. – A person aspiring for or seeking an
elective public office and nominees of party-list groups shall declare all prohibited family
relationships and attach to his or her sworn certificate of candidacy birth and marriage
certificates, or an affidavit stating the legal name and degree of relationship of each family
member.

        SEC. 6. Summary Administrative and Judicial Remedies. – For the purpose of giving
full effect to Section 4, the prohibition against political dynasties shall be implemented in the
following manner:

               a)​ Summary Administrative Procedure Through Motu Proprio Action of the
                   COMELEC. – The COMELEC shall encode all family relationships, as
                   contained in the birth and marriage certificates, or affidavit, of each candidate

                    and nominee of a party-list group in a database or databases after each day of
                    filing of certificate of candidacy.
                             At the end of and within ten days after the period for the certificate of
                    candidacy, the COMELEC shall issue notices to candidates covered by the
                    prohibited family relationships and motu proprio resolve the issue of which
                    family member will be prohibited and allowed to run for public office. The
                    resolution of the COMELEC shall be limited to determining and stating: i) at
                    the first level, the voluntary agreement among the family members on who
                    will run for public office, and ii) at the second level, in the event of an
                    absence of voluntary agreement among the family members, the candidate
                    who will run for public office through the drawing of lots.

                b)​ Summary Administrative Procedure Through Petition to Implement the
                    Prohibition by any Registered Voter. – At any day not later than twenty days
                    before the start of printing of the official ballots, any registered voter may file
                    a petition to implement the prohibition. The registered voter shall state in a
                    form, which shall be designed by the COMELEC: i) the legal names of the
                    candidates, ii) the prohibited degree of relationship, iii) request for
                    verification of prohibited degree of relationship based on the information
                    submitted by the candidate during the filing of Certificate of Candidacy or
                    any other information as may be submitted by the registered voter with the
                    form, and iv) request to resolve the issue of which family member will be
                    prohibited and allowed to run.

                             Within ten days from receipt of the request, the COMELEC shall issue
                    notices to candidates and resolve the issue of which family member will be
                    prohibited and allowed to run for public office. The resolution of the
                    COMELEC shall be limited to determining and stating: i) at the first level, the
                    voluntary agreement among the family members on who will run for public
                    office, and ii) at the second level, in the event of an absence of voluntary
                    agreement among the family members, the candidate who will run for public
                    office through the drawing of lots.

                c)​ Quo warranto proceedings. – After the proclamation of the results of election,
                    any registered voter may file a sworn petition for quo warranto on the ground
                    of ineligibility to hold public office due to the prohibition before the:

                        i. Presidential Electoral Tribunal for President and Vice-president,
                        ii. Senate Electoral Tribunal for Senators,
                        iii. House of Representatives Electoral Tribunal for members of the House
                        of Representatives, and
                        iv. The COMELEC for regional, provincial, city, municipal, and barangay
                        officials.

       SEC. 7. Election Offense and Penalties. – The failure to declare any pertinent
information or the declaration of untruthful information as to family relationship in the certificate
                                                                                                     5
of candidacy shall be considered as an election offense. The offense shall be punished with
imprisonment of not less than one year but not more than six years and shall not be subject to
probation. In addition, the guilty party shall be sentenced to suffer perpetual disqualification from
holding public office.

        SEC. 8. Separability Clause. – If any provision of this Act is declared unconstitutional or
invalid, the other provisions not affected thereby shall remain in full force and effect.

        SEC. 9. Repealing Clause. – All laws, decrees, executive orders, rules and regulations, or
parts thereof inconsistent with this Act are hereby repealed or modified accordingly.

        SEC. 10. Effectivity. - This Act shall take effect fifteen (15) days after its complete
publication in the Official Gazette or in a newspaper of general circulation.
