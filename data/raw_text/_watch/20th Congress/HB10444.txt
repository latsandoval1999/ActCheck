                                      EXPLANATORY NOTE

         In the Philippines, persons with disabilities continue to face significant barriers
to full participation in an increasingly digital society. According to the National Council
for Disability Affairs (NCDA), as of July 2026, about three (3) million Filipinos are
registered as persons with disabilities — 1,072,692 with physical disability, 459,759
with speech impairment, 415,547 with psychosocial disability, and 315,642 with visual
disability.1

       The consequences of this gap are measurable. In 2020, the Digital Accessibility
Rights Evaluation (DARE) Index, compiled by the Global Initiative for Inclusive ICTs
(G3ict), scored the Philippines 53 out of 100, reflecting comparatively strong legal
commitments but weak institutional capacity and outcomes. The country registered
only 18 of a possible 50 points across ten (10) measured areas of ICT accessibility,
and to date, no agency holds an exclusive mandate over accessible ICTs2.

        This exclusion runs against the highest law of the land. Article XIII, Section 1 of
the 1987 Constitution provides that "[t]he Congress shall give highest priority to the
enactment of measures that protect and enhance the right of all the people to human
dignity, reduce social, economic, and political inequalities, and remove cultural
inequities by equitably diffusing wealth and political power for the common good."
Section 13 of the same Article goes further, mandating that the State establish a
special agency for persons with disabilities to support their rehabilitation, self-
development, self-reliance, and integration into the mainstream of society.

        The Philippines has also undertaken this commitment internationally. It ratified
the United Nations Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities (UNCRPD)
on April 15, 2008, and Article 9 thereof obligates the State to ensure that persons with
disabilities have access, on an equal basis with others, to information and
communications technologies (ICT) and other services open to the public.

       Domestically, this constitutional and treaty-level commitment is carried through
Republic Act No. 7277, the Magna Carta for Persons with Disabilities, as amended,
which mandates that persons with disabilities be afforded equal opportunity in
education, employment, and access to public facilities and services 3. It is further
operationalized through a series of administrative issuances, chief among them are
the 2010 ICTO-NCDA Joint Memorandum Circular on web accessibility4 and DICT
Memorandum Circular No. 004, Series of 20175, which adopted the Web Content
Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG) 2.0 as the technical standard for government
websites.

       DICT M.C No. 004, in particular, requires the use of alternative text for non-text
content, captions and transcripts for multimedia, and descriptive links and headings;
mandates regular accessibility audits against WCAG 2.0; and requires each
government agency to stand up its own web accessibility compliance team. These
issuances have laid meaningful technical groundwork, but they remain administrative
circulars rather than statute.

      Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG) 2.0 was developed through the
World Wide Web Consortium (W3C) process, in cooperation with individuals,
organizations, and governments worldwide, to provide a single, shared standard for
web content accessibility6. WCAG explains how to make web "content", natural
information such as text, images, and sound; the code or markup defining structure
and presentation; and dynamic content, multimedia, mobile applications, and AI-driven
web interfaces, accessible to persons with disabilities7.

       WCAG rests on four principles: content must be (1) perceivable, presentable to
users through means they can sense; (2) operable, not requiring interaction a user
cannot perform; (3) understandable, with information and interface operation that
users can comprehend; and (4) robust, interpretable reliably across a wide range of
user agents, including assistive technologies, even as those technologies evolve8.

         This proposed Digital Accessibility Act intends to close the gap between the
international technical standard and the country's legal framework by enacting, for the
first time, a stand-alone statute that squarely covers digital accessibility. It adopts
WCAG 2.2 Level AA as the minimum enforceable standard for all covered entities—
national and local government agencies, government-owned or -controlled
corporations (GOCCs), and private establishments offering goods and services to the
public through digital means, including banks, e-commerce platforms, educational
institutions, healthcare providers, and telecommunications and broadcast companies.
It requires covered entities to integrate accessibility into the full lifecycle of digital
products and services, from procurement through maintenance; establishes
graduated compliance periods calibrated to entity size; and creates a coordinated
enforcement framework led by the DICT, in partnership with the NCDA, combining
administrative penalties and criminal liability for willful non-compliance.

       By anchoring digital accessibility obligations in statute rather than
administrative circular, and by establishing a lead agency with clear audit and sanction
authority, this bill directly responds to the monitoring and enforcement gaps identified
by the UN CRPD Committee and international benchmarking bodies alike. It builds on,
rather than displaces, existing measures such as the Filipino Sign Language Act9, the
closed-captioning law10, and the Accessibility Law11, and ensures that as the
Philippines' digital infrastructure continues to expand under recent measures such as
the E-Governance Act12 and the Konektadong Pinoy Act13, no Filipino is left behind on
account of disability.

      This bill was crafted after consultations with multiple stakeholders in various
groups of persons with disabilities and advocacy networks.

        Early approval of this measure is earnestly sought.

 1          SEC. 17. Complaint, Investigation, and Administrative Enforcement. – Any
 2   person who is denied the rights guaranteed under this Act or encounters a digital
 3   accessibility barrier may file a complaint with the DICT, the NCDA, or the appropriate
 4   regulatory agency having jurisdiction over the covered entity.
 5
 6          The DICT, in coordination with the NCDA and appropriate regulatory agencies,
 7   shall establish accessible and effective mechanisms for the receipt, investigation,
 8   mediation, and resolution of complaints and may conduct accessibility audits,
 9   inspections, or investigations to determine compliance with this Act.
10
11           Where a violation is found, the implementing agency shall issue a Notice of
12   Non-Compliance identifying the accessibility deficiencies and directing the covered
13   entity to undertake corrective measures within a reasonable period. Failure to comply
14   without justifiable cause shall constitute grounds for the imposition of administrative
15   penalties under this Act.
16
17          SEC. 18. Civil Remedies and Private Right of Action. – Nothing in this Act shall
18   prevent an aggrieved person from instituting the appropriate civil or administrative
19   action before the proper court, tribunal, or government agency to enforce the rights
20   guaranteed under this Act.
21
22         A person who is denied equal access to a digital product or service, excluded
23   from participation, or otherwise subjected to digital discrimination in violation of this
24   Act may seek appropriate relief, including:
25
26                 (a) An order requiring the removal of accessibility barriers or the
27           provision of accessible alternatives;
28                 (b) Injunctive relief to prevent or remedy continuing violations;
29                 (c) Recovery of actual, moral, nominal, or exemplary damages, where
30           warranted under existing laws, and reimbursement of reasonable litigation
31           expenses, including attorney's fees and costs of suit, where authorized by
32           existing laws or ordered by the court;
33                 (d) Such other relief as may be just and equitable under the
34           circumstances.
35
36           SEC. 19. Administrative Penalties. – After due notice and hearing, a covered
37   entity that fails to comply with this Act, its implementing rules and regulations, or a final
38   compliance order shall be subject to the following administrative penalties:
39
40                (a) First violation – A written warning and an order directing full
41          compliance within a specified period, which shall not exceed six (6) months from
42          the date of issuance, subject to the technical complexity, operational impact,
43          and public significance of the non-compliant digital asset as determined by the
44          implementing agency;

                                                  13
 1
 2                (b) Second violation – An administrative fine of not less than Fifty
 3          Thousand Pesos (₱50,000.00) but not more than five hundred Thousand Pesos
 4          (₱500,000.00);
 5
 6                  (c) Third and subsequent violations – An administrative fine of not less
 7          than five hundred Thousand Pesos (₱500,000.00) but not more than One
 8          Million Pesos (₱1,000,000.00), without prejudice to the suspension, revocation,
 9          or cancellation of any government-issued license, permit, accreditation,
10          registration, franchise, or authority to operate, where authorized under
11          applicable laws.
12
13           Where the violation is committed by a juridical entity, the appropriate regulatory
14   agency may likewise recommend or impose the temporary suspension of its digital
15   operations, the blocking or deactivation of its websites or electronic platforms within
16   the Philippine jurisdiction, or the removal of its mobile applications from accessible
17   digital marketplaces, in accordance with applicable laws, where continued operation
18   would result in repeated or willful violations of this Act; Provided, That physical closure
19   or suspension of the establishment may be enforced as a fallback measure for
20   domestic entities.
21
22         The DICT, in coordination with the NCDA, the DTI, the Securities and Exchange
23   Commission (SEC), the Department of the Interior and Local Government (DILG), and
24   other relevant regulatory agencies, shall develop a uniform penalty assessment
25   framework and matrix for determining the appropriate administrative penalty. Such
26   framework shall consider, among others:
27
28             (a) The nature and gravity of the violation;
29             (b) The number of persons affected;
30             (c) The duration and frequency of the violation;
31             (d) The degree of accessibility denied;
32             (e) The size, annual gross revenue, or financial capacity of the covered
33                 entity;
34             (f) Whether the violation involves an essential public service;
35             (g) Previous violations and history of compliance; and
36             (h) The good faith efforts undertaken by the covered entity to prevent or
37                 remedy the violation.
38
