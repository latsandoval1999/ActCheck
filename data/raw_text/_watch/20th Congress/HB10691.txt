                               EXPLANATORY NOTE

       Article XIV, Section 1 of the 1987 Constitution is explicit that the State
must protect and promote the right of all citizens to quality education at all
levels and take appropriate steps to make such education accessible to all.

      The extraordinary disruption of the COVID-19 pandemic in the early
2020s first forced the country to reckon with the fragility of its traditional,
classroom-bound model of instruction. Nearly six years on, however, the more
pressing threat to continuous learning is no longer a single pandemic, but a
recurring pattern of climate-driven disruption that has become a permanent
feature of the school calendar.

       According to the Second Congressional Commission on Education
(EDCOM II), disasters forces frequent face-to-face class suspensions
nationwide between, disrupting school days leading to substantial loss of
schooling time to typhoons, flooding, and extreme heat.1 Some localities have
fared far worse. Just this year, the Department of Education has begun
drafting an executive framework for automatic class suspensions once the
heat index reaches 40°C or hazard thresholds for typhoons and poor air
quality are met, an acknowledgment that disruption is no longer the exception
but the expectation.2

Unlike the pandemic, these disruptions are typically localized and short-lived,
but the response to them remains improvised. When classes are suspended,
the fallback is routinely for schools to shift to what DepEd terms an
Alternative Delivery Mode, yet whether this materializes as an online class, a
printed module, or an outright loss of instructional time still depends almost
entirely on whether a school or student has reliable internet access.

That dependency remains the weakest link in the chain. As of 2024, only 48.8
percent of Filipino households had stable broadband access3, meaning
generally available internet for use by all members of the household at any
time, regardless of whether it is actually used, still far behind regional peers
such as Vietnam and Thailand, and internet penetration among the poorest
households remains a fraction of that among the wealthiest.
      While more than 78% of public schools nationwide now have internet
connectivity, this still leaves out 12,000 schools nationwide, mostly in
Geographically Isolated and Disadvantaged Areas (GIDAs), without internet
connection.4 Government efforts to close this gap, including the National Fiber
Backbone and the Department of Education's push to connect all public
schools by the end of 2025, are meaningful but partial, and they leave open
the question of what happens the moment those connections go down,
whether from a typhoon, a fiber cut, or a simple lack of coverage in a remote
barangay.


       Hence the need for this Act. Rather than treating connectivity as a
stopgap measure for a single crisis, it seeks to institutionalize a standing,
multimodal learning framework that allows instruction to continue
seamlessly across online, offline, and blended formats whenever physical
schooling becomes unsafe or impossible, whether due to a public health
emergency, a typhoon, extreme heat, or any other climate-related hazard. It
shall mobilize the National Telecommunications Commission (NTC) to identify
priority areas for the establishment of reliable cyber infrastructure and to
work with the Department of Education toward the goal of universal,
comprehensive, and reliable internet connectivity for all learners, so that the
continuity of education no longer depends on how quickly the weather clears.

     It is for the foregoing premises that the approval of this Bill is earnestly
sought.

      AN ACT INSTITUTIONALIZING A MULTIMODAL LEARNING
 FRAMEWORK THROUGH THE PROVISION OF UNIVERSAL INTERNET
   ACCESS TO ENSURE THE CONTINUITY OF EDUCATION DURING
 PUBLIC HEALTH EMERGENCIES, NATURAL DISASTERS, AND OTHER
   CLIMATE-RELATED DISRUPTIONS, MOBILIZING THE NATIONAL
   TELECOMMUNICATIONS COMMISSION FOR THE PURPOSE, AND
               APPROPRIATING FUNDS THEREFOR

       Be it enacted by the Senate and the House of Representatives of the
Philippines in Congress assembled:

      SECTION 1. Short Title. – This Act shall be known as the “Universal
Wireless Internet Connection Act of 2026.”

        SECTION 2. Declaration of Policy. – In recognition of the vital role of
information and communications technology in nation-building, the State
declares its policy to promote an environment for the development of
infrastructure that would ensure the availability and accessibility to reliable
and secure internet access suitable to the needs and aspirations of the nation.
The State shall therefore institutionalize the delivery of education digitally by
providing universal, comprehensive, reliable, and wireless internet connection
for all.

      SECTION 3. Minimum Internet Speed Requirement. – The National
Telecommunications Commission (NTC) shall set the minimum standards of
internet speed for compliance of all internet service providers (ISPs) in the
country.

      SECTION 4. Universal Wireless Internet Connection Access. – The NTC
shall identify key areas where reliable cyber infrastructure shall be
established. Such key areas shall be strategic locations which must be able
to maximize internet connectivity reach throughout the country, especially in
the rural areas where there is little to no cyber infrastructure available and
accessible, so as to cater to the demands of the digital age.
      SECTION 5. Mechanisms for Free Student Internet Access. – The NTC,
in consultation with DepEd, sectoral organizations, non-government
organizations, research institutions, people’s organizations, and the private
sector, shall come up with a mechanism that will provide students free, fast,
and reliable internet access.

      SECTION 6. Appropriations. – The funds necessary for the initial
implementation of this Act shall be charged against the appropriations of the
Department of Department of Information and Communications Technology
(DICT), Department of Education (DepEd), and the Commission on Higher
Education (CHED). Thereafter, funding shall be included in the budgets of the
DICT, DepEd, and CHED under the annual General Appropriations Act.

       SECTION 7. Implementing Rules and Regulations. – Within thirty (30)
days after this Act takes effect, the DICT, through the NTC, DepEd, and
CHED, in consultation and coordination with appropriate government
agencies, representatives from the private sector, and other stakeholders,
shall promulgate the necessary implementing rules and regulations for the
effective implementation of this Act.

       SECTION 8. Separability Clause. – If any provision or part of this Act is
held invalid or unconstitutional, the remaining provisions or parts unaffected
shall remain in full force and effect.

      SECTION 9. Repealing Clause. – All laws, executive orders, presidential
decrees or issuances, letters of instruction, administrative orders, rules, and
regulations contrary to or inconsistent with the provisions of this Act are
hereby repealed, amended, or modified accordingly.

      SECTION 10. Effectivity Clause. – This Act shall take effect fifteen (15)
days after its publication in the Official Gazette or in a newspaper of general
circulation.
