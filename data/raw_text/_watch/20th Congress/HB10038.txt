                         EXPLANATORY NOTE


      Article XV, Section 3(2) of the 1987 Constitution requires the State to
protect the right of children to assistance and special protection from all
forms of neglect, abuse, cruelty, exploitation, and other conditions
prejudicial to their development. Furthermore, the Constitution recognizes
the vital role of the youth in nation-building and mandates the State to
promote and protect their physical, moral, spiritual, intellectual, and social
well-being. Schools must therefore remain safe, inclusive, and supportive
spaces where learners can develop free from fear, violence, discrimination,
and abuse.
       More than a decade after the enactment of Republic Act No. 10627,
otherwise known as the “Anti-Bullying Act of 2013,” bullying remains a
pervasive and alarming problem in Philippine educational institutions.
Findings cited by the Second Congressional Commission on Education
(EDCOM 2) reveal that 65% of Filipino students reported experiencing
bullying at least a few times a month, among the highest rates recorded
internationally.1 More recent data show that 43% of girls and 53% of boys
in the Philippines experience bullying at least a few times a month—more
than double the average among member countries of the Organisation for
Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD).2


       Bullying is not merely a disciplinary issue. It has profound
consequences on the mental health, safety, and academic performance of
learners. Studies also show that frequent exposure to bullying is associated
with significantly lower learning outcomes, with students who are bullied
weekly scoring substantially lower in Mathematics and Science than their
peers.3 Victims of bullying are likewise more vulnerable to anxiety,
depression, social isolation, self-harm, and other adverse outcomes that
undermine their ability to learn and thrive.4 Beyond its effects on individual
learners, bullying contributes to unsafe school environments and
undermines the ability of educational institutions to fulfill their mission of
fostering learning, growth, and development.

       Despite the existence of legal protections, many schools continue to
face challenges in preventing, identifying, and responding to bullying.
EDCOM 2 also reported that around 10,018 public schools have yet to fully
comply with anti-bullying policy requirements, while researchers have
emphasized the need for school-wide approaches that address not only
incidents of bullying but also the broader school environment in which such
behavior occurs.5


       Since the enactment of Republic Act No. 10627, the Department of
Education      has    undertaken        significant     reforms      to   strengthen       the
implementation of anti-bullying policies through the Revised Implementing
Rules and Regulations of the Anti-Bullying Act of 20136 and Department
Order No. 6, s. 2026, otherwise known as the Guidelines on Ensuring a Safe
and Motivating Learning Environment. These issuances recognize that
preventing bullying requires more than disciplinary sanctions. They adopt
a whole-of-school approach that emphasizes learners’ rights, prevention,
early identification, timely intervention, psychosocial support, restorative
practices, and shared accountability among schools, families, learners, and
communities. There is an opportunity to build upon these reforms by
institutionalizing       their    fundamental         principles     in    statute,     while
strengthening accountability mechanisms, expanding coverage to higher
education and technical-vocational institutions, and introducing additional
safeguards to ensure their sustained implementation.

in educational institutions by: (1) expanding the coverage of the Anti-

identification and referral mechanisms for students who may be
experiencing or exhibiting signs of bullying, social isolation, emotional

appropriate personnel; (7) strengthening rehabilitation and intervention
programs; and (8) institutionalizing a whole-of-school approach to bullying
prevention and student well-being.
