EXPLANATORY NOTE

Technology has changed the way Filipinos work. From offices and factories to businesses
operating under remote and hybrid arrangements, employers increasingly use digital tools to
monitor attendance, measure productivity, and evaluate employee performance. While these
technologies can help businesses operate more efficiently, their growing use must not come at the
expense of workers' rights and job security.

For many employees, workplace monitoring is no longer limited to attendance records or
direct supervision. Some companies use software that tracks computer activity, records keystrokes,
captures screenshots, or generates automated productivity scores. While these systems may serve
legitimate business purposes, they do not always reflect the full extent of an employee's work. The
integration of Automated Workplace Monitoring Systems (AWMS) and algorithmic tools
highlights the critical need to maintain clear professional boundaries, transparency, and personal
privacy in the workplace.

A worker may be marked inactive because of an unstable internet connection, a technical
problem, or time spent performing tasks that monitoring software cannot properly measure. An
automated report may indicate poor performance without considering the actual work completed
or the circumstances surrounding it. When these reports become the basis for disciplinary action,
reduced compensation, or dismissal, workers may find themselves defending their livelihood
against information they cannot fully explain or question.

No Filipino worker should risk losing a job simply because a computer system produces
an unfavorable score. Technology may help employers assess performance, but decisions affecting
a person's employment must still involve fairness, accountability, and human judgment.

Existing laws, particularly Republic Act No. 10173, or the Data Privacy Act of 2012,
already protect employees against improper collection and processing of personal information.
Labor laws likewise provide safeguards against unlawful dismissal and other employment
violations. However, the growing use of automated workplace monitoring raises a specific
concern: how automated reports are verified and used in decisions affecting workers.

This bill seeks to establish practical safeguards when automated monitoring records are
used in employment decisions. It requires employers to inform workers when monitoring results
may affect their employment, ensures that such records undergo meaningful human review, and
gives affected employees a reasonable opportunity to question inaccurate findings. It likewise
protects workers against retaliation for exercising these rights.

The measure does not prohibit employers from using technology or exercising legitimate
management prerogatives. Rather, it seeks to ensure that technological advancement is
accompanied by fairness and accountability in the workplace.

‘House Bill No. 11797

Introduced by Representative KARL FERNANDEZ LEGAZPI

AN ACT
PROTECTING EMPLOYEES AGAINST UNFAIR EMPLOYMENT DECISIONS
ARISING FROM AUTOMATED WORKPLACE MONITORING SYSTEMS, AND FOR
OTHER PURPOSES

Be it enacted by the Senate and House of Representatives of the Philippines in Congress
assembled:

SECTION 1. Short Title. — This Act shall be known as the "Workplace Monitoring
Transparency and Employee Protection Act."

SECTION 2. Declaration of Policy. —\t is hereby declared the policy of the State to afford
full protection to labor, uphold the dignity and rights of workers, and promote fairness in
employment practices.

The State recognizes the legitimate use of technology by employers to manage work,
improve productivity, and evaluate employee performance. Consistent with existing labor and data
privacy laws, the State shall promote responsible workplace monitoring and protect employees
against unfair employment decisions arising from automated monitoring records, particularly
where such records have not undergone meaningful review or where employees have not been
given a reasonable opportunity to explain or contest the findings.

SECTION 3. Definition of Terms. — For purposes of this Act, the following terms shall
be defined as follows:

(a) “Automated Workplace Monitoring System (AWMS)” refers to any software,
application, electronic device, or automated system used by an employer to collect,
analyze, or evaluate information concerning an employee's work activities or
performance, including systems utilizing artificial intelligence or algorithmic
processing;

(b) “Automated Monitoring Record” refers to any report, score, assessment, or other
information generated or processed by an AWMS concerning an employee's work
activities or performance; and

(c) “Adverse Employment Action” refers to disciplinary action, demotion, suspension,
dismissal, reduction of compensation, or other employment action materially
prejudicial to an employee.

SECTION 4. Scope and Coverage. — This Act shall apply to private sector employers and
employees in the Philippines where automated workplace monitoring systems are used to assess
employee work activities or performance, whether work is performed on-site, remotely, or under
hybrid arrangements.

Nothing in this Act shall diminish the rights and protections available to employees under
existing labor and data privacy laws.

SECTION 5. Disclosure of Workplace Monitoring. — Employers using automated
workplace monitoring systems to assess employee work activities or performance shall inform
affected employees of the monitoring system, the work activities or performance indicators being
assessed, and the manner in which monitoring results may affect their employment.

Such information shall be communicated in writing or through accessible electronic means
before the monitoring system is used for such purposes, without prejudice to the requirements of
Republic Act No. 10173, otherwise known as the Data Privacy Act of 2012.

SECTION 6. Safeguards in Automated Employment Decisions. — No employee shall be
subjected to an adverse employment action based solely on an automated monitoring record
without meaningful review by an authorized employer representative.

Where an automated monitoring record is relied upon as a material basis for an adverse
employment action, the employer shall:

(a) Verify the accuracy and relevance of the monitoring record, taking into account reasonably
identifiable technical errors or system limitations;

(b) Provide the employee with sufficient information concerning the relevant findings relied
upon; and

(c) Give the employee a reasonable opportunity to explain or contest such findings before the
adverse employment action is imposed.

Where an employee identifies a possible error or discrepancy in an automated monitoring
record materially affecting an employment decision, the employer shall provide a reasonable
opportunity for verification and correction. Any verified error shall be taken into account in the
employment decision concerned.

These safeguards shall be implemented without prejudice to existing requirements
governing disciplinary action and termination of employment under applicable labor laws.

SECTION 7. Protection Against Retaliation. - No employer shall dismiss, demote,
suspend, discriminate against, or otherwise subject an employee to adverse treatment for raising a
good-faith concern, requesting verification or correction of an automated monitoring record, or
filing a complaint concerning an alleged violation of this Act.

Nothing in this section shall prevent an employer from enforcing lawful and reasonable
workplace policies or imposing disciplinary measures for legitimate grounds in accordance with
applicable labor laws.

SECTION 8. Enforcement and Remedies. - The Department of Labor and Employment
(DOLE), through its Regional Offices, shall be responsible for monitoring and enforcing
compliance with this Act.

For this purpose, the Secretary of Labor and Employment or duly authorized
representatives may exercise visitorial and enforcement powers under Article 128 of the Labor
Code, as amended, and, after due process, issue appropriate compliance orders for violations of
this Act, subject to the procedures, limitations, and remedies provided under applicable laws.

Nothing in this Act shall affect the jurisdiction of Labor Arbiters and the National Labor
Relations Commission over matters falling within their jurisdiction under existing labor laws, or
the jurisdiction of the National Privacy Commission under Republic Act No. 10173, otherwise
known as the Data Privacy Act of 2012.

SECTION 9. Implementing Rules and Regulations. — Within ninety (90) days from the
effectivity of this Act, the Department of Labor and Employment (DOLE), in coordination with
the National Privacy Commission (NPC) and in consultation with representatives of employers
and workers, shall promulgate the rules and regulations necessary for its implementation.
