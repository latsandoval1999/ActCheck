                                  EXPLANATORY NOTE

        The Tax Reform for Acceleration and Inclusion (TRAIN) law introduced a
significant change to the Philippine income tax system by exempting individuals
earning up to ₱250,000.00 annually from personal income tax. This reform was
designed to ease the tax burden on low- and lower-middle-income earners, allowing
them to retain a larger portion of their income. However, once annual earnings exceed
this threshold, a progressive tax structure applies, with rates starting at 15 percent and
rising to 35 percent for top earners. As a result, even modest increases in income can
lead to disproportionately higher tax liabilities. For example, a worker earning
₱300,000.00 annually pays around ₱625.00 monthly in income tax, while at
₱360,000.00, this more than doubles to approximately ₱1,375.00.1

      Notwithstanding the exemption threshold, employees continue to face
mandatory contributions to government social protection programs, including 4.5
percent for the Social Security System (SSS), 2.25 percent for PhilHealth, and 1
percent for Pag-IBIG. These contributions, totaling nearly 8 percent of monthly income,
are deducted alongside income taxes, significantly reducing the take-home pay.
Combined, taxes and contributions can amount to ₱2,500.00 to ₱3,700.00 per month,
or ₱30,000.00 to ₱44,000.00 annually, even before individuals allocate income for
essential living expenses such as housing, food, and transportation.2

        Taxes remain the lifeblood of the government, enabling the provision of public
funds for critical public services such as education, healthcare, and public safety.
However, tax policy must be responsive to evolving economic conditions.3 In particular,
inflation plays a crucial role in shaping the real impact of taxation. As prices rise, wages
and nominal incomes tend to increase correspondingly, but without adjustments to tax
brackets, taxpayers may be pushed into higher tax rates despite no real gain in
purchasing power4, a phenomenon commonly known as “bracket creep.”

       The interaction between a progressive tax system and persistent inflation can
lead to increased government revenues while unfortunately shifting the tax burden
across income groups at the same time. Individuals who were previously exempt may
become taxable, and those already paying taxes may face higher effective rates. If left
unaddressed, this dynamic could result in a growing number of taxpayers being
subjected to higher marginal tax rates, potentially affecting income distribution,
economic behavior, and the overall effectiveness of taxation as a fiscal policy tool.5

      Ultimately, while the TRAIN law aimed to increase disposable income for lower
earners and stimulate economic activity through higher consumer spending, there is
an equally important need for periodic review and adjustment of our tax policies.6
Ensuring that tax systems remain equitable, efficient, and responsive to inflation is
essential to balancing revenue generation with the protection of taxpayers’ real income
and purchasing power.

      In the 20th Congress, similar bills were filed by Senator Paolo Benigno “Bam”
Aquino IV, Representatives Tobias M. Tiangco, Ferdinand Martin G. Romualdez,
Yedda Marie K. Romualdez, Andrew Julian K. Romualdez, Jude A. Acidre, and Iris
Marie Demesa Montes. This bill fully adopts Senator Bam Aquino’s SBN 267.
