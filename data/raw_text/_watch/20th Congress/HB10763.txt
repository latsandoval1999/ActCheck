                                                      EXPLANATORY NOTE

       Disasters are unpredictable in timing, but they are hardly unexpected in the
Philippines. We face recurring typhoons, floods, earthquakes, and other hazards that
destroy public assets, disrupt livelihoods, interrupt essential services, and impose
substantial fiscal costs. This recurring exposure makes financial preparedness as
necessary as operational preparedness.

       The World Bank estimates that the country is expected to incur, on average, about
US$3.5 billion in annual asset losses from typhoons and earthquakes alone.1 The
experience of Super Typhoon Yolanda is a stark reminder of the scale involved: damages
and losses were estimated at US$12.9 billion, equivalent to about 4.7% of the country’s
2013 gross domestic product.2

       The country already employs contingency funds, contingent credit, sovereign
insurance, and other disaster-financing instruments. In 2019, for example, the World Bank
issued catastrophe bonds providing the Philippines up to US$225 million in protection
against earthquake and tropical cyclone risks, with payouts tied to predefined disaster
parameters.3 These instruments demonstrate that disaster risk can be planned for, priced,
pooled, and transferred before a calamity occurs.

       Gaps, however, remain. In 2025, the Department of Finance, while updating the
national DRFI Strategy, acknowledged the continuing need for cost-effective and
accessible financial protection for vulnerable people and communities and identified
greater use of prearranged finance, parametric insurance, microinsurance, and
anticipatory financing as areas for further development.4

       This bill institutionalizes a Philippine Disaster Risk Financing and Insurance
Framework anchored on risk reduction, risk layering, prearranged and anticipatory
financing, fiscal sustainability, and transparency. Instead of relying on a single source of
financing for every disaster, the measure matches appropriate financing instruments to
different levels of risk, from regular appropriations and contingency resources, to
contingent financing and insurance, to parametric and catastrophe-linked arrangements
for more severe risks.

       The measure also strengthens the National Asset Registry System, protects critical
public assets, supports highly exposed and financially constrained local governments,
and promotes financial protection for vulnerable and productive sectors. It does so
principally through existing institutions, while requiring sound fiscal management,
timely claims processing, accountability, and proper use of disaster-financing proceeds.

        The proposed Disaster Risk Financing Insurance Act forms part of the Common
Legislative Agenda of the Executive and Legislative branches for the Twentieth Congress,
reflecting a shared policy objective to provide stronger financial safeguards against
calamities. Its passage would help turn disaster financing from an exercise in post-
calamity improvisation into a deliberate system of preparedness, protection, and fiscal
resilience.

        We may not know when the next disaster will strike. But with sound planning, we
will be able to know how to finance the response, protect essential services, and help our
communities recover. Disasters may remain unpredictable, but our financial
preparedness should not.

       In view of the foregoing, the immediate enactment of this bill is earnestly sought.

 1          Section 1. Short Title. – This Act shall be known as the “Philippine Disaster Risk
 2   Financing and Insurance Act.”
 3
 4          Section 2. Declaration of Policy. – It is hereby declared the policy of the State to
 5   strengthen the financial resilience of the Government, local government units,
 6   communities, and productive sectors against disasters and climate-related risks and to
 7   ensure the timely restoration of essential public services and livelihoods.
 8
 9          For this purpose, the State shall establish a comprehensive, transparent, and
10   financially sustainable disaster risk financing and insurance system based on the
11   following principles:
12          (a) Risk reduction first, recognizing that financial protection shall complement, and
13              not substitute for, disaster prevention, mitigation, preparedness, climate
14              adaptation, resilient infrastructure, appropriate land-use planning, and proper
15              asset maintenance;
16          (b) Risk layering, through the use of appropriate financing instruments according
17              to the frequency, severity, and fiscal impact of disaster risks;
18          (c) Prearranged and anticipatory financing, to ensure the timely availability of
19              resources before or immediately after qualifying disaster events;
20          (d) Sound fiscal management, taking into account affordability, fiscal sustainability,
21              contingent liabilities, and value for money;
22          (e) Risk-informed decision-making, based on reliable asset information, disaster and
23              climate science, actuarial analysis, and catastrophe-risk modeling;

 2       public assets, highly exposed and financially constrained LGUs, and sectors
 3       with limited capacity to absorb disaster losses;
 4   (g) Transparency and accountability in the procurement, receipt, release, and use of
 5       disaster-related financing; and
 6   (h) Institutional efficiency, through the use and strengthening of existing
 7       government institutions, personnel, funds, and information systems and the
 8       avoidance of unnecessary duplication.

10   Section 3. Definition of Terms. – As used in this Act:
11
12   (a) Anticipatory financing refers to financing made available before the full impact
13       of a disaster based on forecasts, early-warning information, or predetermined
14       triggers to prevent or reduce expected losses;
15
16   (b) Critical public asset refers to a public asset whose destruction, impairment, or
17       prolonged unavailability would materially affect public safety, economic
18       activity, national security, or the continuous delivery of essential public
19       services;
20
21   (c) Disaster Risk Financing and Insurance (DRFI) refers to policies, financing
22       instruments, institutional arrangements, and related mechanisms for
23       managing the financial consequences of disasters and climate-related risks;
24
25   (d) Financial protection gap refers to the difference between estimated disaster-
26       related financial losses and the financial resources reasonably available or
27       accessible through existing budgets, reserves, insurance, prearranged
28       financing, and other risk-transfer mechanisms;
29
30   (e) Parametric risk-transfer arrangement refers to insurance or another lawful risk-
31       transfer mechanism under which payment is determined by the occurrence or
32       measured intensity of a predefined event, parameter, or index without
33       requiring a determination of the actual loss as the basis for payment.;
34
35   (f) Prearranged financing refers to funds, financing facilities, insurance, or other
36       risk-transfer arrangements established before a disaster and made available
37       upon agreed conditions;
38
39   (g) Risk layering refers to the use of different financing instruments for different
40       levels of disaster risk based on their frequency, severity, and expected financial
41       impact;
42
43   (h) Risk pooling refers to an arrangement through which participating entities
44       combine or share risks to diversify exposure, reduce transaction costs, or

 1             improve financial protection; and

 3         (i) Vulnerable sectors refer to individuals, households, communities, and
 4             productive sectors that face heightened exposure to disaster risk and have
 5             limited financial capacity to anticipate, absorb, or recover from disaster-related
 6             losses, including low-income households, farmers, fisherfolk, micro and small
 7             enterprises, persons with disabilities, older persons, and other vulnerable and
 8             marginalized groups recognized under applicable law.

13           Section 4. Philippine Disaster Risk Financing and Insurance Framework. – The
14   Department of Finance (DOF), through the Bureau of the Treasury (BTr), in coordination
15   with the National Disaster Risk Reduction and Management Council (NDRRMC), shall
16   formulate and coordinate the implementation of a Philippine Disaster Risk Financing and
17   Insurance Framework, hereinafter referred to as the DRFI Framework, without prejudice
18   to the powers and functions of the NDRRMC under Republic Act No. 10121.
19
20         The DRFI Framework shall:
21
22         (a) Identify and periodically assess disaster-related fiscal risks, contingent
23             liabilities, and financial protection gaps;
24         (b) Establish appropriate levels of risk retention and risk transfer;
25         (c) Match appropriate financing instruments to different layers of disaster risk;
26         (d) Provide for adequate financial protection of critical public assets;
27         (e) Support appropriate disaster financial protection for LGUs and vulnerable
28             and productive sectors;
29         (f) Establish standards and guidelines for public-asset valuation, insurance,
30             reinsurance, risk pooling, parametric arrangements, contingent financing,
31             claims administration, and use of proceeds;
32         (g) Facilitate access, when appropriate, to domestic, regional, bilateral,
33             multilateral, private-sector, and international disaster and climate-finance
34             mechanisms; and
35         (h) Establish measurable indicators for financial protection, payout, service
36             restoration, transparency, and accountability.
37
38         The NDRRMC shall ensure the integration of the DRFI Framework into the
39   National Disaster Risk Reduction and Management Framework and Plan.
40
41          Matters involving national fiscal policy, annual and medium-term expenditure
42   requirements, national government-LGU cost sharing, borrowing, and other matters
43   within the statutory mandate of the Development Budget Coordination Committee
44   (DBCC) shall be submitted to the DBCC for appropriate action.
