27                                         EXPLANATORY NOTE
28
29           Wealth distribution in the Philippines is extremely unequal. In 2022, the
30   combined wealth of the top 10% richest Filipinos amounted to ₱40 trillion, which is
31   more than twice as large as the combined wealth of the bottom 90% amounting to ₱15
32   trillion. Moreover, the top 1% richest Filipinos had ₱17.9 trillion in wealth, which is
33   almost three times more than the ₱6 trillion wealth of the poorest 50% combined.
34   Worse, of the approximately ₱8.2 trillion of the country’s wealth cornered by 3,000
35   billionaires, 72% or ₱5.9 trillion was concentrated among only 700 Filipinos.
36
37           In fact, even amidst the pandemic when the Philippine economy shrank, the net
38   worth of the 50 wealthiest Filipinos grew by 30% to ₱4 trillion. By 2021, the 17
39   wealthiest in our country made it to the Annual Forbes List of the world’s richest
40   individuals. In 2024, the collective wealth of the 50 richest Filipinos totaled US$80.8
41   billion, equivalent to approximately ₱4.63 trillion.1
42
43         In 2018, a scandalously minuscule two percent (2%) of Filipino families had a
44   monthly income of ₱500,000 to more than ₱10 million. On the other hand, over 17

 1   million Filipino families, or 66 percent, live on a monthly income of ₱26,000 or less.
 2   The gross income inequality is exemplified by how low the highest minimum wage is
 3   in the Philippines. Minimum wage earners in the National Capital Region receive just
 4   ₱695 daily or around ₱15,290 monthly,2 which is way below the family living wage
 5   amounting to ₱1,220 per day or ₱26,840 per month.
 6
 7           For two decades, the basic salaries and wages of workers nationwide remained
 8   virtually stagnant, increasing only slightly to keep pace with inflation. Yet, the
 9   Philippine government is finding ways and inventing new regressive taxes in order to
10   squeeze out more revenue from the people. Philippine taxation for the longest time has
11   been largely collected from what people pay for, what they consume, or from what they
12   earn, and has never implemented a tax on large fortunes. Hence, this Bill proposes a
13   wealth tax on the billionaires or the super-rich. A tax of 1% on wealth above ₱1 billion,
14   2% on wealth above ₱2 billion, and 3% above ₱3 billion is hereby proposed.
15
16          According to the estimates of IBON Foundation, the total wealth tax that can be
17   generated from the country’s 3,000 billionaires is at least ₱508 billion annually. In fact,
18   even just from the 50 richest Filipinos alone, who by any standard are those who can
19   best afford to pay much higher taxes, the Philippine government can generate ₱289
20   billion revenue annually from this billionaire wealth tax. Even after paying the
21   proposed billionaire wealth tax, the 3,000 billionaires will still have ₱5.9 trillion in
22   wealth, while the 50 richest Filipinos will still have ₱4.6 trillion in wealth.
23
24           Billionaires are just 0.0026% of the population, so 99.974% are unaffected by
25   the billionaire wealth tax, unlike the majority of the current tax measures. The billions
26   in revenue from this tax would aid the government in pursuing its anti-poverty measures
27   and other social programs that would help in closing the widening divide between the
28   rich and the poor.
29
30          This tax would further help shift the burden away from regressive consumption
31   taxes towards the handful of the wealthiest who are capable of contributing more to our
32   public coffers.

29
30   SECTION 1. Short Title. – This Act shall be known as the “Billionaire Wealth Tax
31   Act.”
32
33   SEC. 2. Declaration of Policy. – It shall be the declared policy of the State to correct
34   the regressive structure of the Philippine taxation, dismantle entrenched oligarchic
35   privilege, and mobilize domestic resources for free public health, education, social
36   protection, employment, and housing without adding debt burden to the general public,
37   especially to the poor.
38
39   SEC. 3. The National Internal Revenue Code of 1997 is hereby amended by the

17   SEC. 6. International Tax and Asset Transparency Cooperation. – To implement the
18   provisions of this Act effectively, the Commissioner of Internal Revenue, in
19   coordination with the Department of Finance and relevant agencies, is authorized to:
20
21        (A) Participate in and comply with international tax transparency standards,
22            including but not limited to the OECD Common Reporting Standard (CRS)
23            recommendations, and United Nations protocols on asset recovery and
24            beneficial ownership disclosure;
25
26        (B) Enter into treaties, conventions, memoranda of understanding, and exchange
27            of information agreements with foreign governments and multilateral bodies;
28
29        (C) Establish mechanisms for mutual assistance in tax matters and enforcement
30            of this Act across jurisdictions; and
31
32        (D) Access and utilize foreign-sourced data in accordance with international
33            agreements and domestic data protection laws.
34
35   SEC. 7. Wealth-Tax Unit. – Within 180 days from the effectivity of this Act, the BIR
36   shall create a Wealth-Tax Unit composed of specialized revenue officers and
37   government appraisers that shall review and assess the Self-Assessment Statement of
38   Wealth filed and the wealth tax paid by the billionaires. The Unit shall be authorized to
39   conduct lifestyle audits, asset tracing, and to access domestic and international financial
40   records to verify the accuracy of wealth declarations. The Unit shall investigate and
41   recommend the filing of charges for avoidance, misdeclaration, non-declaration of
42   assets and other offenses, if any.
43

10   SEC. 9. Non-Substitution of Existing Appropriations for Social Services. – The
11   revenues collected under this Act and earmarked for the identified social services shall
12   be used exclusively to supplement existing appropriations and shall not be used as a
13   substitute for any part of the regular annual General Appropriations Act (GAA) funding
14   for these social services.
15
16   In no case shall the release or use of these earmarked funds result in a reduction,
17   reallocation, or realignment of existing budgetary appropriations for the same purpose
18   or program.
19
20   The DBM shall certify that the annual appropriations for social services in the GAA are
21   no less than the previous fiscal year’s allocations, adjusted for inflation and population
22   growth.
23
24   The Commission on Audit (COA) shall conduct an annual audit to ensure compliance
25   with this provision and shall submit a report to Congress.
26
27   SEC. 10. Separability Clause. – If any provisions of this Act is declared invalid or

29   to be in full force and effect.
30

34
