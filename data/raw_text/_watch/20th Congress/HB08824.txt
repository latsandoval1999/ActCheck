                                   EXPLANATORY NOTE

        In the face of extraordinary global oil volatility, the State cannot sit idly by while
the Filipino people bear the full and immediate burden of surging fuel prices.

        Republic Act No. 8479, otherwise known as the Downstream Oil Industry
Deregulation Act of 1998, was enacted to liberalize and deregulate the downstream
oil industry to ensure a truly competitive market under a regime of fair prices and an
adequate and continuous supply of environmentally clean and high-quality petroleum
products1.

      The Philippine experience shows that deregulation has, to a significant extent,
encouraged competition in the downstream oil industry. Market structure and
investment indicators reflect substantial entry and diversification following the
enactment of R.A 8479. From an industry dominated by only three “big players” before
deregulation, the number of participants expanded to around 400 firms as of
December 2021, while the combined market share of the three dominant oil
companies declined from 95.7% in 1998 to 49.4% in 20212. These developments

       More than two decades since its passage, however, material gaps remain in
the law’s regulatory and enforcement framework, particularly in consumer protection,
price transparency, market monitoring, and the State’s capacity to respond to
extraordinary price shocks arising from global market volatility and geopolitical
disruption.

       These gaps are especially consequential for the Philippines, which remains a
highly oil-dependent economy. Domestic petroleum production is negligible, and the
country is heavily reliant on imported crude oil and finished petroleum products. 95%
of the country’s crude oil requirements are sourced from the Persian Gulf countries,
rendering the Philippines particularly vulnerable to disruptions in global oil supply
chains and to sudden increases in international oil prices3.

         Recent geopolitical developments in West Asia (more popularly known as the
Middle East) have underscored the urgency of strengthening the legal framework
governing the downstream oil industry. On March 27, 2026, the Islamic Republic of
Iran officially declared the Strait of Hormuz closed to any vessel not explicitly
authorized by Tehran, effectively blockading a maritime chokepoint through which
approximately 20% of the world’s crude oil and liquefied natural gas (LNG) historically
transits.4 This development highlighted the Philippines’ acute exposure to external
supply shocks as a net oil-importing country that depends heavily on the Persian Gulf
for its crude oil requirements.

        Amid these tensions, the Department of Foreign Affairs (DFA) announced on
April 2, 2024, that it had a “very productive” phone conversation on Maundy Thursday
with Iranian Foreign Minister Seyed Abbas Araghchi. During the call, Araghchi assured
Foreign Affairs Secretary Ma. Theresa Lazaro that Iran would guarantee the safe,
unhindered, and expeditious passage of Philippine-flagged vessels, energy
shipments, and Filipino seafarers through the Strait of Hormuz5.

       This vulnerability is reflected in official energy data. The Department of Energy’s
(DOE) 2024 Key Energy Statistics show that the Philippines imported 45,366 thousand
barrels of crude oil in 2024, of which 43,931 thousand barrels came from the Middle
East. The country likewise imported 54,060 thousand barrels of diesel during the same

        The domestic effects of such external disruptions have been immediate and
severe. Data published by GlobalPetrolPrices, based on DOE’s price data, show that
diesel prices in the Philippines stood at Php 119.20 per liter as of 30 March 2026,
compared with Php 60.79 per liter one month earlier, representing an increase of
96.1%.7 Further reports suggest that another wave of oil price hikes is imminent.
Projections indicate that regular diesel prices could climb to as high as P165 per liter,
with premium diesel surpassing P170 per liter8. Gasoline prices are likewise expected
to rise by P3 to P5 per liter, potentially bringing super premium, premium, and regular
gasoline prices to approximately P120, P117, and P110 per liter, respectively9. These
sharp escalations evidence the extraordinary pass-through of international price
shocks to domestic pump prices during periods of conflict.

       Comparative reporting and market commentary indicate that the Philippines
was among the worst affected by higher oil prices in the region and may have posted
the steepest diesel price increase among its Asia-Pacific Peers10. Unlike neighboring
Southeast Asian countries that employ broad-based fuel subsidies or regulated pricing
mechanisms, the Philippines maintains fully market-linked retail fuel prices. As a result,
domestic pump prices have risen sharply in response to global supply disruptions, with
limited capacity for immediate government intervention11.

       This constraint has been openly acknowledged by the DOE. Secretary Sharon
Garin reported that domestic pump prices were expected to increase by around Php
17.00 to Php 24.00 per liter in a single week, which she described as the “highest
jump,” and further explained that the DOE has no authority to impose a price cap
because the oil industry is deregulated under existing law12.

        Notwithstanding its declared objectives, R.A 8479 has, during periods of
extreme international volatility, limited the national government’s ability to intervene in
retail pricing and has allowed global price shocks to be passed through almost
immediately and in full to domestic consumers.

       This bill seeks to amend R.A 8479 to preserve deregulation as the general rule,
while strengthening the government’s legal and institutional capacity to respond to
actual market abuse and extraordinary petroleum price shocks.

       Among others, the following are the salient features of the bill:

       First, the measure strengthens competition enforcement by mandating the
Philippine Competition Commission (PCC), in coordination with the DOE and the
Department of Trade and Industry (DTI), to ensure strict compliance with Republic Act
No. 10667, otherwise known as the Philippine Competition Act (PCA). Cartel,
monopolistic, and other anticompetitive acts in the downstream oil industry shall now
be addressed under R.A 10667.

       Second, it abolishes the DOE-DOJ Task Force, which was originally mandated
to investigate reports of unreasonable increases in petroleum prices. In its place,
enforcement authority is centralized in the PCC, while the DOE retains monitoring and
operational authority, including the power to temporarily take over or direct industry
operations during a national emergency.

       Third, it rationalizes remedies and enforcement by vesting in the PCC the
authority to conduct fact-finding and preliminary inquiry into violations of Section 11 of
R.A 8479 and, when warranted, to initiate the proper complaints and actions before
the appropriate court.

       Fourth, it authorizes the President, upon recommendation of the Development
Budget Coordination Committee (DBCC) in coordination with the Secretary of Energy,
to suspend or reduce excise taxes and/or value-added tax on fuel through Executive
Order, subject to an oil price trigger, duration limits, automatic reversion, and
congressional reporting, consistent with the provisions of Republic Act No. 1231613.

       Finally, it establishes an Emergency Oil Price Stabilization Fund (EOPSF) as a
limited, time-bound emergency mechanism to address abnormal and excessive pump
price increases caused by severe disruptions in international oil markets, foreign
exchange volatility, war, global supply shortages, natural hazards, and similar events.
Its use is allowed only upon a Presidential determination, on recommendation of the
DBCC in coordination with the Secretary of Energy, that an energy supply or price
emergency exists and immediate intervention is necessary.

       In sum, this measure does not abandon the policy of deregulation; rather, it
corrects its deficiencies by equipping the State with clearer enforcement powers,
stronger competition safeguards, and narrowly tailored emergency tools to protect the
public from extraordinary fuel price shocks.

      At a time when global instability can, in a matter of days, reverberate through
domestic pump prices, transportation fares, food costs, and the overall cost of living,
the government cannot remain a passive observer. The State must be able to act
