                              EXPLANATORY NOTE

       Section 15, Article II of the 1987 Constitution affirms that “the State shall
protect and promote the right to health of the people and instill health consciousness
among them.” Complementing this, Section 11, Article XIII mandates the State to
“adopt an integrated and comprehensive approach to health development” and to
make essential goods, health services, and other social services accessible to all at
affordable cost.

       Taken together, these provisions establish two fundamental principles. First,
health is a human right that the State is duty-bound to respect, protect, and fulfill.
Second, health and well-being are indispensable to national development. A nation’s
health cannot be separated from its economic trajectory. When preventable illness
persists, productivity declines, inequalities widen, and economic opportunities
contract. Conversely, investing in health is not merely a social commitment—it is a
strategic economic imperative that strengthens human capital, drives inclusive growth,
and enables communities to participate fully in the country’s development.

      With significant laws passed in the recent years such as the Cheaper Medicines
Act (Republic Act No. 9502), the Philippine Mental Health Act (Republic Act No.
11036), the Sin Tax Reform Act (Republic Act 10351), the Responsible Parenthood
and Reproductive Health Law (Republic Act No. 10354), the Kalusugan at Nutrisyon
ng Mag-Nanay Act (Republic Act 11148) the Universal Health Care Act (Republic Act
No. 11223), the Philippines still falls behind in terms of achieving a genuine universal
and equitable health care for Filipinos.

         Despite PhilHealth coverage reportedly exceeding 90% of the population,
financial protection remains alarmingly weak. World Bank data indicate that out-of-
pocket health spending in the Philippines stood at 44.36% in 2023, a level starkly
inconsistent with the UHC Law’s promise to substantially reduce the financial burden
on Filipino households.1 This means that nearly half of total health expenditures are
still paid directly by families at the point of care. Major cost drivers for out-of-pocket
expenses in the Philippines include purchase of outpatient drugs, hospitalization and
preventive care2.

       Regionally, the Philippines ranks fourth highest in out-of-pocket spending in
ASEAN. Only Myanmar (71.13%), Cambodia (60.60%), and Lao PDR (44.84%) report
higher shares in 2023. The Philippines (44.36%) fares worse than Viet Nam (39.20%),
Malaysia (37.09%), Indonesia (31.08%), Singapore (25.42%), and dramatically lags
behind Thailand (9.93%), Brunei Darussalam (7.41%), and Timor-Leste (6.99%).

       Beyond persistent weaknesses in financial risk protection, the Philippines
continues to face significant challenges in morbidity and mortality across multiple
disease domains. Non-communicable diseases linked to tobacco, alcohol, and
unhealthy diets already account for a growing share of deaths and healthcare costs in
the Philippines. One study estimated that these risk factors cost our economy an
estimate of at least Php 1.055 trillion annually. 3

        Simultaneously, the country is confronting a rapidly expanding HIV epidemic.
Following a 500 percent increase in incidence among individuals aged 15–25 years,
the Philippines declared a public health emergency for HIV and AIDS. In 2025,
surveillance data indicate an average of 57 newly reported HIV cases per day,
compared to 37 daily cases in the preceding year, suggesting accelerating
transmission dynamics, particularly among younger populations.4 Tuberculosis (TB)
remains a persistent public health concern. The Philippines ranks third globally in total
TB burden, contributing approximately 6.8 percent of global TB cases.5

the National Nutrition Survey found that more than 2 million or one in four children
were stunted, making it one of the highest rates in the country.6

       Maternal and child health outcomes similarly remain off-target. The infant
mortality rate (IMR) was 14.22 per 1,000 live births in 2022, exceeding the target of
12 per 1,000. The maternal mortality ratio (MMR) reached 83.8 per 100,000 live births
in 2023, substantially above the target of 70 per 100,000.7

       In line with this, this bill seeks to create a Congressional Commission on Health
(HEALTHCOM) to conduct a comprehensive assessment and evaluation of the
Philippine health care system. This bill adopts the framework of Republic Act No.
11899 or the Second Congressional Commission on Education (EDCOM II) Act. With
its impactful studies, policy outputs, and inter-agency coordination, EDCOM II has
been able to surface the deep-seated problems in our education system.

       The HEALTHCOM will be engaging different experts, stakeholders, and leaders
in the government, medical institutions, medical and allied health professions,
research institutions, and civil society organizations through consultations and
research in order to propose evidence-based and data-driven programs, policies, and
reforms to address the gaps in the current system.

          In view of the foregoing, the immediate passage of this bill is earnestly sought.

 4         Sec. 2. Declaration of Policy. – It is the declared policy of the State to
 5   protect and promote the right of all citizens to quality, accessible, affordable, and
 6   equitable health care. Health is essential to human development, economic
 7   productivity, disaster resilience, and national progress.
