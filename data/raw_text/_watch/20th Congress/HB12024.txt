EXPLANATORY NOTE

Sa isang screen na kasingliit ng palad, ang mga salitang “scam” at “mastermind” ay maaaring
lumitaw sa tabi ng mukha ng isang tao, mabilis mabasa, mabilis maipasa, at mahirap nang bawiin.
Ang payak na pangyayaring ito ay nagpapakita ng mas malawak na hamon ng ating panahon: bago pa
man makapagtanong ang isang Pilipino kung saan nagmula ang isang video, larawan, o boses, maaari
na niya itong paniwalaan, ipasa, pagbasehan sa pagbabayad, o gamitin sa paghusga sa kapwa. Kapag
ang nakikita at naririnig ay kayang likhain o baguhin nang halos hindi matukoy, ang unang nawawala
ay hindi lamang impormasyon. Ang unang nawawala ay ang tiwala na ang mata at tainga ay may

matibay na masasandalan.

The central problem is not that every synthetic work is harmful, nor that every alteration is
deceptive. Artificial intelligence can support education, accessibility, artistic expression, public
communication, and innovation. The danger arises when a realistic but undisclosed synthetic or
materially altered recording is presented as authentic in circumstances where people are likely to rely
upon it, particularly when it can induce payment, impersonate a person, damage a reputation, harass
a Victim, or create public disorder. What many Filipinos may not yet appreciate is that a convincing
recording no longer proves, by appearance alone, that the event it depicts occurred or that the speaker
uttered the words attributed to them. In this environment, provenance, meaning reliable information
about where content came from and how it was created or changed, becomes a practical public

safeguard rather than a technical luxury.

Available research underscores both the urgency and the limits of a responsible legislative
response. A regional policy overview on AI Generated Content Labelling in the Indo-Pacific reports

that the Philippines currently has no comprehensive binding law specifically requiring the labeling or

This bill recognizes and completes work already underway. It builds upon DICT’s reported

of the reported election-specific synthetic-media rules under COMELEC Resolution No. 11064,

during the 2025 elections.’ The gap this measure fills is outside any single campaign period or single
platform: a durable, risk-based framework for high-risk deceptive synthetic content in public
information, commercial dealings, and other settings in which a Filipino may reasonably act upon a

recording as authentic.

The antagonist is not the artist who uses new tools, the small entrepreneur who cannot hire a
compliance department, the platform moderator facing an impossible volume of uploads, or the citizen
who shares a convincing clip in good faith. The antagonist is the missing system between creation and
reliance: a system that allows a provenance claim to be disclosed clearly, checked through
interoperable methods, and, where a participant chooses, supported by an auditable trusted-content
registry. This measure therefore does not establish a general license for the State or private platforms
to police opinion. It does not treat anonymous expression as suspicious. It distinguishes between

lawful creativity and deceptive presentation in high-risk contexts; it provides proportionate

May mabigat na tanong dito, at nararapat lamang na harapin ito nang tapat. Ang sapilitang
pagsisiwalat ng paggamit ng Al ay maaaring magbunga ng pag-aalala sa malayang pananalita at sa
pribadong pagkakakilanlan; ang maliliit na creator at serbisyo ay maaaring mahirapang sumunod sa
magastos o komplikadong pamantayan; at ang watermark ay maaaring tangkaing burahin, baguhin, o
iwasan gamit ang open-source na kagamitan. An article published by the International
Telecommunication Union, or ITU, identifies trusted custody of private information and the possible
circumvention of open-source watermarking schemes as genuine challenges. It further states that a
watermark should be integral to a model and not modifiable or removable.* Those objections are not
reasons to abandon the task. They are reasons to enact it carefully: with risk-based duties rather than
indiscriminate mandates, interoperable and resilient standards rather than one proprietary solution,
voluntary participation in the trusted registry, due regard for lawful anonymity, and regular technical

review as methods of evasion evolve.

The principle of this bill is straightforward: when synthetic or materially altered content can
cause real-world reliance and harm, Filipinos must be able to see a meaningful disclosure and verify
a credible provenance claim without surrendering privacy, speech, or equal access to the digital
economy. This approach is consistent with the direction identified in international and regional
governance guidance. The same article states that the proliferation of generative Al necessitates a
public registry of watermarked models and universal detection tools. ISO/IEC 42001:2023, the NIST
AI Risk Management Framework, and the ASEAN Guide on Al Governance and Ethics have been
identified as standards or guidance emphasizing human-centricity, transparency, and accountability.
The proposed registry is consequently voluntary and auditable, designed as a trust signal rather than
as a compulsory catalogue of all online speech. It complements, rather than duplicates, responsible
platform practices and lawful remedies that may be available after fraud, impersonation, or identity

abuse has occurred.

The cost of leaving this gap open is measured by the clock. Every month that provenance
remains opaque is another month in which a family may hesitate before believing a relative’s voice
asking for help, a small business may act on a fabricated testimonial, a worker may fear a manipulated

recording, and a community may be pushed into panic by a clip whose origin cannot be tested. Delay

AN ACT ESTABLISHING A RISK-BASED FRAMEWORK FOR THE DISCLOSURE,
PROVENANCE, AND VERIFICATION OF SYNTHETIC AND MATERIALLY ALTERED
DIGITAL CONTENT, CREATING A VOLUNTARY TRUSTED CONTENT
PROVENANCE REGISTRY, PROVIDING SAFEGUARDS FOR PRIVACY, ANONYMOUS
EXPRESSION, AND SMALL DIGITAL SERVICE PROVIDERS, AND FOR OTHER
PURPOSES

Be it enacted by the Senate and House of Representatives of the Philippines in Congress assembled:

SECTION 1. Short Title. —- This Act shall be known as the "Tiwala-Tingin Act".

SECTION 2. Declaration of Policy. — Pursuant to Article Il, Section 24 and Article III,
Section 4 of the Constitution, it is the policy of the State to enable every person to make informed
decisions before trusting, sharing, paying, or acting upon digital content that purports to depict a real
person, event, statement, or occurrence. The State shall promote transparent, interoperable, privacy-
protective, and technically resilient means of communicating the provenance of synthetic and

materially altered digital content.

The State recognizes that synthetic-content tools may serve legitimate purposes in education,
art, accessibility, research, entertainment, and commerce. Regulation under this Act shall therefore be
content-neutral, proportionate to the reasonably foreseeable risk of material deception and harm,
protective of lawful anonymous expression, and calibrated to the technical capacity and resources of

the person or digital service provider concerned.

Priority in publicly supported implementation, technical assistance, and capacity-building

shall be given to micro, smail, and medium enterprises, community-based creators, and digital service

SECTION 3. Definition of Terms. — As used in this Act:

(a) "Authenticity claim" refers to a representation that digital content is an unaltered recording
of an actual person, event, statement, or occurrence, or a representation concerning the known

origin, chain of custody, or material editing history of such content;

(b) "Covered distributor" refers to a person who, in the course of business or organized
activity, publicly distributes, promotes, monetizes, or causes the public distribution of covered
high-risk content, and who has actual knowledge or reasonable control over the content at the
time of distribution. It does not include a person whose activity is limited to providing passive
connectivity, storage, or transmission without selection or material modification of the

content;

(c) "Covered high-risk content" refers to synthetic content or materially altered content that a
reasonable person would understand as an authentic depiction or recording and that is
reasonably likely to cause a person to part with money or property, disclose sensitive
information, take or refrain from taking a material action affecting health or safety, or believe
that a real person made a statement or committed an act that the person did not make or

commit;

(d) "Digital service provider" refers to a person or entity that provides an online service
through which users may create, host, transmit, discover, share, promote, or access digital

content;

(e) "Materially altered content" refers to digital content altered in a manner that changes its
apparent meaning, context, speaker, subject, event, or evidentiary appearance in a way that is
material to an authenticity claim. It does not include ordinary cropping, color correction,
compression, captioning, translation, or accessibility adaptation that does not create a

materially false appearance;

(f) "Provenance record" refers to a machine-readable or human-readable record associated

with digital content that identifies, to the extent known and voluntarily disclosed, its origin,

SECTION 4, Required Disclosure for Covered High-Risk Content. — A covered distributor
that knows, or has reason to know from the content's provenance record or from circumstances within
its reasonable control, that content is covered high-risk content shall provide a clear and reasonably

conspicuous disclosure that the content is synthetic or materially altered.

The disclosure shall be presented in a form reasonably appropriate to the medium, including
an on-screen notice for video or image content, an audible notice and accompanying text for audio
content, or a persistent text notice adjacent to other digital content. The disclosure shall identify the
content as synthetic or materially altered without characterizing the underlying viewpoint, message,

or lawful expression.

A disclosure under this section shall not be required where the context would make the
synthetic or altered nature of the content apparent to a reasonable person, including clearly fictional,
satirical, parodic, artistic, or fantastical content, unless the covered distributor uses the content in a

manner that creates a reasonably foreseeable risk described in paragraph (c) of Section 3.

No person shall be held liable under this section for the removal, corruption, or non-display
of a disclosure caused by a third party or by a technical process beyond that person's reasonable
control, if the person used reasonably practicable measures to provide the disclosure at the time of

distribution.

Such standards shall:

creator unless adequate technical support or funding has been made available, or the duty may be met

SECTION 7. Proportionate Duties of Digital Service Providers. — A digital service provider
that receives a complete and credible notice alleging that publicly available content within its
reasonable control lacks a required disclosure under Section 4 may provide the uploader or responsible
covered distributor a reasonable opportunity to add, correct, or contest the disclosure, except where
delay presents a specific and articulable risk of imminent fraud, physica! harm, or unlawful disclosure

of intimate or sensitive content.

A small digital service provider shall not be required to deploy automated scanning, proactive
monitoring, proprietary detection software, or continuous watermark verification. Its duty under this
Act is limited to reasonably practicable action upon a complete and credible notice, taking into account
its resources, technical capacity, control over the content, and the availability of publicly supported

tools.

In assessing compliance by a digital service provider, the administering agency shall compare
providers with similarly situated providers based on size, function, technical capacity, volume of
covered high-risk content, and access to support. Assessment shall recognize measurable
improvement in response practices, user notice, and use of available standards, in addition to absolute

achievement.

SECTION 8. Complaints, Correction, and Cause Attribution. —- Any person may submit a
complaint concerning an alleged failure to provide a disclosure required under Section 4, a materially
inaccurate registry entry, or an unauthorized public disclosure of protected registry information. The
administering agency shail establish an accessible process for receipt, acknowledgment, review,

correction, and appeal of complaints.

No adverse finding shall be made against a covered distributor or digital service provider
unless the finding identifies the conduct within that person's reasonable control, the applicable duty,
the notice or knowledge establishing responsibility, and the causal connection between that conduct
and the alleged failure. A person shall not be held responsible for a failure caused solely by the
unavailability of a public verification system, an upstream provider's failure to supply provenance
information, a third-party technical alteration, or another circumstance beyond the person's reasonabie

control, unless the person knowingly exploited or materially contributed to that failure.

The administering agency may issue corrective guidance, facilitate voluntary correction, or
refer conduct to the appropriate authority where the facts may indicate a violation of another
applicable law. Nothing in this Act creates a presumption that synthetic or materially altered content

is unlawful.

SECTION 9. Privacy, Anonymous Expression, and Due Process Safeguards. -~
Implementation of this Act shall respect privacy, data minimization, freedom of expression, freedom

of the press, artistic expression, and lawful anonymous or pseudonymous speech.

No registry, verification, or disclosure requirement under this Act shall require the retention
or public disclosure of biometric information, private keys, identity documents, precise location data,
or other information not reasonably necessary to establish or evaluate a provenance claim. A person
whose registry entry is denied, corrected, suspended, or removed shall receive notice of the grounds
and a reasonable opportunity to respond, except where temporary action is necessary to prevent a

specific and articulable risk of imminent harm.

SECTION 12. Appropriations. — The amount necessary for the initial implementation of

Thereafter, such sums as may be necessary for the continued implementation of this Act shall be

included in the annual General Appropriations Act.

No local government unit shall be required to establish a registry, procure technology, conduct
enforcement, or undertake a new activity under this Act without a corresponding appropriation,

technical support, or voluntary agreement.

SECTION 13. Separability Clause. — If any provision of this Act is declared unconstitutional

or invalid, the other provisions not affected thereby shall remain in full force and effect.

SECTION 14. Repealing Clause. — All laws, decrees, executive orders, rules and
regulations, and other issuances or parts thereof inconsistent with this Act are hereby repealed,

amended, or modified accordingly.

SECTION 15. Effectivity. — This Act shall take effect fifteen (15) days after its publication

in the Official Gazette or in a newspaper of general circulation.
