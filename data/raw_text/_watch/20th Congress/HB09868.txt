                        AN ACT
RECLASSIFYING QUALIFIED THEFT OF COCONUTS AND FISH AS
  SIMPLE THEFT UNDER ARTICLE 308, AMENDING FOR THIS
PURPOSE ARTICLE 310 OF ACT NO. 3815, OTHERWISE KNOWN AS
        “THE REVISED PENAL CODE,” AS AMENDED

                           EXPLANATORY NOTE

Criminal laws must be founded on principles of fairness, reasonableness, and
proportionality. Persons who commit similar acts causing similar harm should
generally be subject to similar penalties. Yet, under Article 310 of the Revised
Penal Code, the theft of coconuts from the premises of a plantation and fish
taken from a fishpond or fishery are classified as qualified theft and punished
two degrees higher than ordinary theft, regardless of whether the value of the
coconuts and fish are minimal. These distinctions create an arbitrary disparity
in the treatment of offenders and is difficult to justify under present conditions.
The consequences of this disparity were illustrated in the case of Pedro
Amarille, a farmer from Maribojoc, Bohol. In 2011, he was convicted of
qualified theft and sentenced to six (6) years of imprisonment for taking 200
coconut fruits valued at only P2,000.00. After years of litigation, the Supreme
Court ultimately acquitted him in 2023.1

In his separate concurring opinion on the same case, Supreme Court Associate
Justice Marvic Leonen took the opportunity to manifest that:

       “Singling out theft of coconuts as a qualified theft has become
       anachronistic and amounts to a violation of the equal protection of the
       laws. It is discriminatory not only against other food products, but also
       against the poor.”2

The special treatment accorded to coconut theft has a specific historical
context. In the 1950 case of People vs. Isnain,3 the Supreme Court explained
that the reason for a heavier penalty on the theft of coconuts was “to encourage
and protect the development of the coconut industry as one of the sources of
our national economy. Unlike rice and sugar cane farms where the range of
vision is unobstructed, coconut groves cannot be efficiently watched because of
the nature of the growth of coconut trees; and without a special measure to
protect this kind of property, it will be, as it has been in the past the favorite
resort of thieves.”          While the coconut industry remains an important
contributor to the country's economy, the conditions that justified this
exceptional treatment more than seven decades ago have substantially
changed. Advances in technology, transportation, and farm security now

More fundamentally, there is no substantial distinction that justifies granting
coconuts greater protection than other agricultural products. As Justice
Leonen further explained:

         “[T]here is no substantial distinction as to why the coconut industry is
         given more protection from thieves, while other fruits, cereals, or forest
         or farm products are not. The nature of growth of coconut trees does
         not make for real differences, as some people still resort to stealing
         coconuts, as in Gaviola. Hence, there is no reasonable classification
         between coconuts and other fruits, cereals, or forest or farm products
         so as to justify the imposition of a higher penalty for the theft of
         coconuts compared to other food or farm products.4

The same observation applies with equal force to fish taken from fishponds or
fisheries. Like coconuts, fish receive special treatment under Article 310 solely
because of the nature of the property involved, even though theft of other
agricultural products, produce, or aquaculture resources may cause
comparable economic harm. There is no substantial distinction that warrants
punishing the theft of fish from a fishpond two degrees more severely than the
theft of similarly valued property.

Indeed, modern advances in farm management, aquaculture practices,
surveillance technology, and law enforcement have significantly improved the
ability of owners to protect plantations and fisheries from theft. At the same
time,     the    penalties     for    qualified      theft    have     become       increasingly

disproportionate in cases involving low-value coconuts or fish, producing
outcomes that are difficult to reconcile with the principles of fairness and
proportionality that underpin the criminal justice system.

Moreover, Article 310 already protects particularly blameworthy forms of
theft, such as those committed by domestic servants, with grave abuse of
confidence, or during calamities and civil disturbances. Those qualifying
circumstances relate to the manner in which the crime is committed or the
heightened culpability of the offender. By contrast, singling out coconuts and
fish based solely on the type of property taken creates an outdated
classification that no longer serves a compelling public purpose.

This bill therefore seeks to remove the classification of theft of coconut and fish
as qualified theft and instead punish these acts under the general provisions
on simple theft. Offenders will remain criminally liable, but the applicable
penalties will be determined in accordance with the ordinary rules governing
theft rather than an obsolete statutory exception. By doing so, the measure
promotes a more equitable, rational, and proportionate criminal justice system
while continuing to hold offenders accountable for unlawful conduct.

Finally, the bill removes the reference to large cattle in Article 310. This
amendment is intended to harmonize the Revised Penal Code with existing
law, as theft involving large cattle is already governed by Presidential Decree
No. 533, otherwise known as the Anti-Cattle Rustling Law of 1974, which
superseded the corresponding provision in Article 310.

By eliminating outdated distinctions that no longer serve a compelling public
purpose, this bill advances the constitutional principles of equal protection,
fairness, and proportionality in the administration of criminal laws.
